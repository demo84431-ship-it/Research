# Chapter 35: Research Ethics and Integrity

## Opening

Research ethics and integrity are not optional extras — they are the foundation upon which all credible science is built. Every data point you collect, every analysis you run, and every paper you write must be conducted with honesty, transparency, and respect for the people who make your research possible. Violations of research ethics — from fabrication and falsification to plagiarism and p-hacking — damage not only your career but the entire scientific enterprise. This final chapter brings together the ethical principles, practical guidelines, and integrity practices that every psychology researcher must master.

---

## 35.1 The Pillars of Research Integrity

### 35.1.1 Honesty

Report methods, data, and findings truthfully. Do not fabricate, falsify, or misrepresent.

- Report all results, including non-significant findings
- Do not manipulate images or data to support your hypothesis
- Disclose conflicts of interest

### 35.1.2 Transparency

Make your methods and data available for scrutiny.

- Pre-register hypotheses when possible (e.g., on OSF or AsPredicted)
- Share data and analysis scripts (de-identified)
- Describe methods in sufficient detail for replication

### 35.1.3 Respect

Treat participants, colleagues, and research subjects with dignity.

- Obtain informed consent
- Protect confidentiality
- Acknowledge contributions

### 35.1.4 Responsibility

Take ownership of your work and its consequences.

- Ensure accuracy before publication
- Correct errors promptly
- Report misconduct when you observe it

## 35.2 Research Misconduct

### 35.2.1 Fabrication

**Making up data or results that do not exist.**

> *Example:* A researcher claims to have surveyed 500 participants but actually generated fake responses using a random number generator.

**Consequences:** Retraction of papers, loss of funding, termination of position, permanent damage to reputation.

### 35.2.2 Falsification

**Manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes, or changing data.**

> *Example:* A researcher removes 20 participants from their dataset because including them makes the results non-significant, without reporting the exclusion.

**Consequences:** Same as fabrication.

### 35.2.3 Plagiarism

**Using others' words, ideas, or work without proper attribution.**

Types of plagiarism:
- **Direct plagiarism** — Copying text word-for-word without quotation marks or citation
- **Self-plagiarism** — Submitting your own previous work without disclosure
- **Mosaic plagiarism** — Patching together phrases from multiple sources without citation
- **Ideas plagiarism** — Taking someone's unique idea and presenting it as your own

> *Example:* A student copies three paragraphs from a published paper, changes a few words, and includes it in their thesis without citation. This is plagiarism, even with the word changes.

### 35.2.4 Questionable Research Practices (QRPs)

QRPs are not outright fraud but undermine research integrity:

- **P-hacking** — Running multiple analyses until you find a significant result
- **HARKing** — Hypothesizing After Results are Known (presenting post-hoc hypotheses as if they were a priori)
- **Selective reporting** — Only reporting significant outcomes
- **Salami slicing** — Splitting one study into multiple papers to inflate publication count
- **Citation manipulation** — Citing papers to increase their citation count, not because they are relevant

> **Pro Tip:** Pre-registration is the best defense against p-hacking and HARKing. By registering your hypotheses and analysis plan before collecting data, you commit to testing specific predictions rather than fishing for significance.

## 35.3 The Publication Process and Ethics

### 35.3.1 Authorship

Authorship should reflect genuine intellectual contribution. The APA and ICMJE recommend that authors:

1. Made substantial contributions to conception, design, data collection, or analysis
2. Drafted or critically revised the manuscript
3. Approved the final version
4. Agreed to be accountable for the work

**Ghost authorship:** Omitting someone who contributed significantly
**Gift authorship:** Including someone who did not contribute significantly

Both are unethical.

> *Example:* Including your supervisor as an author on a paper they did not contribute to (gift authorship) is just as unethical as excluding a research assistant who collected and analyzed all the data (ghost authorship).

### 35.3.2 Conflicts of Interest

Disclose any financial, personal, or professional relationships that could influence your research:

- Funding sources
- Advisory board memberships
- Personal relationships with participants or co-authors
- Financial interests in the outcome (e.g., if you developed the therapy being tested)

### 35.3.3 Peer Review Ethics

If you are a peer reviewer:

- **Be constructive** — Critique the work, not the person
- **Be timely** — Return reviews by the deadline
- **Be confidential** — Do not share or use unpublished manuscripts
- **Declare conflicts** — If you know the authors or have a competing interest, recuse yourself

## 35.4 Open Science Practices

Open science makes research more transparent, reproducible, and trustworthy.

### 35.4.1 Pre-Registration

Register your study hypotheses, methods, and analysis plan **before** collecting data.

**Platforms:**
- **OSF (Open Science Framework)** — Free, widely used
- **AsPredicted** — Simple, quick registration
- **ClinicalTrials.gov** — For clinical trials

### 35.4.2 Open Data

Share your de-identified data so others can verify and build upon your findings.

**Benefits:**
- Increases transparency and trust
- Allows replication and meta-analysis
- Increases citation rates

**Considerations:**
- Must protect participant privacy (de-identify before sharing)
- Some data cannot be shared (sensitive clinical data, identifiable information)
- Provide a data dictionary explaining variables

### 35.4.3 Open Materials

Share your survey instruments, analysis scripts, and experimental code.

**Platforms:**
- **OSF** — For all materials
- **GitHub** — For code
- **PsyArXiv** — For preprints

### 35.4.4 Registered Reports

A publication format where the journal reviews and accepts the study **before** data collection, based on the research question and methods. This eliminates publication bias and p-hacking.

> **Pro Tip:** Consider submitting your next study as a registered report. Journals like *Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology*, *BMC Psychology*, and *PLOS ONE* accept this format.

## 35.5 Handling Research Misconduct

### If You Witness Misconduct

1. **Document** — Keep records of what you observed
2. **Report** — Inform your institution's research integrity officer
3. **Protect** — Do not confront the individual directly
4. **Cooperate** — Assist with any investigation

### If You Make a Mistake

1. **Acknowledge** — Do not try to hide errors
2. **Correct** — Issue a correction or erratum
3. **Retract** — If the error is severe, retract the paper
4. **Learn** — Update your practices to prevent recurrence

## 35.6 Ethics in the Indian Context

### Regulatory Framework

- **ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017)** — For biomedical and health research
- **UGC Regulations** — For academic integrity in universities
- **Institutional Ethics Committees (IECs)** — Review research proposals
- **National Ethical Review Guidelines for Social Science Research** — For social science research

### Common Challenges

- **Pressure to publish** — The "publish or perish" culture can tempt misconduct
- **Limited training** — Many researchers receive little formal ethics training
- **Supervisory power dynamics** — Students may feel pressured to include supervisors as authors
- **Language barriers** — Plagiarism may be harder to detect in non-English publications
- **Resource constraints** — Limited access to ethical review infrastructure

### Building an Ethical Culture

- **Mentorship** — Senior researchers should model ethical behavior
- **Training** — Institutions should provide regular research ethics workshops
- **Support** — Create safe channels for reporting misconduct
- **Recognition** — Reward transparency and integrity, not just publication quantity

## 35.7 A Checklist for Ethical Research

### Before Data Collection
- [ ] Ethics approval obtained from institutional committee
- [ ] Informed consent form prepared and approved
- [ ] Pre-registration completed (if applicable)
- [ ] Conflicts of interest disclosed

### During Data Collection
- [ ] Participants treated with respect and dignity
- [ ] Confidentiality maintained
- [ ] Data stored securely
- [ ] Adverse events monitored and reported

### During Analysis
- [ ] All pre-registered analyses conducted
- [ ] No p-hacking or selective reporting
- [ ] Outliers handled according to pre-specified criteria
- [ ] Assumptions checked and reported

### During Writing
- [ ] All sources properly cited
- [ ] No plagiarism (self or otherwise)
- [ ] Authorship reflects genuine contributions
- [ ] Limitations honestly acknowledged

### Before Submission
- [ ] All co-authors approved the manuscript
- [ ] Conflicts of interest disclosed
- [ ] Data availability statement included
- [ ] Ethics approval number included in methods

## Key Takeaways

- **Research integrity** rests on honesty, transparency, respect, and responsibility.
- **Fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism** are the three pillars of research misconduct.
- **Questionable research practices** (p-hacking, HARKing, selective reporting) are also harmful.
- **Authorship** should reflect genuine contribution — no ghost or gift authorship.
- **Open science** (pre-registration, open data, open materials) increases transparency and trust.
- **Indian context** — follow ICMR guidelines, address power dynamics, and build an ethical culture.
- **Ethics is not a hurdle** — it is what makes your research credible and trustworthy.
