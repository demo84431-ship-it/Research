# Chapter 22: Mixed Methods Design

## Opening

Quantitative research tells you *what* and *how much*. Qualitative research tells you *how* and *why*. Mixed methods research combines both, providing a more complete picture than either approach alone. In psychology, some questions are best answered with numbers, others with words, and many with both. This chapter covers the philosophy behind mixed methods, the major designs, how to integrate quantitative and qualitative data, and how to report mixed methods studies effectively.

---

## 22.1 What Is Mixed Methods Research?

Mixed methods research is an approach that **combines quantitative and qualitative data collection and analysis** within a single study or program of research. The goal is to leverage the strengths of each approach while compensating for their weaknesses.

**Quantitative strengths:** Generalizable, precise, testable hypotheses
**Quantitative weaknesses:** May miss context, nuance, and meaning

**Qualitative strengths:** Rich, contextual, explores meaning and experience
**Qualitative weaknesses:** Not generalizable, subjective, time-consuming

**Mixed methods:** Gets the best of both worlds — when done well.

> *Example:* You survey 500 adolescents about social media use and anxiety (quantitative), then interview 20 of them to understand *how* social media affects their daily lives (qualitative). The numbers show the relationship; the interviews explain the mechanism.

---

## 22.2 Philosophical Foundations

Mixed methods research is often associated with **pragmatism** — the philosophy that the best method is the one that answers the research question, regardless of paradigm.

| Paradigm | View of Reality | Method Preference |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Positivism** | Objective, measurable | Quantitative |
| **Constructivism** | Subjective, socially constructed | Qualitative |
| **Pragmatism** | What works | Mixed methods |

> **Pro Tip:** You do not need to commit to a single philosophical paradigm. Pragmatism allows you to use whatever methods best serve your research questions.

## 22.3 Types of Mixed Methods Designs

### 22.3.1 Convergent (Concurrent) Design

Quantitative and qualitative data are collected **at the same time**, analyzed separately, and then compared or merged.

**Timing:** QUAN + QUAL simultaneously
**Purpose:** To see if findings from both methods converge (agree)

> *Example:* Administer a depression scale to 300 patients (QUAN) and conduct interviews with 20 patients (QUAL) at the same time. Compare whether the themes from interviews align with the survey results.

### 22.3.2 Explanatory Sequential Design

Quantitative data is collected **first**, and qualitative data is collected **second** to help explain the quantitative findings.

**Timing:** QUAN → QUAL
**Purpose:** To explain unexpected or interesting quantitative results

> *Example:* A survey finds that 40% of participants who use a mental health app report no improvement. You then interview 15 of these non-responders to understand why the app did not work for them.

### 22.3.3 Exploratory Sequential Design

Qualitative data is collected **first** to explore a topic, and quantitative data is collected **second** to test or measure the qualitative findings.

**Timing:** QUAL → QUAN
**Purpose:** To develop a measurement tool or test emerging theories

> *Example:* You interview 25 therapists about their experiences with AI tools (QUAL), identify key themes, then develop a survey measuring those themes and administer it to 500 therapists (QUAN).

### 22.3.4 Embedded Design

One method is embedded within a study primarily using the other method.

**Timing:** QUAN(qual) or QUAL(quan)
**Purpose:** To supplement the primary method

> *Example:* In a large RCT testing a new therapy (QUAN), you embed qualitative interviews with 15 participants to understand their experience of the therapy (qual).

### 22.3.5 Transformative Design

Uses a theoretical lens (e.g., feminism, critical race theory) to guide the entire mixed methods study.

**Timing:** Varies
**Purpose:** To give voice to marginalized populations while also generating generalizable data

## 22.4 Integration of Methods

The key to good mixed methods research is **integration** — not just collecting both types of data, but connecting them.

### 22.4.1 Integration at the Design Stage

- **Merge:** Combine QUAN and QUAL during interpretation
- **Connect:** Use one method to inform the other
- **Embed:** Place one method within the other

### 22.4.2 Integration at the Analysis Stage

- **Joint display:** A table showing quantitative results alongside qualitative themes
- **Meta-inference:** A conclusion that draws on both datasets
- **Transforming:** Converting qualitative codes into quantitative variables (quantitizing) or vice versa (qualitizing)

### 22.4.3 Joint Display Example

| Quantitative Finding | Qualitative Theme | Integration |
|---------------------|-------------------|-------------|
| 40% reported app not helpful | "Felt impersonal" | App lacks human connection |
| Higher engagement in younger users | "It's like a game" | Gamification increases engagement |
| No change in anxiety scores | "I use it but don't do the exercises" | Low adherence explains null results |

## 22.5 Worked Example: AI Therapy Experience

**Research question:** How do Indian college students experience AI-powered therapy, and what factors predict engagement and satisfaction?

**Design:** Explanatory sequential mixed methods

**Phase 1 — Quantitative:**
- 200 students use an AI therapy chatbot for 4 weeks
- Pre-post measures: GAD-7, PHQ-9, Working Alliance Inventory
- Usage data: frequency, duration, modules completed
- Analysis: Paired t-tests, regression, mediation

**Phase 2 — Qualitative:**
- Semi-structured interviews with 20 students (selected from Phase 1: 10 high engagers, 10 low engagers)
- Interview topics: experience, perceived helpfulness, barriers, suggestions
- Analysis: Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006)

**Integration:**
- Joint display comparing quantitative predictors with qualitative themes
- Meta-inferences drawing on both datasets

**Hypothetical findings:**
> Phase 1: Anxiety scores decreased significantly (d = 0.65). Working alliance predicted engagement (β = .42, p < .001). Phase 2: Students who engaged more described the AI as "always available" and "non-judgmental." Low engagers reported "feeling like talking to a machine" and "preferring human connection." Integration: Working alliance — the sense of connection with the AI — was both a significant quantitative predictor and the dominant qualitative theme, suggesting that relational factors matter even in human-AI interactions.

## 22.6 Sample Size in Mixed Methods

There is no single rule for sample size in mixed methods. General guidance:

| Component | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Quantitative** | Follow power analysis for your statistical tests |
| **Qualitative** | 12–30 interviews for thematic analysis (data saturation) |
| **Priority** | Give more weight to the component that answers your primary question |

> **Pro Tip:** In convergent designs, the quantitative and qualitative samples can be different. In sequential designs, the qualitative sample is often drawn from the quantitative sample.

---

## 22.7 Reporting Mixed Methods Studies

Follow the **GRAMMS** (Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study) guidelines:

1. **Describe the justification** for using mixed methods
2. **Describe the design** (convergent, sequential, embedded, etc.)
3. **Describe each method** in enough detail for replication
4. **Describe the integration** — how were QUAN and QUAL connected?
5. **Describe the limitations** of the mixed methods approach
6. **Describe the insights** gained from mixing methods that would not have been gained from either alone

---

## 22.8 Common Mistakes

1. **Collecting both but not integrating** — Two separate studies in one paper is not mixed methods
2. **Unequal quality** — Strong quantitative analysis with weak qualitative analysis (or vice versa)
3. **No justification** — "I used mixed methods because my supervisor said to" is not a justification
4. **Ignoring contradictions** — What do you do when QUAN and QUAL findings disagree? Address it.
5. **Overwhelming the reader** — Mixed methods papers are long; be concise in each component

> *Example:* **Weak justification:** "A mixed methods approach was chosen to provide a comprehensive understanding." **Strong justification:** "An explanatory sequential design was used because quantitative survey results identified a significant association between social media use and anxiety, and qualitative interviews were needed to understand the mechanisms underlying this association from the participants' perspective."

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Mixed methods research** combines quantitative and qualitative approaches to provide a more complete understanding.
- Major designs include **convergent** (simultaneous), **explanatory sequential** (QUAN → QUAL), and **exploratory sequential** (QUAL → QUAN).
- **Integration** is the key — connect your quantitative and qualitative findings through joint displays, meta-inferences, or transformation.
- Use **pragmatism** as your philosophical foundation — choose methods that answer your research questions.
- Follow **GRAMMS guidelines** for reporting and justify why mixed methods is appropriate for your study.
- **Do not just collect both types of data** — integrate them in analysis and interpretation.
