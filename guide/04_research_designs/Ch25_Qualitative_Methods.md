## 18.1 What Is Qualitative Research?

Quantitative research counts and measures. Qualitative research explores and understands. When you want to know *how much* or *how many*, you use numbers. When you want to know *how* and *why* — how people experience something, why they think the way they do, what meaning they attach to events — you use qualitative methods.

Qualitative research is not "less rigorous" than quantitative research. It is differently rigorous. Where quantitative research uses statistical power, sample size calculations, and p-values to establish rigor, qualitative research uses thick description, reflexivity, member checking, and triangulation to establish **trustworthiness**.

### Philosophical Foundations

Before you start coding data, you should know where you stand philosophically. Your epistemology (theory of knowledge) and ontology (theory of reality) shape every decision you make:

- **Positivism**: Reality is objective and measurable. This is the default stance of quantitative research.
- **Constructivism/Interpretivism**: Reality is constructed through human experience and language. Multiple valid perspectives exist. This is the default stance of most qualitative research.
- **Critical Realism**: An objective reality exists, but our access to it is always mediated by language, culture, and perspective. This is increasingly popular in psychology.

Most qualitative psychology papers adopt a constructivist or critical realist position. You do not need to write a philosophy treatise, but a sentence or two in your methods section stating your epistemological position adds depth and clarity.

## 18.2 Major Qualitative Approaches in Psychology

### 18.2.1 Thematic Analysis

The most widely used method in psychology. Identifies, analyzes, and reports patterns (themes) across data. Flexible — can be used with any theoretical framework.

### 18.2.2 Grounded Theory

Develops theory from data through iterative coding and comparison. Uses theoretical sampling, constant comparison, and theoretical saturation. More structured than thematic analysis.

### 18.2.3 Interpretive Phenomenological Analysis (IPA)

Explores how individuals make sense of their lived experiences. Focuses on personal meaning-making. Uses small samples (3–6 participants) for deep analysis.

### 18.2.4 Narrative Analysis

Analyzes the stories people tell — how they structure their experiences into narratives with characters, plots, and themes.

### 18.2.5 Discourse Analysis

Examines how language constructs social reality. Focuses on what language *does* rather than what it *says*.

For this chapter, we will focus primarily on **thematic analysis** because it is the most common, the most flexible, and the best starting point for researchers new to qualitative methods.

## 18.3 Thematic Analysis: Braun and Clarke's 6-Step Model

Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase model is the most cited approach to thematic analysis in psychology. Their 2006 paper has been cited over 100,000 times. Here is how it works:

### Phase 1: Familiarization

Immerse yourself in the data. Read and re-read transcripts. Listen to audio recordings. Write initial observations.

**Practical steps:**
- Transcribe your data verbatim (or use AI transcription tools and then verify)
- Read each transcript at least twice
- Write brief notes in the margins: "This is interesting," "This connects to X," "This participant seems conflicted"
- Do not start formal coding yet — just get to know your data

### Phase 2: Generating Initial Codes

Codes are the building blocks of your analysis. A code is a label that captures what a segment of data is about.

**Example:**

> Participant: "After the lockdown started, I stopped going to the gym. At first I thought I'd exercise at home, but I just couldn't motivate myself. The living room doesn't feel like a gym, you know?"

Possible codes:
- Loss of exercise routine
- Failed intention to adapt
- Environmental barrier
- Motivation decline
- Place identity (gym vs. home)

**How to code:**
1. Go through each transcript systematically
2. Code every segment that seems relevant to your research question
3. Use descriptive codes (what the participant says) and interpretive codes (what it might mean)
4. Keep codes close to the data — do not over-interpret at this stage

**In NVivo:**
- Create a new project and import your transcripts
- Highlight text segments and assign codes (called "nodes" in NVivo)
- You can code at multiple levels: descriptive, interpretive, and pattern codes

### Phase 3: Searching for Themes

Once you have a long list of codes (often 80–200+), start grouping them into potential themes. A theme is a pattern that captures something important about the data in relation to your research question.

**Process:**
1. Print out or display all your codes
2. Look for codes that cluster together — they seem to be about the same thing
3. Create a "miscellaneous" pile for codes that do not fit anywhere yet
4. Use sticky notes, mind maps, or NVivo's coding stripes to visualize clusters

**Example:**

| Codes | Potential Theme |
|---|---|
| Loss of routine, gym closure, disrupted schedule, no structure | Disruption of established routines |
| Motivation decline, procrastination, inertia, guilt | Motivational barriers |
| Home workouts, online classes, walking, creative solutions | Adaptation and resilience |
| Anxiety about going out, fear of infection, health anxiety | Pandemic-related anxiety |

### Phase 4: Reviewing Themes

Check your themes against the data. Ask:
- Does this theme have enough supporting data? (At least 3–4 coded extracts)
- Is the theme coherent? Do all the codes within it really belong together?
- Is the theme too broad? Should it be split?
- Is the theme too narrow? Should it be merged with another?
- Does the theme accurately represent the data, or have I imposed my own interpretation?

**Two levels of review:**
1. Level 1: Check themes against coded extracts. Re-read the data within each theme. Does it cohere?
2. Level 2: Check themes against the entire dataset. Re-read all transcripts. Are there important data that your themes do not capture?

### Phase 5: Defining and Naming Themes

For each theme, write:
- A clear name (concise, evocative, informative)
- A definition: What exactly does this theme capture?
- The scope: What is included and what is excluded?
- The story: How does this theme relate to your research question?

**Good theme names:**
- "The Invisible Burden: Hidden Labor in Remote Work"
- "Between Fear and Freedom: Navigating Health Behaviors During COVID-19"
- "Digital Exhaustion: The Paradox of Online Connection"

**Bad theme names:**
- "Theme 1" (says nothing)
- "Participants discussed motivation" (too vague)
- "The relationship between self-efficacy and academic performance in undergraduate students aged 18–25 in urban India" (this is a research question, not a theme name)

### Phase 6: Writing Up

The final phase is producing the report. This is where many students stumble — they have great themes but cannot write about them compellingly.

**Structure for presenting themes:**

For each theme:
1. **Introduce the theme** (1–2 sentences): What is this theme about? Why does it matter?
2. **Describe the theme** (1–2 paragraphs): What patterns did you find? How prevalent was this theme?
3. **Illustrate with quotes** (2–4 quotes): Direct quotes from participants. Choose quotes that are clear, concise, and representative. Do not use long, rambling passages.
4. **Interpret the theme** (1–2 paragraphs): What does this theme mean? How does it connect to existing literature? How does it answer your research question?

**Common Mistake:** Presenting a quote without interpretation. A quote is evidence, not analysis. After presenting a quote, explain what it tells you and why it matters.

## 18.4 Grounded Theory Basics

Grounded theory was developed by Glaser and Strauss (1967) and aims to generate theory from data, rather than testing existing theories. If thematic analysis asks "What patterns exist?", grounded theory asks "What theory explains these patterns?"

### Key Features

- **Theoretical sampling**: You do not decide your full sample upfront. Instead, you collect data, analyze it, and then decide who to sample next based on what you are finding. You sample for theoretical development, not representativeness.
- **Constant comparison**: You compare data with data, code with code, category with category. This iterative comparison is the engine of theory development.
- **Theoretical saturation**: You stop sampling when new data no longer generates new insights. Your categories are "saturated."
- **Memo-writing**: Throughout the process, you write memos — analytical notes about your emerging ideas, connections, and theoretical insights.

### When to Use Grounded Theory

- When you want to develop a new theory, not just describe patterns
- When existing theories do not adequately explain your phenomenon
- When you have the time and resources for iterative data collection and analysis

### When NOT to Use Grounded Theory

- When you are describing experiences (use thematic analysis or IPA)
- When you have a fixed dataset and cannot collect more (theoretical sampling is not possible)
- When you are new to qualitative research (it is more complex than thematic analysis)

## 18.5 Phenomenological Approaches

Phenomenology asks: "What is the essence of this experience?" It is concerned with how individuals perceive and make meaning of a particular phenomenon.

### Interpretive Phenomenological Analysis (IPA)

Developed by Jonathan Smith, IPA combines phenomenology (examining experience) with hermeneutics (interpretation) and idiography (focus on the particular).

**Key features:**
- Small samples (typically 3–6 participants)
- In-depth, semi-structured interviews
- Double hermeneutic: The researcher is trying to make sense of the participant who is trying to make sense of their experience
- Analysis moves from individual cases to cross-case patterns

**When to use IPA:**
- When your research question is about lived experience
- When you want deep, idiographic analysis
- When participants have a shared experience (e.g., all survived a specific event)

## 18.6 Coding in Practice: A Worked Example

Let us walk through coding a hypothetical interview excerpt.

**Research question**: "How did healthcare workers experience moral distress during the COVID-19 pandemic?"

**Interview excerpt:**

> "There was one night — I'll never forget it — we had three patients dying at the same time and only one ventilator available. The doctor had to choose. I understood the decision intellectually, but emotionally... I felt like I was abandoning the other two. I went home that night and couldn't sleep. I kept thinking, 'What if that was my mother?' I didn't talk about it with anyone for weeks. I was afraid people would think I was weak or that I couldn't handle the job."

**Level 1 codes (descriptive):**
- Resource scarcity (ventilator shortage)
- Triage decision witnessed
- Emotional distress
- Survivor guilt ("abandoning")
- Personal connection ("my mother")
- Sleep disturbance
- Silence and suppression
- Fear of judgment
- Professional identity threat

**Level 2 codes (interpretive):**
- Moral injury from impossible choices
- Emotional labor of healthcare work
- Personal-professional boundary erosion
- Stigma around vulnerability
- Isolation in suffering

**Potential theme**: "Trapped Between Duty and Conscience: The Moral Isolation of Frontline Workers"

This theme captures the pattern: healthcare workers witnessed ethically impossible situations, felt deep distress, but could not express it because of professional norms and fear of judgment.

## 18.7 Using NVivo: A Workflow Guide

NVivo is the most widely used qualitative data analysis software. Here is a practical workflow:

### Step 1: Create a New Project

Open NVivo → File → New Project → Name it descriptively (e.g., "COVID_Moral_Distress_2024").

### Step 2: Import Data

- Import transcripts (Word or PDF files)
- Import audio/video files if you want to code directly from media
- Organize files into folders (e.g., by participant ID or data collection wave)

### Step 3: Create a Node Structure

- Start with broad "parent nodes" (potential themes)
- Create "child nodes" (sub-themes or codes within themes)
- You can restructure as your analysis evolves

### Step 4: Code Your Data

- Open a transcript
- Highlight a text segment
- Right-click → Code Selection → At Existing or New Node
- Code at multiple nodes if a segment is relevant to more than one theme

### Step 5: Use Queries

- **Word frequency query**: Which words appear most often? Useful for initial familiarization.
- **Text search query**: Find all instances of a specific word or phrase.
- **Coding query**: See all data coded at a specific node. This is how you review your themes.
- **Matrix coding query**: Cross-tabulate codes by demographic variables (e.g., do male and female participants differ in their experiences?).

### Step 6: Visualize

- Use NVivo's explore tools to create mind maps, hierarchical charts, and comparison diagrams
- These are useful for presentations and for your own analytical thinking

### Step 7: Export and Write

- Export coded data (with node summaries) to Word for writing up
- NVivo does not write your analysis for you — it organizes your data so you can think more clearly

**Pro Tip:** NVivo is a tool, not an analytical framework. It helps you manage data, but the intellectual work — coding, interpreting, theorizing — is yours. Do not let the software drive your analysis.

**Hypothetical reconstruction based on common qualitative psychology papers:**

Let me instead provide a structural template based on how well-written qualitative papers in psychology are organized, drawing on the conventions established by Braun and Clarke and demonstrated across hundreds of published papers.

### Structure of a Qualitative Psychology Paper

**Title**: Should reflect the methodology (e.g., "A Qualitative Study of...", "Exploring... Through Thematic Analysis", "Lived Experiences of...")

**Abstract**: Includes the research question, method (qualitative approach), sample, key findings (themes), and conclusion.

**Introduction**:
1. Context and background
2. What existing research has found (brief literature review)
3. What gap exists (often: quantitative studies have measured prevalence, but we lack understanding of lived experience)
4. Research question and aim

**Methods**:
1. **Research design**: State the qualitative approach (thematic analysis, IPA, grounded theory)
2. **Epistemological position**: Briefly state where you stand (constructivist, critical realist)
3. **Participants**: Who, how many, how recruited, inclusion/exclusion criteria
4. **Data collection**: Interview format (semi-structured), guide development, interview duration, setting
5. **Data analysis**: Step-by-step description of the analytical process
6. **Rigor/trustworthiness**: How you ensured quality (reflexivity, member checking, peer debriefing, audit trail)
7. **Ethics**: Approval, consent, confidentiality

**Results/Findings**:
- Organized by themes, with sub-themes
- Each theme has: a name, a description, illustrative quotes, and interpretive commentary
- A thematic map (figure) showing the relationship between themes

**Discussion**:
- Interpretation of findings in relation to research question
- Connection to existing literature
- Implications (clinical, policy, theoretical)
- Limitations
- Future research directions

**Conclusion**: Brief summary of key insights

## 18.9 Rigor and Trustworthiness

Qualitative research does not use p-values and confidence intervals to demonstrate rigor. Instead, it uses the concept of **trustworthiness**, which has four components (Lincoln & Guba, 1985):

### Credibility (≈ Internal Validity)

Did you accurately capture participants' meanings?

Strategies:
- **Member checking**: Share your themes with participants and ask if they resonate
- **Prolonged engagement**: Spend enough time with your data to understand it deeply
- **Triangulation**: Use multiple data sources, methods, or analysts
- **Peer debriefing**: Discuss your analysis with a colleague who challenges your interpretations

### Transferability (≈ External Validity)

Can your findings apply to other contexts?

Strategy:
- **Thick description**: Provide enough detail about your participants, context, and methods that readers can judge whether findings apply to their situation

### Dependability (≈ Reliability)

Would another researcher find similar patterns?

Strategies:
- **Audit trail**: Document every decision you made — from research question to final themes
- **Transparent methods**: Describe your process in enough detail that someone could replicate it

### Confirmability (≈ Objectivity)

Are your findings grounded in the data rather than your biases?

Strategies:
- **Reflexivity**: Acknowledge your position, assumptions, and potential biases. Write a reflexive statement.
- **Audit trail**: Same as above — transparency allows others to check your work

### Writing a Reflexivity Statement

```markdown
## Researcher Positionality

The first author is a clinical psychologist with 5 years of experience 
working with trauma survivors. This professional background may have 
influenced the analysis in several ways: a tendency to interpret 
participants' experiences through a clinical lens, and a potential bias 
toward emphasizing pathology over resilience. To mitigate this, the 
second author, who has no clinical background, independently coded 
all transcripts and discrepancies were discussed.
```

## 18.10 Template: Methods Section for a Qualitative Study

```markdown
## Methods

### Research Design
This study employed [thematic analysis / IPA / grounded theory] to 
explore [phenomenon]. We adopted a [constructivist / critical realist] 
epistemological position, recognizing that participants' experiences 
are mediated through language and context.

### Participants
[N] participants were recruited using [sampling method]. Inclusion 
criteria: [list]. Participants ranged in age from [X] to [Y] years 
(M = [Z], SD = [W]). [Demographic summary table.]

### Data Collection
Data were collected through semi-structured interviews lasting [X–Y] 
minutes. An interview guide was developed based on [literature review / 
expert consultation / pilot interviews] (see Appendix A). Interviews 
were conducted [in person / via video call] by [who]. All interviews 
were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

### Data Analysis
Data were analyzed following Braun and Clarke's (2006, 2019) six-phase 
thematic analysis: (1) familiarization with the data, (2) generating 
initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, 
(5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report. Coding 
was conducted using NVivo [version]. [Second coder details if applicable.]

### Trustworthiness
Credibility was enhanced through [member checking / peer debriefing / 
triangulation]. An audit trail was maintained throughout the analysis. 
The first author kept a reflexive journal to document assumptions and 
analytical decisions. Thick description is provided to support 
transferability.

### Ethical Considerations
This study was approved by [institution] Ethics Committee (No. [XXXX]). 
All participants provided written informed consent. Pseudonyms are used 
to protect confidentiality.
```

## 18.11 Checklist: Before You Submit

- [ ] The qualitative approach is clearly named and justified
- [ ] Epistemological position is stated (even briefly)
- [ ] Participants and sampling are described in detail
- [ ] Data collection procedure is transparent (interview guide in appendix)
- [ ] Analysis follows a named, systematic approach (not just "we analyzed the data thematically")
- [ ] Themes are supported by direct quotes with interpretive commentary
- [ ] A thematic map or figure is included
- [ ] Trustworthiness is addressed (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability)
- [ ] Reflexivity/positionality is discussed
- [ ] Ethics approval and informed consent are reported
- [ ] Limitations are honestly discussed

## 18.12 Common Mistakes in Qualitative Research

**Mistake 1: "Themes Emerged"**

Themes do not emerge — you construct them through active, iterative analysis. Braun and Clarke are emphatic about this. Use language like "we identified," "we constructed," or "we developed" themes.

**Mistake 2: Collecting Data, Then Analyzing**

Qualitative analysis should begin during data collection. After each interview, write memos, note emerging ideas, and refine your interview guide. Analysis and collection are iterative, not sequential.

**Mistake 3: Weak Themes**

A theme is not just a topic. "Participants discussed their experiences" is not a theme. A theme has a central organizing concept, a story, and interpretive depth.

**Mistake 4: Quote Dumping**

Presenting long quotes without interpretation. Every quote should be introduced, presented, and then interpreted. The quote is evidence for your analysis — it is not the analysis itself.

**Mistake 5: Ignoring Negative Cases**

When most participants say one thing but one says the opposite, do not ignore the outlier. Discuss it. It may reveal important boundary conditions or alternative perspectives.

**Mistake 6: Claiming Generalizability**

Qualitative findings are not generalizable in the statistical sense. Use language like "transferability" — your findings may resonate with readers in similar contexts, but they are not meant to represent a population.

## 18.13 Key Takeaways

1. Qualitative research is rigorous, systematic, and valuable — not "soft" science.
2. Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke) is the most accessible and widely used method. Master it first.
3. Code data carefully, group codes into themes, and always interpret — do not just describe.
4. Use NVivo to organize your data, but remember that software does not think for you.
5. Trustworthiness (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability) is how you demonstrate rigor.
6. Be reflexive. Acknowledge your position and how it shapes your analysis.
7. Themes are constructed by you, the researcher. They do not "emerge" from the data like magic.
