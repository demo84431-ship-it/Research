## 15.1 What Is a Narrative Review?

Imagine you have been reading dozens of papers on a topic — say, the psychological effects of social media on adolescents. You notice patterns, contradictions, gaps. A narrative review is your chance to step back, synthesize what you have read, and tell the story of a research field as you understand it.

Unlike a systematic review (which follows a rigid, pre-registered protocol), a **narrative review** offers the author more interpretive freedom. You select the literature you consider most relevant, organize it thematically or chronologically, and present an argument or overview that advances understanding of the topic.

A **mini-review** is simply a shorter, more focused version. It typically covers a narrower slice of a topic, uses fewer sources (perhaps 30–60 papers instead of hundreds), and is often published in journals that value concise, timely syntheses.

### Key Characteristics

| Feature | Narrative Review | Systematic Review |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Broad, exploratory | Specific, PICO-structured |
| Search strategy | Flexible, author-driven | Pre-registered, exhaustive |
| Selection criteria | Subjective judgment | Explicit inclusion/exclusion |
| Synthesis | Thematic, interpretive | Quantitative (meta-analysis) or structured qualitative |
| Bias risk | Higher (selection bias) | Lower (rigorous protocol) |
| Length | Variable (3,000–8,000 words) | Often longer (5,000–12,000 words) |

**When should you write a narrative review?**

- When a field is emerging and too few studies exist for a meta-analysis
- When you want to offer a fresh perspective or theoretical synthesis
- When the topic is broad and you want to map the landscape
- When you have deep expertise and can offer interpretive insight that a purely systematic approach cannot

## 15.2 The Purpose and Audience

Before you write a single word, ask yourself two questions:

1. **What is the purpose of this review?** Are you summarizing a field, resolving a debate, identifying gaps, or proposing a new framework?
2. **Who is the audience?** A clinical audience wants practical takeaways. A cognitive neuroscience audience wants mechanistic detail. A policy audience wants implications.

Your answers shape everything — the depth of methodological discussion, the style of writing, the kind of examples you choose.

## 15.3 How to Organize a Review Paper

There is no single correct structure, but most successful narrative reviews follow a recognizable pattern:

### Structure Template

```
1. Title
2. Abstract
3. Introduction
   - Background and context
   - Rationale: Why this review, why now?
   - Objective/scope statement
4. Body (Thematic Sections)
   - Theme 1: [Concept or Finding Area]
   - Theme 2: [Concept or Finding Area]
   - Theme 3: [Concept or Finding Area]
   - (Each theme: define → present evidence → interpret → transition)
5. Discussion
   - Synthesis: What does the collective evidence tell us?
   - Gaps and contradictions
   - Implications (clinical, theoretical, policy)
   - Limitations of the review itself
6. Conclusion
   - Key takeaways (3–5 bullet points work well)
   - Future directions
7. References
```

### Choosing Your Organizational Strategy

You have three main options:

- **Thematic**: Group studies by topic or concept. Best when the field has several distinct sub-areas.
- **Chronological**: Trace how thinking has evolved over time. Best for historical or developmental topics.
- **Methodological**: Organize by how studies were conducted. Best when methods are the key issue (e.g., comparing experimental vs. correlational findings).

Most mini-reviews use the thematic approach because it is the most flexible and reader-friendly.

## 15.4 Worked Example: Deconstructing a Published Mini-Review

Let us now take apart a real paper to see how it was built.

**Paper**: Mittal, S., & Singh, T. (2020). Gender-Based Violence During COVID-19 Pandemic: A Mini-Review. *Frontiers in Global Women's Health*, 1, 4.

This paper examines how the COVID-19 pandemic created conditions that exacerbated gender-based violence (GBV) worldwide. It was published at a time when the world was in lockdown and emerging reports suggested a shadow pandemic of domestic violence.

### 15.4.1 The Title

> "Gender-Based Violence During COVID-19 Pandemic: A Mini-Review"

**Why this works:**
- It names the topic (GBV), the context (COVID-19), and the paper type (mini-review)
- A reader scanning a database immediately knows what this is about
- The term "mini-review" sets expectations about scope and length

**Pro Tip:** Always signal the paper type in your title when writing reviews. Use phrases like "A Narrative Review," "A Mini-Review," "A Scoping Review," or "A Critical Review" so readers and editors know what they are getting.

### 15.4.2 The Abstract

The abstract follows a structured format: Background → Purpose → Methods (brief) → Key Findings → Conclusion. Even though this is a narrative review, the authors describe their search approach briefly ("We reviewed literature on...") and summarize the main themes they identified.

**Lesson**: Even for a narrative review, your abstract should mention how you selected the literature. It does not need to be a full PRISMA statement, but a sentence like "We searched PubMed, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar using the terms..." adds credibility.

### 15.4.3 The Introduction

The introduction does several things in sequence:

1. **Opens with a global context**: The authors describe the COVID-19 pandemic and the public health response (lockdowns, quarantines).
2. **Introduces the problem**: They note that confinement measures, while necessary for public health, create conditions known to increase GBV — economic stress, isolation, disrupted support services.
3. **Provides historical precedent**: They reference prior crises (Ebola, natural disasters) where GBV increased, establishing that this is not a new phenomenon.
4. **States the purpose**: The review aims to synthesize emerging evidence on GBV during COVID-19 and identify vulnerable populations and intervention gaps.

**Why this structure works:**

The introduction follows a **funnel pattern** — it starts broad (global pandemic), narrows to the specific issue (GBV), provides evidence that the issue matters (historical precedent), and then states what this paper will do.

**Common Mistake:** Many student reviews start with a dictionary definition ("Gender-based violence is defined as..."). Avoid this. Start with the real-world context that makes your review necessary. Define terms naturally as you discuss them.

### 15.4.4 The Body Sections

The body of the mini-review is organized into thematic sections. Here is how the authors structured their argument:

**Section 1: The Scope of the Problem**

The authors present data from multiple countries showing that reports of domestic violence increased during lockdowns. They cite UN Women data, hotline call volumes, and police reports.

*What they are doing*: Establishing that the problem is real and widespread, not anecdotal.

**Section 2: Risk Factors and Vulnerable Populations**

The authors discuss why certain groups are more vulnerable: women in abusive relationships, children, LGBTQ+ individuals, migrant workers, and those in conflict zones. They connect each vulnerability to specific pandemic-related mechanisms (e.g., economic dependence, loss of access to shelters).

*What they are doing*: Moving from "what is happening" to "why it is happening and to whom." This deepens the analysis.

**Section 3: Mechanisms — How Pandemic Conditions Increase GBV**

Here the authors draw on established frameworks (e.g., the ecological model of violence) to explain the pathways: economic stress → frustration → violence; isolation → reduced access to help; alcohol consumption → increased aggression.

*What they are doing*: Connecting empirical observations to theoretical frameworks. This elevates the review from a summary to an analysis.

**Section 4: Interventions and Responses**

The authors review what governments, NGOs, and health systems have done — helplines, emergency shelters, mobile apps, awareness campaigns. They note successes and failures.

*What they are doing*: Making the review actionable. A good review does not just describe problems; it evaluates solutions.

**Section 5: Gaps and Future Directions**

The authors identify what we still do not know: the long-term psychological impact on survivors, the effectiveness of pandemic-specific interventions, the needs of marginalized populations.

*What they are doing*: Setting the agenda for future research. This is one of the most valuable contributions a review can make.

### 15.4.5 The Discussion and Conclusion

The discussion synthesizes the key themes and emphasizes the urgency of addressing GBV as a public health crisis intertwined with the pandemic. The conclusion is brief — three main takeaways and a call to action.

**Lesson**: In a mini-review, your conclusion should be sharp and memorable. Do not repeat everything. Distill the one or two insights that matter most.

## 15.5 The Writing Process: Step by Step

Now that you have seen how a real mini-review is structured, here is how to write your own:

### Step 1: Define Your Scope

Write a one-sentence statement of what your review covers and what it does not.

*Example*: "This review examines the relationship between mindfulness-based interventions and anxiety symptoms in university students, focusing on randomized controlled trials published between 2015 and 2023."

### Step 2: Search and Read Broadly

Use databases like PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Start with review articles to map the field, then drill into primary studies. Aim for 40–80 papers for a mini-review.

### Step 3: Build a Literature Matrix

Create a table with columns for: Author, Year, Sample, Method, Key Finding, and Relevance to Your Themes. This becomes your organizational backbone.

### Step 4: Identify 3–5 Themes

Read through your matrix and look for patterns. Group studies into thematic clusters. These become your body sections.

### Step 5: Write the Introduction Last (Almost)

Draft the body first so you know exactly what your review covers. Then write an introduction that sets up the reader for what is coming.

### Step 6: Write the Discussion as a Synthesis

Do not summarize each section again. Instead, answer: "If someone read only this discussion, what would they understand about the state of the field?"

### Step 7: Revise with Fresh Eyes

After finishing, wait at least 24 hours, then re-read. Check for: logical flow between sections, consistency of tone, and whether each paragraph advances the argument.

## 15.6 Template for Writing Your Own Narrative Review

```markdown
# [Title]: A Narrative Review

## Abstract
[Background: 2 sentences] [Purpose: 1 sentence] [Methods: 1-2 sentences 
describing search approach] [Key findings: 3-4 sentences] [Conclusion: 
1-2 sentences]

## 1. Introduction
- Open with the broader context (why this topic matters now)
- Narrow to the specific issue
- Briefly note what existing reviews have covered
- State your review's purpose and scope
- Mention your organizational approach (thematic, chronological, etc.)

## 2. [Theme 1 Title]
- Define and contextualize the concept
- Present key studies (synthesize, do not just list)
- Interpret: What do these findings mean?
- Transition to next theme

## 3. [Theme 2 Title]
- (Same pattern as above)

## 4. [Theme 3 Title]
- (Same pattern as above)

## 5. Discussion
- Synthesize across themes: What is the big picture?
- Identify contradictions and explain them if possible
- Discuss limitations of the review (not just the studies)
- Offer implications for practice, policy, or theory
- Suggest specific future research directions

## 6. Conclusion
- 3-5 key takeaways
- A forward-looking statement

## References
```

## 15.7 Checklist: Before You Submit

- [ ] The title clearly states the topic and paper type
- [ ] The abstract mentions how you selected the literature
- [ ] The introduction explains why this review is needed now
- [ ] Each body section has a clear purpose and transitions smoothly
- [ ] You synthesize across studies rather than listing them one by one
- [ ] The discussion identifies gaps and offers implications
- [ ] You acknowledge the limitations of a narrative review approach
- [ ] All claims are supported by citations
- [ ] The paper is within the journal's word limit for reviews

## 15.8 Common Mistakes in Narrative Reviews

**Mistake 1: The Annotated Bibliography**

Writing one paragraph per study without synthesis. Each paragraph should connect to the next and contribute to a larger argument.

**Mistake 2: The Missing Voice**

A narrative review should have a point of view. If your review could have been written by anyone who read the same papers, you are not adding enough interpretation.

**Mistake 3: Ignoring Contradictions**

When two studies disagree, do not sweep it under the rug. Discuss possible reasons: different populations, methods, measures, or cultural contexts.

**Mistake 4: No Scope Statement**

Readers need to know what you included and excluded. A sentence like "We focused on studies from 2010–2023 in English-language journals" manages expectations.

**Mistake 5: Forgetting Self-Critique**

Every review has limitations. Yours is not an exception. Acknowledge selection bias, language bias, and the limits of your expertise.

---

## 15.9 Key Takeaways

1. A narrative review is a scholarly argument, not a summary. You are telling the story of a field.
2. Mini-reviews are focused, concise, and timely — ideal for emerging or fast-moving topics.
3. Organize thematically unless you have a strong reason to use another structure.
4. Synthesize across studies; do not create an annotated bibliography.
5. Always state your scope, acknowledge your limitations, and end with a clear contribution.
6. Use the worked example from Mittal & Singh (2020) as a model: context → problem → mechanisms → solutions → gaps.
