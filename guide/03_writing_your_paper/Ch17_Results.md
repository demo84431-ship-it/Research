# Chapter 11: Results

## Opening

The results section is where you present what you found — nothing more, nothing less. It is not the place for interpretation, speculation, or discussion of what your findings mean. That comes later. The results section is a factual, organized presentation of your data and the outcomes of your statistical or qualitative analyses. Many researchers find this section challenging because the line between reporting and interpreting can feel thin. This chapter teaches you how to present quantitative and qualitative results clearly, how to use tables and figures effectively, how to report statistical tests correctly, and how to avoid the common mistakes that lead to desk rejection.

---

## 11.1 Purpose of the Results Section

The results section serves one purpose: **to answer your research questions with data.**

It should:
- Present findings in a logical sequence (usually matching your research questions or hypotheses)
- Report statistical tests, effect sizes, and confidence intervals
- Use tables and figures to supplement (not replace) the text
- Avoid interpretation — save "what it means" for the discussion

> **Pro Tip:** Think of the results section as a courtroom presentation. You are presenting evidence to the jury (your readers). The closing argument (interpretation) comes in the discussion.

---

## 11.2 Structure of the Results Section

A well-structured results section typically follows this order:

1. **Participant flow and demographics** — Who participated? Any attrition?
2. **Descriptive statistics** — Means, standard deviations, frequencies
3. **Main analyses** — Tests of your primary research questions/hypotheses
4. **Secondary/exploratory analyses** — Additional findings
5. **Assumptions and checks** — Reliability, normality, multicollinearity (if relevant)

### Reporting Participant Flow

For experimental and survey studies, begin with a clear description of your sample:

> "A total of 245 participants were recruited. After removing 12 incomplete responses and 8 multivariate outliers, the final sample consisted of 225 participants (Mage = 22.4, SD = 3.1; 62% female, 35% male, 3% non-binary)."

For studies with multiple groups or time points, consider using a **CONSORT flow diagram** (for RCTs) or a participant flow table.

## 11.3 Reporting Quantitative Results

### 11.3.1 Descriptive Statistics

Always report descriptive statistics before inferential tests. This gives readers context for understanding your findings.

**For continuous variables:**
> "Participants reported moderate levels of anxiety (M = 12.3, SD = 4.2, range = 0–21) and high levels of life satisfaction (M = 24.1, SD = 5.6, range = 0–30)."

**For categorical variables:**
> "Among the 225 participants, 138 (61.3%) reported using social media for more than 3 hours daily, while 87 (38.7%) reported 3 hours or less."

### 11.3.2 Reporting Statistical Tests

Each statistical test should include the test statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value, effect size, and confidence interval (where applicable).

**t-test:**
> "An independent-samples t-test revealed that participants in the mindfulness group (M = 8.2, SD = 3.1) reported significantly lower anxiety scores than those in the control group (M = 12.7, SD = 4.3), t(223) = 8.74, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.18, 95% CI [3.2, 5.8]."

**ANOVA:**
> "A one-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of group on anxiety scores, F(2, 222) = 14.56, p < .001, η² = .12. Post-hoc comparisons using Tukey's HSD indicated that the mindfulness group (M = 8.2) scored significantly lower than both the control group (M = 12.7, p < .001) and the exercise group (M = 10.1, p = .02)."

**Correlation:**
> "Social media use was positively correlated with anxiety (r = .34, p < .001, 95% CI [.22, .45]) and negatively correlated with self-esteem (r = −.28, p < .001, 95% CI [−.39, −.16])."

**Regression:**
> "A multiple regression analysis revealed that social media use (β = .28, p < .001), sleep quality (β = −.32, p < .001), and perceived stress (β = .25, p < .001) significantly predicted anxiety scores, R² = .41, F(3, 221) = 51.67, p < .001."

### 11.3.3 APA Style for Statistics

| Element | Format |
|---------|--------|
| Test statistic | *t*, *F*, *r*, *χ²*, *z* (italicized) |
| Degrees of freedom | In parentheses: *t*(24), *F*(2, 45) |
| p-value | *p* < .001 (not *p* < 0.001); *p* = .03 (exact if > .001) |
| Effect size | *d*, *η²*, *r²*, *Cohen's d* |
| Confidence interval | 95% CI [lower, upper] |
| Means and SDs | *M* = 12.3, *SD* = 4.2 (italicized) |

> **Pro Tip:** Always report exact p-values (unless p < .001). Saying *p* = .03 is more informative than *p* < .05. And always report effect sizes — a statistically significant result with a tiny effect size may not be meaningful.

## 11.4 Reporting Qualitative Results

Qualitative results are organized by **themes** (in thematic analysis), **categories** (in grounded theory), or **descriptions** (in phenomenology).

### Thematic Analysis Results

For each theme, provide:

1. **Theme name** — Clear, descriptive label
2. **Definition** — What the theme captures
3. **Supporting quotes** — 2–3 illustrative quotes from participants
4. **Prevalence** — How many participants expressed this theme (optional but helpful)

> **Theme 1: Digital Fatigue**
>
> Participants described a sense of exhaustion from constant digital connectivity. This theme emerged from 14 of 20 participants (70%).
>
> *"I feel like my brain never gets a break. Even when I put my phone down, I'm thinking about what I might be missing."* — Participant 7, female, age 21
>
> *"By the end of the day, I don't want to look at any screen. But then I worry I'll miss something important."* — Participant 12, male, age 23

### Presenting Qualitative Findings

- Use **pseudonyms** or participant codes, not real names
- Include **demographic information** for each quote (age, gender, relevant characteristics)
- **Italicize** direct quotes and use quotation marks
- **Bold** theme names for clarity
- **Do not over-quote** — select the most illustrative quotes, not every relevant statement

> **Pro Tip:** When presenting qualitative results, your voice as the researcher should be dominant. Quotes are evidence to support your analysis, not the analysis itself. For every theme, the majority of the text should be your interpretation, with quotes as supporting evidence.

## 11.5 Using Tables and Figures

### When to Use Tables

Tables are ideal for:
- Presenting descriptive statistics for multiple variables
- Comparing groups on multiple measures
- Showing correlation matrices
- Displaying regression results

### When to Use Figures

Figures are ideal for:
- Showing trends or patterns (line graphs)
- Comparing group means (bar charts)
- Displaying distributions (histograms, box plots)
- Illustrating relationships (scatter plots)
- Showing participant flow (CONSORT diagrams)

### APA Table Format

| Variable | n | M | SD | Min | Max |
|----------|---|---|-----|-----|-----|
| Anxiety (GAD-7) | 225 | 12.3 | 4.2 | 2 | 21 |
| Depression (PHQ-9) | 225 | 10.1 | 5.3 | 0 | 24 |
| Life Satisfaction (SWLS) | 225 | 24.1 | 5.6 | 5 | 35 |

### Rules for Tables and Figures

- **Every table/figure must be referenced in the text** ("As shown in Table 1...")
- **Do not repeat the same data** in both a table and the text — use one or the other
- **Tables and figures should be interpretable independently** — include clear titles, labels, and notes
- **Number tables and figures sequentially** (Table 1, Table 2; Figure 1, Figure 2)
- **Place tables and figures after the paragraph** where they are first mentioned (in APA 7th)

## 11.6 Common Mistakes

1. **Interpreting results** — "This suggests that social media causes anxiety" belongs in the discussion, not the results
2. **Omitting effect sizes** — Always report them alongside p-values
3. **Selective reporting** — Report all pre-registered analyses, not just the significant ones
4. **Overloading with tables** — If a table has 20 rows, consider splitting it or moving some to supplementary materials
5. **No confidence intervals** — Modern APA style encourages reporting CIs
6. **Reporting non-significant results as "no effect"** — Correct: "No significant difference was found." Incorrect: "There was no difference."
7. **Mixing results and discussion** — Keep them separate

> *Example:* **Incorrect:** "The mindfulness group had lower anxiety, which shows that mindfulness is an effective intervention." **Correct:** "The mindfulness group had significantly lower anxiety scores than the control group, t(223) = 8.74, p < .001, d = 1.18."

---

## Key Takeaways

- The results section **presents findings without interpretation** — save meaning-making for the discussion.
- Report **descriptive statistics first**, then inferential tests matching your research questions.
- Include **test statistics, degrees of freedom, p-values, effect sizes, and confidence intervals** for every test.
- Use **tables and figures** to present complex data clearly, but always reference them in the text.
- For **qualitative research**, organize by themes with supporting quotes, using pseudonyms.
- Follow **APA style** for statistics — italicize test statistics, use exact p-values, and report effect sizes.
- **Report all pre-registered analyses**, including non-significant results.
