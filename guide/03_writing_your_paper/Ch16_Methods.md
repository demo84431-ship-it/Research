# Chapter 10: Methods

## The Blueprint of Your Study

If the introduction is the argument for why your study matters, the methods section is the blueprint for how you conducted it. Its purpose is singular and absolute: **to enable replication.** Another researcher, reading your methods section alone, should be able to reproduce your study exactly—from recruiting participants to running the final analysis.

The methods section is also where reviewers evaluate the rigor of your research. A brilliant research question paired with a poorly designed study is a wasted opportunity. Reviewers scrutinize your methods to determine whether your conclusions are justified by your data. Every decision you made—whom you sampled, what measures you used, how you analyzed the data—must be defensible.

This chapter walks you through each component of a methods section, for both quantitative and qualitative studies.

---

## The Structure of a Methods Section

A standard quantitative methods section in psychology contains the following subsections:

1. **Participants** (or Sample)
2. **Research Design**
3. **Materials and Measures**
4. **Procedure**
5. **Data Analysis**
6. **Ethical Considerations** (sometimes placed at the end of Participants)

For qualitative studies, the structure is similar but adapted:

1. **Participants** (or Research Context)
2. **Research Design** (e.g., phenomenological, grounded theory)
3. **Data Collection** (interviews, focus groups, observations)
4. **Data Analysis** (thematic analysis, IPA, etc.)
5. **Reflexivity and Trustworthiness**
6. **Ethical Considerations**

---

## Participants (or Sample)

This subsection tells the reader who was in your study and how they were recruited. It answers the questions: Who? How many? How were they selected?

### What to Include

**Sample size and demographics:**
Report the total number of participants and key demographic characteristics. For quantitative studies, include means and standard deviations for continuous variables (age) and frequencies for categorical variables (gender, education level).

**Example:**
> A total of 320 undergraduate students (M age = 20.4 years, SD = 1.8; 58.1% female, 41.9% male) participated in the study. Participants were recruited from three public universities in Delhi, India. The majority were pursuing degrees in arts and humanities (45%), followed by science (32%) and commerce (23%).

**Sampling method:**
Describe how participants were recruited. Common methods include:

- **Convenience sampling:** Participants were recruited through posters, social media, or class announcements. This is the most common method in student research but limits generalizability.
- **Purposive sampling:** Participants were selected based on specific characteristics (e.g., "Only students who had experienced a depressive episode in the past year were included"). Common in qualitative research.
- **Snowball sampling:** Existing participants recruit additional participants. Useful for hard-to-reach populations.
- **Random sampling:** Every member of the population has an equal chance of selection. Ideal but rarely feasible in psychology research.
- **Stratified sampling:** The population is divided into subgroups (strata), and participants are randomly sampled from each stratum. Ensures representation.

**Inclusion and exclusion criteria:**
State explicitly who was eligible and who was not.

**Example:**
> Inclusion criteria were: (a) aged 18–25 years, (b) currently enrolled as a full-time undergraduate student, and (c) able to read and respond in English or Hindi. Students with a current diagnosis of a psychiatric disorder or those receiving psychotherapy were excluded to minimize confounding effects on emotional regulation.

**Response rate and attrition:**
If applicable, report how many people were invited versus how many participated, and how many dropped out.

**Example:**
> Of the 450 students invited, 356 responded (response rate = 79.1%). Thirty-six responses were excluded due to incomplete data, resulting in a final sample of 320.

> **Common Mistake:** Reporting only the final sample size without mentioning exclusions or dropouts. Reviewers want to know about potential selection bias. If you invited 500 people and only 100 responded, that is relevant information.

### Sample Size Justification

Increasingly, journals expect you to justify your sample size. The most common approach is a **power analysis**.

**Example:**
> An a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007) indicated that a minimum sample of 285 participants was required to detect a small-to-medium effect size (f² = .10) in a multiple regression with 5 predictors, assuming α = .05 and power = .95. Our final sample of 320 exceeded this threshold.

For qualitative studies, sample size is justified by **data saturation**:

> Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved—that is, until no new themes emerged from additional interviews. Saturation was reached after 12 interviews.

> **Pro Tip:** If you used convenience sampling and did not conduct a power analysis, acknowledge this as a limitation rather than ignoring it. Reviewers will notice either way; acknowledging it shows methodological awareness.

## Research Design

This subsection identifies the type of study you conducted. It is usually brief—often just one or two sentences—but it frames the reader's understanding of everything that follows.

**Example (quantitative):**
> A cross-sectional, correlational research design was employed to examine the relationship between emotional regulation difficulties and psychological well-being.

**Example (experimental):**
> A between-subjects randomized controlled trial was conducted to compare the effects of nature exposure versus urban exposure on cognitive performance.

**Example (qualitative):**
> This study employed a qualitative research design grounded in interpretive phenomenological analysis (IPA; Smith et al., 2009) to explore the lived experiences of emotional labor among clinical psychologists.

**Example (mixed methods):**
> A sequential explanatory mixed-methods design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018) was used. In Phase 1, a quantitative survey examined the relationship between variables. In Phase 2, semi-structured interviews were conducted to explore the mechanisms underlying the quantitative findings.

### Common Research Designs in Psychology

| Design | Purpose | Causal Inference? |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-sectional survey | Measure variables at one time point | No |
| Longitudinal survey | Measure variables over time | Limited |
| Experimental (RCT) | Manipulate IV, measure DV | Yes |
| Quasi-experimental | Compare groups without random assignment | Limited |
| Correlational | Examine relationships between variables | No |
| Case study | In-depth analysis of one or few cases | No |
| Phenomenological | Explore lived experiences | N/A |
| Grounded theory | Develop theory from data | N/A |
| Mixed methods | Combine quantitative and qualitative | Depends on design |

## Materials and Measures

This subsection describes every instrument, tool, questionnaire, or piece of equipment you used. The reader needs enough detail to find and use the same materials.

### For Questionnaires and Scales

For each measure, report:

1. **Full name and citation:** "The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004)"
2. **What it measures:** "The DERS is a 36-item self-report questionnaire that assesses difficulties across six dimensions of emotional regulation."
3. **Response format:** "Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always)."
4. **Scoring:** "Total scores range from 36 to 180, with higher scores indicating greater difficulties."
5. **Psychometric properties:** "The DERS has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .93) and good test-retest reliability (Gratz & Roemer, 2004). In the present sample, Cronbach's alpha was .89."
6. **Adaptation (if applicable):** "The scale was translated into Hindi using the back-translation method (Brislin, 1970) and piloted with 20 bilingual students to ensure conceptual equivalence."

**Example paragraph:**

> **Emotional regulation difficulties.** Emotional regulation difficulties were assessed using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004). The DERS is a 36-item self-report measure that evaluates difficulties across six dimensions: non-acceptance of emotional responses, difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior, impulse control difficulties, lack of emotional awareness, limited access to emotion regulation strategies, and lack of emotional clarity. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always), with total scores ranging from 36 to 180. Higher scores indicate greater difficulties in emotional regulation. The DERS has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including excellent internal consistency (α = .93) and good test-retest reliability over a period of 6 to 8 weeks (Gratz & Roemer, 2004). In the present sample, Cronbach's alpha for the total scale was .89, and subscale alphas ranged from .72 to .85.

> **Common Mistake:** Simply writing "Participants completed the DERS." This is insufficient. The reader needs to know what the DERS measures, how it is scored, and whether it is reliable in your sample.

### For Experimental Materials

Describe stimuli, equipment, and software in sufficient detail for replication.

**Example:**
> Stimuli were presented on a 15.6-inch laptop screen (resolution: 1920 × 1080 pixels) using E-Prime 3.0 software (Psychology Software Tools, 2016). The Stroop task consisted of 120 trials: 40 congruent (e.g., the word "RED" displayed in red font), 40 incongruent (e.g., the word "RED" displayed in blue font), and 40 neutral (e.g., the word "TABLE" displayed in colored font). Each trial began with a fixation cross (500 ms), followed by the stimulus (2000 ms maximum) and an inter-trial interval of 1000 ms. Response accuracy and reaction time were recorded for each trial.

### For Qualitative Studies

Describe your data collection instrument—typically an interview guide.

**Example:**
> Data were collected through semi-structured interviews lasting 45–70 minutes. The interview guide was developed based on the research questions and existing literature on emotional labor (Hochschild, 1983). The guide included open-ended questions such as: "Can you describe a time when you had to manage your emotions during a therapy session?" and "How does this emotional management affect your personal life?" Follow-up probes were used to elicit deeper responses. The interview guide was piloted with two clinical psychologists (not included in the final sample) and refined based on their feedback.

## Procedure

The procedure subsection is the step-by-step account of what happened during data collection. Write it in chronological order, as if you are instructing someone to replicate your study.

### What to Include

1. **How participants were recruited** (detailed version)
2. **Informed consent process**
3. **What participants did, in order**
4. **Time frame** (when data were collected)
5. **Any manipulation** (for experimental studies)
6. **Debriefing** (if applicable)

**Example (survey study):**

> Data were collected between March and May 2023. Potential participants were recruited through announcements in undergraduate classes and posters displayed in common areas at three universities in Delhi. Interested students were directed to an online survey hosted on Google Forms. The survey began with an informed consent page that explained the study's purpose, voluntary nature, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Participants who provided consent proceeded to the demographic questionnaire (5 items), followed by the DERS (36 items) and the PWBS (42 items). The order of the DERS and PWBS was counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects. Upon completion, participants received a debriefing form and were offered the opportunity to enter a draw for gift vouchers worth ₹500. The entire survey took approximately 20–25 minutes to complete.

**Example (experimental study):**

> Upon arrival at the laboratory, participants were greeted by the researcher and provided with an information sheet. After giving written informed consent, participants completed the baseline Stroop task and N-back task (pre-test). They were then randomly assigned using a computer-generated randomization sequence to either the nature walk condition (n = 30) or the urban walk condition (n = 30). Participants in the nature walk condition walked for 20 minutes along a designated route through a university botanical garden. Participants in the urban walk condition walked for 20 minutes along a busy commercial street adjacent to the university. Both groups were instructed to walk at a comfortable pace and to avoid using their phones. Upon returning to the laboratory, participants completed the Stroop task and N-back task again (post-test). Finally, participants were debriefed and compensated with course credit.

> **Pro Tip:** Write the procedure in enough detail that someone who has never met you could replicate the study. If there is any step you performed that is not in the procedure, a reviewer cannot evaluate it—and might assume you did not do it.

## Data Analysis

Describe the statistical tests or analytical procedures you used and why.

**Example (quantitative):**

> All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies) were computed for all variables. Pearson product-moment correlations were used to examine bivariate relationships between emotional regulation difficulties and psychological well-being. Hierarchical multiple regression was conducted to determine whether specific DERS dimensions predicted psychological well-being after controlling for age and gender. Assumptions of normality, linearity, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity were tested prior to regression analysis. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen's d for group comparisons and R² for regression models. Statistical significance was set at α = .05 (two-tailed).

**Example (qualitative):**

> Interview transcripts were analyzed using Interpretive Phenomenological Analysis (IPA; Smith et al., 2009). Each transcript was read multiple times to achieve familiarity. Initial notes were made on the transcript, focusing on descriptive, linguistic, and conceptual observations. Emergent themes were identified and then abstracted into superordinate themes through a process of abstraction, subsumption, polarization, and contextualization. Analysis was conducted on a case-by-case basis before patterns across cases were examined. The first author conducted the primary analysis, and the second author independently analyzed three transcripts to establish inter-coder agreement. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

## Ethical Considerations

Every study involving human participants must address ethics. Even if your institution's ethics committee approved the study, you should describe the ethical safeguards in the methods section.

**What to include:**

> The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of [University Name] (approval number: [XXX/2023]). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. They were informed that participation was voluntary, that their responses would be kept confidential, and that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were stored on a password-protected computer accessible only to the research team. Identifiable information was separated from survey responses and stored separately. Participants who reported significant distress during the study were provided with referrals to the university counseling center.

## Quantitative vs. Qualitative Methods Sections: Key Differences

| Aspect | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| **Participants** | Large sample, random/probability sampling preferred | Small sample, purposive sampling, saturation criterion |
| **Design** | Defined by variables and controls | Defined by epistemological approach (phenomenology, grounded theory, etc.) |
| **Materials** | Standardized instruments with psychometric properties | Interview guides, observation protocols |
| **Procedure** | Standardized, same for all participants | Flexible, responsive to emerging data |
| **Analysis** | Statistical tests, effect sizes, confidence intervals | Coding, thematic development, interpretation |
| **Quality criteria** | Reliability, validity, power | Trustworthiness, credibility, transferability, dependability |
| **Reflexivity** | Not typically required | Often required (researcher's positionality) |

### Reflexivity in Qualitative Research

Qualitative methods sections increasingly include a reflexivity statement—a brief description of the researcher's position, assumptions, and potential biases.

**Example:**
> The first author is a clinical psychologist with eight years of experience treating anxiety and mood disorders. Her professional background may have influenced her interpretation of participants' accounts of emotional labor. To mitigate this, she maintained a reflexive journal throughout the research process, and the second author (a counseling psychologist with a different theoretical orientation) independently coded a subset of transcripts to provide an alternative perspective.

## Annotated Example: A Complete Methods Section

Below is an annotated methods section for a hypothetical study on mindfulness and test anxiety.

---

> ## Method
>
> ### Participants
>
> ^[SAMPLE SIZE AND DEMOGRAPHICS] A total of 150 undergraduate students (M age = 21.2 years, SD = 2.1; 62% female) from a public university in Mumbai participated in the study. ^[SAMPLING METHOD] Participants were recruited through convenience sampling using announcements in psychology classes and flyers posted on campus. ^[INCLUSION/EXCLUSION CRITERIA] Students were eligible if they were (a) aged 18–25, (b) enrolled full-time, and (c) reported moderate to high test anxiety (scores ≥ 25 on the Westside Test Anxiety Scale). Students currently receiving psychological treatment or taking psychotropic medication were excluded. ^[SAMPLE SIZE JUSTIFICATION] An a priori power analysis using G*Power indicated that a minimum of 128 participants was required to detect a medium effect size (d = 0.50) in an independent-samples t-test with α = .05 and power = .80. ^[ATTRITION] Of the 187 students who completed the screening questionnaire, 150 met inclusion criteria and were enrolled. Four participants dropped out after the first session, resulting in a final sample of 146.
>
> ### Research Design
>
> ^[DESIGN TYPE] A randomized controlled trial with pre-test and post-test measures was employed. ^[RANDOMIZATION] Participants were randomly assigned to either the mindfulness intervention group (n = 73) or the waitlist control group (n = 73) using a computer-generated randomization sequence.
>
> ### Materials and Measures
>
> **Westside Test Anxiety Scale (WTAS; Driscoll, 2007).** ^[MEASURE 1] The WTAS is a 10-item self-report scale that assesses cognitive and somatic components of test anxiety. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all typical of me) to 5 (very typical of me). Scores range from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating greater test anxiety. The WTAS has demonstrated good internal consistency (α = .86; Driscoll, 2007). In the present sample, Cronbach's alpha was .84.
>
> **Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire—Short Form (FFMQ-SF; Bohlmeijer et al., 2011).** ^[MEASURE 2] The FFMQ-SF is a 15-item measure assessing five facets of mindfulness: observing, describing, acting with awareness, non-judging, and non-reactivity. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The FFMQ-SF has shown acceptable psychometric properties across diverse samples (α = .80–.87). In the present study, Cronbach's alpha was .82.
>
> ### Procedure
>
> ^[RECRUITMENT] Participants were recruited over a two-week period through announcements in undergraduate psychology and education classes. ^[SCREENING] Interested students completed the WTAS online. Those scoring ≥ 25 were invited to participate. ^[CONSENT AND BASELINE] Eligible participants attended an in-person session where they provided written informed consent and completed the baseline measures (WTAS and FFMQ-SF). ^[RANDOMIZATION AND INTERVENTION] Participants were then randomly assigned to either the mindfulness group or the waitlist control group. ^[INTERVENTION DESCRIPTION] The mindfulness group attended four weekly 60-minute group mindfulness sessions facilitated by a trained mindfulness instructor. Sessions included guided meditation, body scan exercises, and mindful breathing practices, adapted from the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program (Kabat-Zinn, 1990). Participants were also asked to practice mindfulness at home for 10 minutes daily using a guided audio recording. ^[CONTROL GROUP] The waitlist control group received no intervention during the study period but was offered the mindfulness program after data collection was completed. ^[POST-TEST] At the end of the four-week period, both groups completed the WTAS and FFMQ-SF again. ^[DEBRIEFING] All participants were debriefed and compensated with course credit.
>
> ### Data Analysis
>
> ^[SOFTWARE AND TESTS] Data were analyzed using SPSS 26. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare pre-test scores between groups to confirm randomization success. A 2 (group: mindfulness vs. control) × 2 (time: pre vs. post) mixed-design ANOVA was conducted to examine the effect of the mindfulness intervention on test anxiety. Effect sizes were reported as partial η² and Cohen's d. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni correction. ^[ASSUMPTIONS] Assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variance, and sphericity were tested. ^[SIGNIFICANCE LEVEL] The significance level was set at α = .05.
>
> ### Ethical Considerations
>
> ^[APPROVAL] The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of [University Name]. ^[CONSENT AND SAFETY] All participants provided written informed consent. They were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Participants in the waitlist control group were offered the intervention after the study to ensure equitable access to treatment.

## APA Formatting for the Methods Section

### Heading Levels

- **Method** — Level 1 heading (centered, bold)
- **Participants** — Level 2 heading (flush left, bold)
- **Materials and Measures** — Level 2 heading
- **Procedure** — Level 2 heading
- **Data Analysis** — Level 2 heading

### Tense

- **Past tense** for completed actions: "Participants completed…" "Data were analyzed…"
- **Present tense** for established properties of instruments: "The DERS assesses…"

### Voice

APA 7th edition permits first person: "We recruited 320 participants" or "The researcher recruited 320 participants." Use consistently—do not switch between first and third person within a section.

### Numbers

- Use numerals for all numbers that represent statistical data: "320 participants," "M = 20.4"
- Spell out numbers below 10 that do not represent statistical data: "three universities" (but "3 scales")

## Common Mistakes in the Methods Section

### Mistake 1: Insufficient Detail for Replication

"The DERS was administered." What is the DERS? How many items? What does it measure? What is the response format? Always describe your measures fully.

### Mistake 2: Omitting Reliability for Your Sample

The original authors reported α = .93, but what was the reliability in your sample? Report Cronbach's alpha (or McDonald's omega) for your data.

### Mistake 3: Not Justifying Sample Size

"We recruited 100 participants." Why 100? Is that enough to detect the effects you are looking for? Conduct and report a power analysis.

### Mistake 4: Mixing Past and Present Tense

"Participants complete the survey and were then debriefed." Pick a tense and stick with it.

### Mistake 5: Failing to Report Exclusions

You started with 500 surveys but only analyzed 320. What happened to the other 180? Report exclusions and the reasons for them.

### Mistake 6: Vague Procedure

"Participants were given the questionnaires and asked to fill them out." Where? In a classroom? Online? How long did it take? Were they alone or in groups? Details matter.

### Mistake 7: Forgetting Counterbalancing or Randomization

If you presented measures in a fixed order, you may have introduced order effects. If you did counterbalance, say so. If you randomized, describe the method.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. The methods section must enable replication. Another researcher should be able to reproduce your study from this section alone.
2. Describe participants fully: sample size, demographics, sampling method, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and response rate.
3. Justify your sample size with a power analysis (quantitative) or saturation criterion (qualitative).
4. Describe every measure in detail: what it measures, how it is scored, its psychometric properties, and its reliability in your sample.
5. Write the procedure in chronological order, step by step.
6. Report all statistical analyses, including assumptions testing and effect sizes.
7. Always include ethical approval and consent information.
8. For qualitative studies, include reflexivity and trustworthiness criteria.
9. Use past tense, active voice, and APA formatting consistently.

### Exercise 1: Methods Section Critique
Find a published paper in your area and read only the methods section. Can you replicate the study from this information alone? What is missing? What is well-described? Write a brief critique.

### Exercise 2: Measure Description
Choose a psychological measure you have used (e.g., PHQ-9, Big Five Inventory, Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale). Write a full methods-style paragraph describing it, including: full name, citation, what it measures, number of items, response format, scoring, psychometric properties, and reliability in a hypothetical sample.

### Exercise 3: Procedure Writing
Write a detailed procedure section for one of the following hypothetical studies:
- An online survey on social media use and loneliness
- A lab experiment testing whether chewing gum improves memory
- A qualitative interview study on experiences of burnout among nurses

### Exercise 4: Power Analysis
Using G*Power (free software), conduct a power analysis for a study with the following parameters:
- Test: Independent-samples t-test
- Effect size: d = 0.50 (medium)
- Alpha: .05
- Power: .80
Report the required sample size. Now repeat with power = .90. How does the required sample size change?
