# Chapter 09: The Introduction

## Setting the Stage for Your Research

The introduction is the most intellectually demanding section of a research paper to write. It is also, paradoxically, the section that looks easiest when reading a finished paper. A great introduction feels inevitable—as if the research question could not have been anything else, as if the study simply had to be done. Achieving that sense of inevitability requires careful architecture.

Your introduction must accomplish several things simultaneously: establish the topic's importance, review relevant literature, identify a gap, state your purpose, and present your hypothesis—all while maintaining a logical flow that carries the reader from broad context to your specific study. It is, in essence, an argument: "Here is what we know. Here is what we do not know. Here is why it matters. Here is what I did about it."

This chapter teaches you how to build that argument, paragraph by paragraph.

---

## The Funnel Structure

The most effective introductions in psychology follow what is called the **funnel structure**—moving from broad to specific, like a funnel narrowing from wide opening to narrow spout.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│         BROAD TOPIC / CONTEXT           │
│    (The big picture: why this matters)   │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│       SPECIFIC TOPIC / BACKGROUND       │
│  (What research has shown so far)        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│          THE GAP / PROBLEM              │
│  (What we still don't know)             │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│     PURPOSE / RESEARCH QUESTION         │
│  (What this study does about it)        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│         HYPOTHESIS (if applicable)       │
│  (What you expect to find)              │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

This structure works because it mirrors how humans process new information: from the familiar to the unfamiliar. You start with what the reader already knows, then guide them step by step to what they do not know—your study.

## Component 1: The Opening Paragraph — Establishing Context

The first paragraph of your introduction is prime real estate. It must accomplish two things: establish the topic and convince the reader it matters.

### Strategies for Opening Paragraphs

**Strategy 1: Start with a broad statement about the topic's importance.**

> Emotional regulation—the ability to monitor, evaluate, and modulate emotional reactions—is increasingly recognized as a central determinant of psychological well-being (Gross, 2015). Deficits in emotional regulation have been implicated in a wide range of mental health conditions, including depression, anxiety, and borderline personality disorder (Aldao et al., 2010).

This opening establishes the topic (emotional regulation), its importance (determinant of well-being), and its clinical relevance (implicated in mental health conditions), all in two sentences.

**Strategy 2: Start with a striking statistic or fact.**

> According to the World Health Organization (2022), depression affects approximately 280 million people worldwide, making it one of the leading causes of disability. Despite the availability of pharmacological and psychotherapeutic treatments, a significant proportion of patients do not respond to first-line interventions, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of underlying cognitive mechanisms.

This approach grabs attention immediately and creates urgency.

**Strategy 3: Start with a real-world scenario or problem.**

> Each September, millions of young adults leave home for the first time to begin university. For many, this transition brings excitement and opportunity. For a significant minority, however, it triggers a cascade of psychological difficulties, including anxiety, depression, and loneliness (Cleary et al., 2011). Understanding the factors that predict successful adjustment to university life has important implications for student mental health services.

This approach is engaging and relatable, particularly for applied research topics.

**Strategy 4: Start with a historical or theoretical foundation.**

> Since Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) seminal work on prospect theory, researchers have increasingly recognized that human decision-making is not the rational process classical economics assumed. One of the most robust findings in this literature is the negativity bias—the tendency for negative information to exert a disproportionately strong influence on cognition and behavior (Baumeister et al., 2001).

This approach grounds the reader in the theoretical tradition and positions your work within it.

> **Common Mistake:** Starting too broadly. "Since the beginning of human history, people have experienced stress." This tells the reader nothing useful. Start at the level of your specific topic, not the history of humanity.

> **Pro Tip:** Write your introduction's first paragraph last among all the introduction's paragraphs. Write the specific-to-gap section first, then figure out how broad your opening needs to be.

## Component 2: Background — The Literature Review Within the Introduction

After the opening, you need to review what is already known about your topic. This is not a full literature review (that belongs in a standalone review paper); it is a curated, purposeful selection of research that directly supports your study's rationale.

### Principles for the Background Section

**Principle 1: Be selective, not exhaustive.**
You do not need to cite every study ever published on your topic. Cite the most relevant, most recent, and most authoritative studies. If a meta-analysis exists, cite it rather than the 30 individual studies it summarizes.

**Principle 2: Organize thematically, not chronologically.**
Do not write: "In 2005, Smith found X. In 2008, Jones found Y. In 2012, Patel found Z." This reads like a timeline, not an argument. Instead, organize by theme: "Several studies have examined X (Smith, 2005; Jones, 2008). However, fewer have investigated Y (Patel, 2012)."

**Principle 3: Build toward the gap.**
Every paragraph in the background should bring the reader closer to understanding why your study is needed. If a piece of literature does not contribute to this argument, cut it.

**Principle 4: Use topic sentences that tell a story.**
Each paragraph's first sentence should signal what that paragraph is about and how it connects to the overall narrative.

**Example of thematic organization:**

> **Paragraph 2:** Research has consistently shown that emotional regulation difficulties are associated with poor mental health outcomes (Aldao et al., 2010; Gratz & Roemer, 2004). Specifically, difficulties in impulse control and limited access to regulation strategies have been identified as the strongest predictors of psychopathology.
>
> **Paragraph 3:** Despite this robust evidence, most studies have been conducted in Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) societies (Henrich et al., 2010). The extent to which these findings generalize to collectivist cultures, where emotional expression norms differ substantially, remains unclear.
>
> **Paragraph 4:** A small number of studies have examined emotional regulation in South Asian populations. Chopra et al. (2019) found that Indian adults reported higher levels of suppression compared to their American counterparts, while Sharma and Kumra (2022) reported that emotional regulation mediated the relationship between parenting style and adolescent depression in an Indian sample. However, neither study examined the relationship between emotional regulation and psychological well-being directly.

Notice how each paragraph narrows the focus: from the broad evidence base, to the cultural gap, to the specific studies that come closest to addressing it. The gap is emerging naturally.

## Component 3: The Gap Statement — Your Paper's Reason for Existing

The gap statement is the pivot point of your introduction. Everything before it builds up to it; everything after it flows from it. A weak gap statement undermines the entire paper.

### Types of Gaps

1. **No one has studied this at all.** (Rare, but possible in emerging fields like AI×Psychology.)
2. **No one has studied this in this population.** (Common: "Most research has been conducted in Western samples…")
3. **No one has used this methodology.** (Common: "Previous studies have relied on cross-sectional designs…")
4. **Existing findings are contradictory.** (Common: "While some studies report X, others report Y…")
5. **Previous research has a specific limitation.** (Common: "These studies did not control for Z…")
6. **A new theoretical framework offers a different perspective.** (Common: "From the perspective of Self-Determination Theory…")

### How to Write a Gap Statement

**Template 1 (Population gap):**
> "However, few studies have examined [topic] in [population]. Given that [reason why this population matters], further research is needed."

**Template 2 (Methodological gap):**
> "However, previous research has primarily relied on [limitation]. To address this, the present study employed [better method]."

**Template 3 (Contradictory findings):**
> "The evidence regarding [topic] remains mixed. While [Study A] found [X], [Study B] reported [Y]. These inconsistencies may be due to [possible explanation], highlighting the need for further investigation."

**Template 4 (New context):**
> "To date, no study has examined [topic] in the context of [new context]. This is an important omission because [reason]."

### Example Gap Statement

> Despite the growing body of research on emotional regulation and well-being in Western populations, relatively little is known about this relationship in South Asian contexts, where cultural norms around emotional expression may shape both the experience and consequences of regulatory strategies (Matsumoto et al., 2008). Furthermore, existing Indian studies have focused predominantly on clinical populations, leaving the relationship between emotional regulation and positive psychological outcomes in non-clinical samples largely unexplored.

This gap statement identifies two specific gaps: a cultural gap (South Asian context) and a population gap (non-clinical samples). Both are justified with brief reasoning.

> **Common Mistake:** Claiming "no one has ever studied this" when they have. A quick literature search will reveal whether your gap is real. Overstating the gap damages your credibility with reviewers who know the literature.

> **Pro Tip:** The gap should feel like a natural conclusion from the literature you just reviewed, not an abrupt shift. If the reader is surprised by the gap, you have not built up to it properly.

---

## Component 4: Purpose Statement — What This Study Does

The purpose statement is your thesis. It tells the reader exactly what your study aims to do. It should appear near the end of the introduction, typically in the second-to-last or last paragraph.

### Effective Purpose Statement Templates

**For quantitative studies:**
> "The purpose of this study was to examine the relationship between [IV] and [DV] among [population]."

> "This study aimed to investigate whether [IV] predicts [DV] after controlling for [covariates]."

> "The present study tested the hypothesis that [specific hypothesis]."

**For qualitative studies:**
> "This study explored the lived experiences of [phenomenon] among [population] using [analytical approach]."

> "The aim of this study was to understand how [population] experience and make meaning of [phenomenon]."

**For mixed methods:**
> "Using a sequential explanatory design, this study first examined [quantitative aim] and then explored [qualitative aim] to provide a deeper understanding of [topic]."

### Example Purpose Statement

> The present study aimed to examine the relationship between emotional regulation difficulties and psychological well-being among college students in India. Specifically, we hypothesized that (a) emotional regulation difficulties would be negatively associated with psychological well-being, and (b) specific dimensions of emotional regulation—particularly difficulties in goal-directed behavior and lack of emotional awareness—would be the strongest predictors of reduced well-being.

This purpose statement does several things well: it specifies the variables (emotional regulation difficulties, psychological well-being), the population (Indian college students), and the hypotheses (directional predictions with specific dimensions identified).

---

## Component 5: Hypotheses — Your Predictions

Hypotheses are not required for all studies (exploratory and qualitative studies often do not have them), but when applicable, they should be stated clearly and specifically.

### Characteristics of Good Hypotheses

1. **Specific:** "Participants in the nature walk condition will score higher on the Stroop task than participants in the urban walk condition" is better than "Nature will have a positive effect on cognition."

2. **Testable:** The hypothesis must be something your data can actually evaluate. "Emotional regulation is important" is not testable. "Emotional regulation difficulties will predict lower well-being scores" is.

3. **Grounded in theory or prior research:** Hypotheses should not be guesses. They should follow logically from the literature you reviewed. Reference the studies that support your prediction.

4. **Directional when possible:** "Group A will score higher than Group B" is stronger than "There will be a difference between groups"—unless you genuinely have no basis for predicting the direction.

### Example Hypothesis Section

> Based on the literature reviewed above, we formulated the following hypotheses:
>
> H1: Emotional regulation difficulties will be negatively correlated with psychological well-being among Indian college students.
>
> H2: Among the six dimensions of emotional regulation difficulties, difficulties in goal-directed behavior and lack of emotional awareness will be the strongest negative predictors of psychological well-being.
>
> H3: The relationship between emotional regulation difficulties and psychological well-being will be stronger for female students than for male students, consistent with research suggesting gender differences in emotional processing (McRae et al., 2008).

Each hypothesis is numbered, specific, directional, and grounded in prior research.

## Annotated Example: A Complete Introduction

Below is an annotated introduction adapted from a published study. Each component is labeled.

---

> ^[OPENING — CONTEXT] Emotional regulation—the ability to monitor, evaluate, and modulate emotional reactions—has emerged as a central construct in psychological research over the past two decades (Gross, 2015). Theoretical models, particularly Gross's (1998) process model of emotion regulation, have provided a framework for understanding how individuals manage their emotional experiences, and a large body of empirical work has linked regulatory strategies to psychological health and illness.
>
> ^[BACKGROUND — WHAT WE KNOW] Research has consistently demonstrated that deficits in emotional regulation are associated with a wide range of psychopathological outcomes. A landmark meta-analysis by Aldao et al. (2010) found that maladaptive regulation strategies—such as rumination, suppression, and avoidance—were positively associated with symptoms of depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and substance use across 114 studies. Conversely, adaptive strategies such as cognitive reappraisal and problem-solving have been linked to greater psychological well-being and resilience (Haga et al., 2009; Troy et al., 2016).
>
> ^[BACKGROUND — NARROWING FOCUS] Within this literature, the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004) has become one of the most widely used measures of emotion regulation deficits. The DERS assesses six dimensions: non-acceptance of emotional responses, difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior, impulse control difficulties, lack of emotional awareness, limited access to emotion regulation strategies, and lack of emotional clarity. Studies using the DERS have shown that different dimensions may be differentially related to mental health outcomes, suggesting that emotional regulation is not a unitary construct (Hallion et al., 2018).
>
> ^[BACKGROUND — CULTURAL CONSIDERATIONS] However, the vast majority of this research has been conducted in Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) societies (Henrich et al., 2010). This is a significant limitation because emotional expression and regulation are shaped by cultural norms and values (Matsumoto et al., 2008). In collectivist cultures such as India, emotional suppression may serve social functions—maintaining harmony and avoiding conflict—that are less valued in individualist Western contexts (Butler et al., 2007). Consequently, the relationship between emotional regulation and psychological well-being observed in Western samples may not directly generalize to South Asian populations.
>
> ^[BACKGROUND — INDIAN RESEARCH] A small but growing number of studies have examined emotional regulation in Indian samples. Chopra et al. (2019) found that Indian adults reported higher levels of expressive suppression than American adults, and Sharma and Kumra (2022) demonstrated that emotional regulation mediated the relationship between parenting style and adolescent depression. However, these studies focused primarily on negative outcomes (depression, anxiety) rather than positive psychological constructs such as well-being. Furthermore, neither study examined which specific dimensions of emotional regulation were most predictive of psychological outcomes in Indian populations.
>
> ^[GAP STATEMENT] In summary, while the link between emotional regulation and psychological well-being is well-established in Western contexts, three gaps remain: (a) the relationship has not been examined in Indian non-clinical populations, (b) the differential predictive power of specific DERS dimensions has not been explored in South Asian samples, and (c) positive psychological outcomes (well-being) have received less attention than psychopathological outcomes.
>
> ^[PURPOSE STATEMENT] The present study aimed to address these gaps by examining the relationship between emotional regulation difficulties (as measured by the DERS) and psychological well-being among college students in India. We hypothesized that (H1) emotional regulation difficulties would be negatively associated with psychological well-being, and (H2) difficulties in goal-directed behavior and lack of emotional awareness would be the strongest predictors of reduced well-being, consistent with findings from Western samples (Hallion et al., 2018) but tested here for the first time in an Indian context.

---

**Analysis of this introduction:**

| Component | Paragraphs | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Opening/Context | 1 | Establishes the topic and its importance |
| Background (broad) | 2 | Reviews the main evidence base |
| Background (narrowing) | 3 | Focuses on the specific measure and its dimensions |
| Background (cultural) | 4 | Introduces the cultural limitation |
| Background (Indian) | 5 | Reviews the closest existing studies |
| Gap Statement | 6 | Identifies three specific gaps |
| Purpose + Hypotheses | 7 | States what this study does and predicts |

Notice how the funnel narrows with each paragraph. The reader is never asked to make a sudden leap from one topic to another. Each paragraph builds on the previous one.

## Common Introduction Mistakes and Fixes

### Mistake 1: The "Data Dump" Introduction

**Problem:** Citing every study you have ever read on the topic without synthesis or argument. The introduction reads like a list: "Smith (2010) found X. Jones (2012) found Y. Patel (2014) found Z."

**Fix:** Organize by themes, not by individual studies. Synthesize findings: "Several studies have demonstrated X (Smith, 2010; Jones, 2012), while others have reported Y (Patel, 2014)."

### Mistake 2: The Missing Gap

**Problem:** The introduction reviews literature competently but never explicitly states what is missing. The reader finishes the introduction thinking, "So what? Why does this study exist?"

**Fix:** Write an explicit gap statement. Use signal phrases: "However, no study has examined…" or "A significant gap in the literature is…"

### Mistake 3: The Surprise Hypothesis

**Problem:** The hypothesis appears without any prior discussion supporting it. The reader thinks, "Where did that come from?"

**Fix:** Every hypothesis should be the logical conclusion of the literature you reviewed. If your hypothesis is that emotional regulation predicts well-being, you need to have discussed prior research establishing that link.

### Mistake 4: Too Long or Too Short

**Problem:** Some introductions are 3 pages; others are 15 pages. Both extremes are problematic. Too short means insufficient context; too long means you are writing a review paper, not an introduction.

**Fix:** For a standard empirical paper, aim for 4–7 pages (double-spaced), or approximately 1,000–2,000 words. For a thesis or dissertation, introductions can be longer. Always check your target journal's typical length.

### Mistake 5: Presenting Results in the Introduction

**Problem:** "Our results showed that emotional regulation predicted well-being (β = .45)." No. The introduction provides the rationale; the results section reports findings.

**Fix:** Reserve all results for the Results section. The introduction should discuss only prior research.

### Mistake 6: Weak or Absent Purpose Statement

**Problem:** The reader finishes the introduction unclear about what the study actually does.

**Fix:** Write a clear, explicit purpose statement: "The purpose of this study was to…" or "This study aimed to…"

### Mistake 7: Citing Outdated Sources Exclusively

**Problem:** Every citation is from before 2010. The field has moved on, and your literature review appears stale.

**Fix:** Include recent studies (last 5–10 years). Seminal older works are fine to cite, but the bulk of your references should be current.

## Tense Usage in the Introduction

- **Present tense** for established knowledge: "Emotional regulation is associated with well-being."
- **Past tense** for specific prior studies: "Smith (2020) found that…" or "In a sample of Indian adults, Chopra et al. (2019) reported…"
- **Present tense** for the current study's purpose: "This study examines…" or "The present study aims to…"

> **Pro Tip:** If you are unsure whether something is "established knowledge" (present tense) or a "specific finding" (past tense), ask: "Would I cite this as a single study, or as a general fact supported by many studies?" Single study = past tense. General fact = present tense.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. The introduction follows the funnel structure: broad context → specific background → gap → purpose → hypothesis.
2. The opening paragraph must establish the topic and its importance. Avoid starting too broadly.
3. Organize the literature review thematically, not chronologically. Build toward the gap.
4. The gap statement is the pivot point. Make it explicit, specific, and justified.
5. The purpose statement must clearly state what your study does and, if applicable, what you hypothesize.
6. Every hypothesis must be grounded in prior research or theory.
7. Aim for 1,000–2,000 words for a standard empirical paper introduction.
8. Write the introduction's body first, then the opening paragraph, then the purpose statement. Do not write it in order.

## Practice Exercises

### Exercise 1: Reverse Outline
Find a published paper in your area. Read only the introduction. Create a reverse outline: for each paragraph, write one sentence summarizing its purpose and label it with the appropriate component (context, background, gap, purpose, hypothesis). Does the funnel structure hold?

### Exercise 2: Gap Identification
Read five recent papers in your subfield. For each, identify the gap statement. What type of gap is it (population, methodological, contradictory findings, etc.)? How many sentences does the author use to state the gap?

### Exercise 3: Introduction Writing
Write a complete introduction (4–6 paragraphs) for one of the following hypothetical studies:
- A study on the relationship between procrastination and self-compassion among graduate students
- A study testing whether a brief gratitude intervention improves sleep quality
- A qualitative study exploring how therapists experience the transition to online therapy

Use the funnel structure. Label each paragraph with its component.

### Exercise 4: First Paragraph Workshop
Write three different opening paragraphs for a study on "social media use and body image in adolescents." Use three different strategies (broad statement, striking statistic, real-world scenario). Which works best for your intended audience?

### Exercise 5: Hypothesis Grounding
For each hypothesis below, write the supporting literature that would justify it (you may use real or hypothetical citations):
- H1: Mindfulness training will reduce test anxiety in college students.
- H2: Attachment style will moderate the relationship between stress and depression.
- H3: First-generation college students will report higher levels of imposter syndrome than continuing-generation students.
