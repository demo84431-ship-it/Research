# Chapter 3: Research Design

## Opening

If your research question is the destination, then your research design is the route you take to get there. It is arguably the most consequential decision you will make in the entire research process, because it determines what kind of data you collect, what analyses you can run, and — critically — what conclusions you can draw. A brilliant question paired with a flawed design produces unreliable answers. A modest question paired with a rigorous design can yield powerful, trustworthy findings. This chapter will walk you through every major type of research design used in psychology, help you understand the strengths and limitations of each, and provide a decision framework so you can choose the right design for your own work.

---

## 3.1 The Three Pillars: Experimental, Correlational, and Descriptive

At the broadest level, all research designs in psychology fall into three categories based on what they allow you to **do** with variables.

### 3.1.1 Experimental Designs

In an experimental design, the researcher **manipulates** one variable (the independent variable, IV) and **measures** its effect on another variable (the dependent variable, DV), while **controlling** for extraneous factors. This is the only design that allows you to draw **causal conclusions**.

The hallmarks of a true experiment are:
1. **Manipulation** — You actively change the IV (e.g., giving one group a drug and another a placebo).
2. **Control** — You hold other variables constant or randomize them away.
3. **Random assignment** — Participants are randomly assigned to conditions, ensuring that pre-existing differences between groups are distributed evenly.

> *Real example:* In the classic study by Darley and Batson (1973), seminary students were randomly assigned to conditions where they were either told they were late or on time for a presentation. They then encountered a person slumped in a doorway (a confederate). The IV was time pressure; the DV was whether they stopped to help. This was a true experiment because the researchers manipulated the IV and randomly assigned participants.

**When to use:** When you want to establish cause-and-effect relationships. When it is ethical and practical to manipulate a variable.

**Limitations:** Experiments often lack ecological validity — the controlled laboratory setting may not reflect real-world behavior. They can also be expensive and time-consuming.

### 3.1.2 Correlational Designs

In a correlational design, you **measure** two or more variables and examine the **relationship** between them without manipulating anything. You can determine whether variables co-vary (move together), but you **cannot** determine causation.

> *Real example:* Twenge, Joiner, Rogers, and Martin (2018) analyzed data from over 500,000 adolescents and found that increased screen time was correlated with higher rates of depression and suicidality. But they could not conclude that screen time *caused* depression — it is equally possible that depressed adolescents spend more time on screens.

**Types of correlation:**
- **Positive correlation** — As one variable increases, the other increases (e.g., study hours and exam scores).
- **Negative correlation** — As one variable increases, the other decreases (e.g., anxiety and sleep quality).
- **Zero correlation** — No systematic relationship (e.g., shoe size and intelligence).

**When to use:** When manipulation is impossible or unethical (e.g., you cannot randomly assign people to smoke or not smoke). When exploring relationships between naturally occurring variables.

**Limitations:** The **third variable problem** — an unmeasured variable may explain the observed correlation. The **directionality problem** — even if A is correlated with B, you do not know whether A causes B, B causes A, or something else causes both.

> **Pro Tip:** A correlation of r = 0.30 is statistically significant with a large sample, but it means the two variables share only 9% of their variance (r² = 0.09). Always compute and report r² to convey the practical significance of a correlation.

### 3.1.3 Descriptive Designs

Descriptive designs aim to **describe** the characteristics of a phenomenon, population, or group. They do not test hypotheses about relationships or causation — they simply document *what is*.

Common descriptive designs include:
- **Case studies** — In-depth analysis of a single individual or small group
- **Surveys** — Collecting data from a sample using questionnaires
- **Naturalistic observation** — Observing behavior in its natural setting without intervention
- **Normative studies** — Establishing norms for a measure (e.g., "average IQ scores by age group in India")

> *Real example:* The famous case of Phineas Gage — a railroad worker who survived an iron rod passing through his frontal lobe — is a descriptive case study. Harlow (1868) documented the changes in Gage's personality and behavior. No variables were manipulated; the case was described in detail, and it profoundly influenced our understanding of the frontal lobes and personality.

> *Real example:* The National Mental Health Survey of India (Murthy, 2016) used a descriptive design to estimate the prevalence of mental disorders across 12 Indian states. The goal was not to test causes but to document the scope of the problem.

## 3.2 Qualitative, Quantitative, and Mixed Methods Designs

This distinction (introduced in Chapter 1) deserves a deeper treatment here, because the design implications are profound.

### 3.2.1 Quantitative Designs

Quantitative designs involve numerical data and statistical analysis. They are rooted in the positivist/post-positivist tradition and aim for generalizability.

Key features:
- Large sample sizes (often 100+ participants)
- Standardized measures (questionnaires, reaction time tasks, physiological measures)
- Statistical hypothesis testing
- Emphasis on reliability and validity
- Results expressed as numbers, p-values, and effect sizes

> *Example:* A study measuring the correlation between Big Five personality traits and academic performance in 500 Indian university students using the NEO-FFI and GPA data.

### 3.2.2 Qualitative Designs

Qualitative designs involve non-numerical data — words, images, experiences. They are rooted in constructivist and interpretivist traditions and aim for depth and understanding.

Key features:
- Small sample sizes (often 5–30 participants)
- In-depth data collection (interviews, observations, documents)
- Thematic analysis, grounded theory, IPA, or discourse analysis
- Emphasis on trustworthiness (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability)
- Results expressed as themes, narratives, or theoretical models

> *Example:* A phenomenological study exploring the lived experience of first-generation college students navigating academic pressure, using semi-structured interviews with 12 participants analyzed through IPA (Smith et al., 2009).

**Common misconception:** Qualitative research is not "easier" or "less rigorous" than quantitative research. It has its own standards of quality, and meeting them requires skill, reflexivity, and discipline.

### 3.2.3 Mixed Methods Designs

Mixed methods designs integrate quantitative and qualitative components within a single study or program of research. The rationale is **complementarity** — each method addresses a different aspect of the research question.

Major mixed methods designs:

| Design | Description | When to Use |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| **Convergent Parallel** | Collect QUAN + QUAL simultaneously, merge results | When you want to compare findings from both approaches |
| **Explanatory Sequential** | QUAN → QUAL (quantitative first, then qualitative to explain) | When you have quantitative results that need deeper explanation |
| **Exploratory Sequential** | QUAL → QUAN (qualitative first, then quantitative to test) | When exploring a new topic before measuring it |
| **Embedded** | QUAL or QUAN embedded within a larger study of the other type | When one method supports the other |
| **Transformative** | Uses a theoretical lens (e.g., feminist, critical race) to frame the entire study | When social justice or equity is central |

> *Example:* You conduct a randomized controlled trial showing that a new CBT-based app reduces anxiety (QUAN). Then you interview 15 participants about their experience with the app (QUAL). This is an explanatory sequential design — the qualitative data helps explain *why* the quantitative intervention worked (or did not work for some participants).

> **Pro Tip:** In a mixed methods paper, you need to explain *why* you are using both approaches and *how* you are integrating them. Simply collecting both types of data and reporting them separately is not true mixed methods — the integration is the key.

## 3.3 Cross-Sectional vs. Longitudinal Designs

This distinction concerns **when** data is collected.

### 3.3.1 Cross-Sectional Designs

In a cross-sectional design, data is collected at a **single point in time**. You measure your variables once and analyze the relationships.

> *Example:* A survey of 1,000 adults aged 18–65 measuring their levels of loneliness and social media use. Data is collected once, and you compare loneliness scores across age groups.

**Advantages:** Quick, cheap, no attrition (dropout) problems.

**Limitations:** Cannot establish causation or temporal sequence. The age differences you observe might reflect **cohort effects** (different generations grew up differently) rather than actual developmental changes.

### 3.3.2 Longitudinal Designs

In a longitudinal design, data is collected from the **same participants over time** — months, years, or even decades.

> *Real example:* The Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study has followed over 1,000 people born in Dunedin, New Zealand, in 1972–1973. Data has been collected at ages 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 18, 21, 26, 32, 38, and beyond. This study has produced groundbreaking findings on the developmental origins of mental health, personality, and physical health (Caspi et al., 2003).

**Advantages:** Can establish temporal sequence (A happened before B). Can track change over time. Stronger evidence for causation than cross-sectional designs.

**Limitations:** Expensive, time-consuming, and subject to **attrition** (participants drop out over time, potentially biasing results). Also subject to **practice effects** (participants get better at tests simply by taking them repeatedly).

> **Pro Tip:** If you are planning a longitudinal study for your thesis or dissertation, be realistic about time constraints. A 6-month follow-up is feasible; a 10-year follow-up is not. Consider accelerated longitudinal designs (studying different age groups simultaneously) as a compromise.

## 3.4 Between-Subjects vs. Within-Subjects Designs

When conducting experiments, you must decide how participants experience the conditions of your study.

### 3.4.1 Between-Subjects Design

Each participant experiences **only one condition**. Different groups of participants are compared.

> *Example:* Group A receives the mindfulness intervention; Group B receives no intervention. You compare anxiety levels between the two groups.

**Advantages:** No carryover effects (being in one condition does not affect performance in another). Simpler to administer.

**Limitations:** Requires more participants (you need enough people in each group). Individual differences between groups can add noise (though random assignment helps).

### 3.4.2 Within-Subjects Design

Each participant experiences **all conditions**. The same people are compared across conditions.

> *Example:* Each participant completes a memory test under three conditions: silence, white noise, and music. You compare each person's performance across all three conditions.

**Advantages:** More statistically powerful (individual differences are controlled because each person serves as their own control). Requires fewer participants.

**Limitations:** **Order effects** — the order in which conditions are experienced may affect results. **Carryover effects** — fatigue, practice, or adaptation from one condition may carry over to the next.

**Solution:** Use **counterbalancing** — vary the order of conditions across participants (e.g., half do silence→noise→music, the other half do music→noise→silence).

### 3.4.3 Mixed Design

Some studies combine both: one factor is between-subjects and another is within-subjects.

> *Example:* You compare two groups (between-subjects: mindfulness vs. control) across three time points (within-subjects: pre, post, follow-up). This is a 2 × 3 mixed factorial design.

> **Pro Tip:** When reporting factorial designs, use the standard notation: "a 2 (group: mindfulness vs. control) × 3 (time: pre vs. post vs. follow-up) mixed ANOVA." This tells the reader exactly what your design is in a single sentence.

## 3.5 Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)

The **randomized controlled trial** is the gold standard for establishing causal effects, particularly in clinical and health psychology.

An RCT has these features:
1. **Randomization** — Participants are randomly assigned to conditions.
2. **Control group** — A comparison group that receives no treatment, a placebo, or standard care.
3. **Blinding** — Participants (single-blind) or both participants and experimenters (double-blind) do not know which condition a participant is in.
4. **Pre-specified outcomes** — Primary and secondary outcomes are defined before data collection.

> *Real example:* The landmark study by Kessler et al. (2005) used an RCT design to compare the Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR*D) — testing multiple antidepressant treatments in a sequential, randomized fashion to determine the most effective approach for treatment-resistant depression.

**Consort guidelines:** When reporting an RCT, follow the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) statement. This includes a flow diagram showing participant enrollment, allocation, follow-up, and analysis.

**When to use RCTs:** When you want to test the efficacy of an intervention (therapy, drug, educational program). When ethical randomization is possible.

**Limitations:** Expensive, time-consuming, and not always ethical (you cannot randomly assign people to traumatic experiences). Also, RCTs may lack ecological validity — the controlled conditions of a trial may not reflect real-world practice.

> **Pro Tip:** If you are writing a protocol for an RCT, register it on a trial registry like the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI) or ClinicalTrials.gov before data collection begins. Pre-registration enhances credibility and prevents HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known).

## 3.6 Quasi-Experimental Designs

Quasi-experimental designs are similar to experiments but **lack full randomization**. You compare groups, but participants are not randomly assigned to conditions — they are naturally occurring groups.

> *Example:* Comparing academic performance between students who voluntarily attended a study skills workshop and those who did not. You did not randomly assign students — they chose to attend or not. This is quasi-experimental.

**Common quasi-experimental designs:**
- **Non-equivalent control group design** — Two pre-existing groups are compared, with pre-test measures to assess baseline differences.
- **Interrupted time series** — Data is collected at multiple time points before and after an intervention.
- **Regression discontinuity** — Participants just above and just below a cutoff score are compared.

**When to use:** When randomization is impractical or unethical. Much educational and organizational research is quasi-experimental because you cannot randomly assign students to schools or employees to companies.

**Limitations:** **Selection bias** is the biggest threat. The groups may differ in ways other than the treatment, making causal claims weaker.

> **Pro Tip:** In a quasi-experimental design, use **propensity score matching** or **ANCOVA** to statistically control for pre-existing differences between groups. This does not fully replace randomization, but it strengthens your conclusions.

## 3.7 Single-Case Experimental Designs

Single-case designs (formerly called single-subject designs) study one participant (or a small number of participants) intensively, using repeated measurements over time. They are common in clinical psychology, behavioral analysis, and neuropsychology.

**Common single-case designs:**

- **A-B design** — Baseline (A) → Intervention (B). Simple but cannot rule out other explanations.
- **A-B-A design** — Baseline → Intervention → Withdrawal. Stronger because behavior should return to baseline when the intervention is removed.
- **A-B-A-B design** — The gold standard. Baseline → Intervention → Withdrawal → Re-intervention. If behavior changes each time the intervention is introduced/withdrawn, strong evidence for causation.
- **Multiple baseline design** — The intervention is introduced at different times across behaviors, settings, or participants. If behavior changes only when the intervention is introduced, this is compelling evidence.

> *Real example:* Skinner (1953) used single-case designs extensively in his work on operant conditioning. His intensive study of individual organisms under controlled conditions laid the foundation for behavioral psychology.

> *Example:* A clinical psychologist uses an A-B-A-B design to test whether a token economy system reduces self-injurious behavior in a child with autism. Baseline data is collected, the token economy is introduced (behavior decreases), it is withdrawn (behavior increases), and it is re-introduced (behavior decreases again).

> **Pro Tip:** Single-case designs are powerful for clinical work and are increasingly valued in evidence-based practice. They allow you to demonstrate causal effects in individual cases, which is exactly what clinicians need. Do not dismiss them as "not real research" — they have their own rigorous standards (see Kratochwill et al., 2010).

## 3.8 Other Important Design Considerations

### 3.8.1 Factorial Designs

A factorial design includes **two or more independent variables** (factors), allowing you to examine main effects and interactions.

> *Example:* A 2 × 3 factorial design examining the effect of therapy type (CBT vs. psychodynamic) and severity (mild, moderate, severe) on depression outcomes. This allows you to test:
> - **Main effect of therapy type** — Is one therapy better overall?
> - **Main effect of severity** — Do outcomes differ by severity?
> - **Interaction** — Does the effectiveness of therapy type depend on severity? (Perhaps CBT is better for mild depression, but psychodynamic therapy is better for severe depression.)

### 3.8.2 Repeated Measures Designs

When you measure the same variable at multiple time points (pre-test, post-test, follow-up), you have a repeated measures design. This is common in intervention research.

### 3.8.3 Dose-Response Designs

Instead of just comparing treatment vs. no treatment, you vary the **dose** (intensity, duration, frequency) to find the optimal level.

> *Example:* Randomizing participants to receive 0, 2, 4, or 8 sessions of CBT to determine the minimum effective dose for reducing social anxiety.

## 3.9 Validity in Research Design

Every design must be evaluated for its validity — the degree to which your conclusions are trustworthy.

### 3.9.1 Internal Validity

Internal validity asks: **Did the IV really cause the change in the DV?** Threats to internal validity include:

- **History** — External events occur during the study (e.g., a major news event affects participants' mood).
- **Maturation** — Participants change naturally over time (e.g., children develop regardless of intervention).
- **Testing** — Taking a pre-test affects post-test performance.
- **Instrumentation** — The measurement tool changes between time points.
- **Attrition** — Participants drop out differentially across conditions.
- **Selection bias** — Groups differ at baseline.
- **Regression to the mean** — Extreme scores naturally move toward the average on retesting.

**Best defense:** Random assignment, control groups, blinding, and standardized procedures.

### 3.9.2 External Validity

External validity asks: **Can the findings be generalized to other people, settings, and times?** Threats include:

- **Sample characteristics** — Your sample of college students may not represent the general population (the "WEIRD" problem — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic; Henrich, Heine, & Norenzayan, 2010).
- **Setting** — Lab behavior may not reflect real-world behavior.
- **Time** — Findings from 2005 may not apply in 2025.

**Best defense:** Diverse samples, realistic settings, and replication across contexts.

### 3.9.3 Construct Validity

Construct validity asks: **Are you measuring what you think you are measuring?** If your "anxiety" measure actually measures stress, your study has a construct validity problem.

### 3.9.4 Statistical Conclusion Validity

Statistical conclusion validity asks: **Are your statistical conclusions correct?** Threats include low power, violated assumptions, and inflated Type I error rates from multiple comparisons.

> **Pro Tip:** No design is perfect. Every design involves trade-offs between internal validity, external validity, feasibility, and ethics. The key is to choose the design that best answers your question while acknowledging its limitations honestly.

## 3.10 The Decision Tree: Choosing Your Design

Here is a practical decision framework:

```
START: What is your research question?
│
├─ Do you want to TEST CAUSATION?
│   ├─ Yes → Can you MANIPULATE the IV?
│   │   ├─ Yes → Can you RANDOMLY ASSIGN participants?
│   │   │   ├─ Yes → TRUE EXPERIMENT (RCT)
│   │   │   └─ No → QUASI-EXPERIMENT
│   │   └─ No → CORRELATIONAL / OBSERVATIONAL
│   └─ No → Do you want to DESCRIBE or EXPLORE?
│       ├─ Describe → DESCRIPTIVE (survey, case study, normative)
│       └─ Explore → QUALITATIVE (interview, focus group, thematic analysis)
│
├─ What TYPE OF DATA do you need?
│   ├─ Numbers → QUANTITATIVE
│   ├─ Words/experiences → QUALITATIVE
│   └─ Both → MIXED METHODS
│
├─ WHEN will you collect data?
│   ├─ One time point → CROSS-SECTIONAL
│   └─ Multiple time points → LONGITUDINAL
│
└─ How do participants EXPERIENCE conditions?
    ├─ Each in one condition → BETWEEN-SUBJECTS
    ├─ Each in all conditions → WITHIN-SUBJECTS
    └─ Both → MIXED FACTORIAL DESIGN
```

> **Pro Tip:** Start with your question, not your method. Too many researchers choose a method first and then look for a question to fit it. This leads to poorly designed studies. Let the question drive the design.

## 3.11 Matching Design to Research Stage

Your design should also match where you are in the research process:

| Stage | Recommended Designs |
|-------|-------------------|
| **Exploring a new topic** | Qualitative (interviews, focus groups), descriptive surveys, case studies |
| **Testing a specific hypothesis** | Experiments, quasi-experiments, correlational studies |
| **Integrating existing knowledge** | Systematic reviews, meta-analyses |
| **Developing a measure** | Psychometric studies (EFA, CFA, reliability analysis) |
| **Testing an intervention** | RCTs, single-case designs, dose-response studies |

## 3.12 Common Mistakes in Research Design

1. **Using a correlational design to claim causation** — "Social media causes depression" based on a cross-sectional survey. You cannot make this claim without experimental manipulation.

2. **Ignoring confounding variables** — If you compare students who meditate vs. those who do not, you cannot attribute differences to meditation — maybe meditators are also wealthier, more educated, or have different personalities.

3. **Underpowered studies** — Testing 20 participants in a between-subjects design with 4 conditions gives you only 5 per group. This is virtually guaranteed to miss real effects (Type II error) or find spurious ones.

4. **Not pre-registering** — Without pre-registration, you might (consciously or unconsciously) change your hypotheses after seeing the results. Pre-registration on platforms like OSF (Open Science Framework) prevents this.

5. **Mismatching design to question** — Using a qualitative design for a question that requires measurement and comparison, or using a rigid experiment for a question that requires rich, contextual understanding.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Experimental designs** manipulate variables and use random assignment to establish causation. They are the strongest design for causal claims.
- **Correlational designs** examine relationships without manipulation. They cannot establish causation.
- **Descriptive designs** document what exists without testing relationships.
- **Qualitative designs** explore meaning and experience through words; **quantitative designs** measure and test through numbers; **mixed methods** combine both.
- **Cross-sectional** designs collect data at one time point; **longitudinal** designs follow participants over time.
- **Between-subjects** designs use different groups; **within-subjects** designs use the same participants across conditions.
- **RCTs** are the gold standard for intervention research but are not always feasible or ethical.
- **Quasi-experiments** lack randomization but are common in applied settings.
- **Single-case designs** study individuals intensively and are valuable in clinical contexts.
- **Validity** (internal, external, construct, statistical conclusion) must be considered in every design choice.
- Use the **decision tree** to choose a design that matches your question, not the other way around.

## Practice Questions

1. A researcher wants to study whether a new therapy reduces PTSD symptoms. She recruits 30 patients who chose to receive the therapy and compares their symptoms before and after treatment. What design is this? What is the main limitation? How could the design be improved?

2. Explain the difference between a quasi-experiment and a true experiment. Give an example of a research question where a quasi-experiment is the only ethical option.

3. You are studying the effect of background music on reading comprehension. Would you use a between-subjects or within-subjects design? Justify your choice and describe how you would address potential order effects.

4. A colleague claims that her cross-sectional survey of 500 adults proves that loneliness causes poor sleep. Critique this claim using the concepts of directionality and third-variable problems.

5. Using the decision tree, identify the appropriate design for each of the following questions:
   - "What is the lived experience of caregivers of patients with Alzheimer's disease?"
   - "Does a 6-week exercise program reduce symptoms of depression in older adults?"
   - "Is there a relationship between emotional intelligence and job satisfaction?"
   - "How prevalent is smartphone addiction among Indian college students?"
