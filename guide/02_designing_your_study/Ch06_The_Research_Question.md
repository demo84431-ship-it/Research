# Chapter 4: The Research Question

## Opening

Everything in a research paper flows from the research question. It determines your methodology, your sample, your measures, your analysis, and ultimately the contribution your work makes to the field. A vague or poorly formulated question produces a vague or poorly focused study. A precise, well-crafted question produces a study that is clear in its purpose and compelling in its findings. This chapter will teach you how to transform a broad interest into a specific, testable research question, how to formulate hypotheses, how to identify and operationalize your variables, and how to use frameworks like PICO/PICOT to sharpen your thinking. We will draw on real examples from psychology — including the work of researchers like Prof. Singh T in cognitive neuroscience and clinical psychology — to show these principles in action.

---

## 4.1 From Broad Interest to Specific Question

Every researcher starts with a **broad interest** — a general area that fascinates them. But a broad interest is not a research question. Your job is to narrow it down through a systematic process.

### The Funnel Process

**Step 1: Broad Interest**
> "I am interested in how technology affects mental health."

**Step 2: Read the Literature**
> You search PubMed and Google Scholar, read 20–30 papers, and discover several sub-topics: social media and depression, smartphone addiction, screen time and sleep, AI and anxiety, cyberbullying, digital therapy.

**Step 3: Identify a Gap**
> You notice that most studies on social media and mental health are conducted in Western populations. Very few studies examine this relationship in Indian adolescents, and even fewer use longitudinal designs.

**Step 4: Narrow to a Sub-Topic**
> "Social media use and depression in Indian adolescents."

**Step 5: Formulate a Specific Question**
> "Does daily social media use (measured in hours) predict depressive symptoms (measured by PHQ-9) in Indian adolescents aged 15–18 over a 6-month period, and does cyberbullying victimization mediate this relationship?"

Notice how the final question is **specific** (variables defined), **measurable** (instruments named), **population-specified** (Indian adolescents, 15–18), **time-bounded** (6 months), and **theoretically grounded** (tests a mediation model).

> **Pro Tip:** If you cannot state your research question in one sentence, it is not specific enough yet. Keep refining until it is clear, concise, and complete.

## 4.2 The PICO/PICOT Framework

The PICO framework was originally developed for clinical research (Richardson et al., 1995) but is now widely used across psychology. It provides a structured way to formulate research questions by specifying four components:

| Letter | Stands For | Description |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **P** | Population | Who are you studying? |
| **I** | Intervention/Exposure | What treatment, exposure, or variable are you examining? |
| **C** | Comparison | What is the comparison group or condition? |
| **O** | Outcome | What are you measuring? |
| **T** | Time | Over what period? (added in the PICOT variant) |

### PICO in Action

**Vague question:** "Does therapy help with anxiety?"

**PICO formulation:**
- **P:** Adults diagnosed with Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)
- **I:** Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) delivered via a mobile app
- **C:** Waitlist control (no treatment)
- **O:** Change in GAD-7 scores from baseline to post-treatment
- **T:** 8 weeks

**Refined question:** "In adults with GAD (P), does app-based CBT (I), compared to waitlist control (C), reduce anxiety symptoms as measured by the GAD-7 (O) over 8 weeks (T)?"

### PICO for Non-Intervention Research

The PICO framework can be adapted for non-intervention studies:

**For correlational studies:**
- **P:** Population
- **E:** Exposure/Predictor variable (instead of Intervention)
- **C:** Comparison (if applicable)
- **O:** Outcome

**Example:** "Among Indian college students (P), is higher social media use (E) associated with lower self-esteem (O) as measured by the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale?"

**For qualitative studies:**
- **P:** Population
- **S:** Situation/Phenomenon of interest
- **Co:** Context

**Example:** "What is the lived experience (S) of first-generation college students (P) navigating academic pressure in Indian universities (Co)?"

> **Pro Tip:** When preparing a systematic review or thesis proposal, write your PICO question explicitly. It will guide your search strategy, your inclusion/exclusion criteria, and your analysis plan. Reviewers and examiners love seeing a well-formulated PICO.

## 4.3 Hypothesis vs. Research Question

These two terms are related but distinct, and confusing them is a common mistake.

### Research Question

A research question is an **open-ended inquiry** that your study aims to answer. It does not predict an outcome — it simply asks.

> *Example:* "What is the relationship between mindfulness practice and emotional regulation in adolescents?"

### Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a **specific, testable prediction** about the outcome of your study. It states the expected relationship between variables.

> *Example:* "Adolescents who practice mindfulness for at least 10 minutes daily will score higher on the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) than those who do not practice mindfulness."

### Types of Hypotheses

1. **Directional hypothesis** — Predicts the direction of the effect.
   > "CBT will be *more effective* than psychodynamic therapy for reducing OCD symptoms."

2. **Non-directional hypothesis** — Predicts a difference but not the direction.
   > "There will be a *difference* in OCD symptom reduction between CBT and psychodynamic therapy."

3. **Null hypothesis (H₀)** — States that there is no effect or no relationship.
   > "There will be *no difference* in OCD symptom reduction between CBT and psychodynamic therapy."

4. **Alternative hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ)** — States that there is an effect or relationship (what you typically test).
   > "There will be a difference in OCD symptom reduction between CBT and psychodynamic therapy."

> *Real example:* In the ego depletion literature, Baumeister et al. (1998) stated a clear directional hypothesis: participants who exerted self-control in one task would perform worse on a subsequent self-control task compared to participants who did not exert initial self-control. This was a specific, testable prediction that drove the entire experimental design.

### When to Use Which

| Situation | Use |
|-----------|-----|
| Exploring a new area | Research question |
| Testing a specific prediction | Hypothesis |
| Conducting qualitative research | Research question (hypotheses are rare in qualitative research) |
| Conducting quantitative research | Hypothesis (or research question, depending on the design) |

> **Pro Tip:** In many psychology journals, you can state both: "This study aimed to answer the following question: [RQ]. Based on previous research, we hypothesized that [H₁]." This gives you flexibility while showing you have a clear prediction.

## 4.4 Operationalization of Variables

One of the most critical steps in research design is **operationalization** — defining your variables in terms of the specific procedures used to measure or manipulate them. This is where abstract concepts become concrete, measurable entities.

### What Is Operationalization?

Operationalization means answering the question: **"How will I measure or manipulate this variable in my study?"**

> *Abstract concept:* "Intelligence"
> *Operationalized:* "Score on the Raven's Progressive Matrices (Raven, 1938), a non-verbal measure of fluid intelligence"

> *Abstract concept:* "Anxiety"
> *Operationalized:* "Score on the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; Spielberger, 1983), a 40-item self-report questionnaire measuring both state and trait anxiety"

> *Abstract concept:* "Social media use"
> *Operationalized:* "Self-reported average daily hours spent on Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp over the past 30 days, measured using a custom questionnaire"

### Why Operationalization Matters

Without operationalization, your study is uninterpretable. Consider this hypothesis:

> "Stress affects memory."

This is meaningless without operationalization. Which kind of stress? Acute or chronic? Physical or psychological? And what kind of memory? Short-term, long-term, working, episodic, procedural?

**Operationalized version:**
> "Participants who undergo the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST; Kirschbaum, Pirke, & Hellhammer, 1993) — a standardized protocol involving a public speaking task and mental arithmetic — will recall fewer words from a 20-item word list (measured 10 minutes after encoding) compared to participants who complete a non-stressful control task."

Now the hypothesis is specific, measurable, and testable.

> **Pro Tip:** When you operationalize a variable, always ask: "Is there an established, validated measure for this construct?" Using an established measure (like the PHQ-9 for depression or the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index for sleep) saves you time and enhances credibility. Creating your own measure is a major undertaking — only do it when no suitable measure exists, and then you must validate it (see Chapter 3 on psychometric studies).

## 4.5 Types of Variables

Understanding variables is fundamental to research design. Here are the key types:

### 4.5.1 Independent Variable (IV)

The independent variable is what you **manipulate** (in an experiment) or **identify as the predictor** (in a correlational study). It is the presumed cause.

> *Example:* In a study testing whether sleep deprivation impairs memory, the IV is sleep condition (sleep-deprived vs. rested).

### 4.5.2 Dependent Variable (DV)

The dependent variable is what you **measure** — the presumed effect. It "depends on" the IV.

> *Example:* In the same study, the DV is the number of words recalled from a memory test.

### 4.5.3 Confounding Variables

A confounding variable is an **uncontrolled variable** that varies systematically with the IV and could provide an alternative explanation for the results. Confounds are the nemesis of good research.

> *Example:* If you compare morning people ("larks") and evening people ("owls") on cognitive performance, and you test everyone at 9 AM, the larks will likely perform better. But is this because of their chronotype (your IV) or because the larks were tested at their peak time while the owls were tested when they were still groggy? Time of testing is a confound.

**How to handle confounds:**
- **Random assignment** — Distributes confounds evenly across groups
- **Control** — Hold confounds constant (e.g., test everyone at the same time)
- **Matching** — Ensure groups are equivalent on key confounds
- **Statistical control** — Include confounds as covariates in your analysis (e.g., ANCOVA)

### 4.5.4 Extraneous Variables

Any variable other than the IV that could affect the DV. Not all extraneous variables are confounds — only those that vary systematically with the IV.

### 4.5.5 Mediator Variables

A mediator explains **how** or **why** the IV affects the DV. It is the mechanism through which the effect occurs.

> *Example:* Social media use (IV) → social comparison (mediator) → depression (DV). Social media use leads to social comparison, which in turn leads to depression. Social comparison is the mechanism.

### 4.5.6 Moderator Variables

A moderator affects the **strength or direction** of the relationship between the IV and DV. It specifies **when** or **for whom** the effect occurs.

> *Example:* Social media use (IV) → depression (DV), moderated by self-esteem. The relationship between social media use and depression is stronger for individuals with low self-esteem than for those with high self-esteem.

> **Pro Tip:** Understanding mediation and moderation is essential for advanced research. Baron and Kenny's (1986) classic paper on mediation remains highly cited, but consider also Hayes (2018) for the more modern PROCESS macro approach. For moderation, Aiken and West (1991) is the standard reference.

### 4.5.7 Control Variables

Variables that you hold constant or statistically control for to isolate the effect of the IV.

> *Example:* In a study on the effect of a mindfulness intervention on anxiety, you might control for baseline anxiety levels, age, gender, and medication status.

## 4.6 Examples from Real Research

Let us walk through how real researchers have formulated their research questions, drawn from various subfields of psychology.

### Example 1: Cognitive Neuroscience

**Research question:** "Does emotional valence of stimuli affect visual working memory capacity?"

**Operationalization:**
- IV: Emotional valence of stimuli (positive, neutral, negative images from the International Affective Picture System — IAPS)
- DV: Visual working memory capacity (measured by change detection task accuracy, K-score)
- Control: Stimulus complexity, luminance, and familiarity

**Hypothesis:** "Participants will show higher visual working memory capacity for emotionally negative stimuli compared to neutral stimuli, consistent with the attentional priority of threat-related information."

### Example 2: Clinical Psychology

**Research question:** "Is Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) skills training effective in reducing self-harm in adolescents with Borderline Personality Disorder features?"

**Operationalization:**
- P: Adolescents (13–18) meeting ≥3 BPD criteria on the SCID-II
- I: 12-week DBT skills training group (2 hours/week)
- C: Treatment as usual (TAU)
- O: Frequency of self-harm episodes (self-report diary + clinician assessment)
- T: 12 weeks + 3-month follow-up

**Hypothesis:** "Adolescents receiving DBT skills training will show a greater reduction in self-harm episodes compared to those receiving treatment as usual."

### Example 3: AI × Psychology

**Research question:** "Can a machine learning model trained on facial expressions predict depressive symptom severity in clinical interviews?"

**Operationalization:**
- IV/Predictor: Facial action units (AUs) extracted from video recordings of clinical interviews using OpenFace
- DV/Outcome: PHQ-9 scores
- Sample: 200 patients from an outpatient psychiatric clinic
- Analysis: Random forest regression with 10-fold cross-validation

**Hypothesis:** "Facial action unit patterns will significantly predict PHQ-9 scores, with the model achieving R² > 0.30 in cross-validated predictions."

> **Pro Tip:** When writing your research question for a thesis or dissertation, include the operationalization in your method section, not in the question itself. The question should be clean and readable; the method section provides the operational details.

## 4.7 Characteristics of a Good Research Question

Use the **FINER** criteria to evaluate your research question (Hulley et al., 2007):

| Criterion | Description | Check |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **F**easible | Can you actually do this study with your resources, time, and access to participants? | ✓ |
| **I**nteresting | Does it interest you? Does it interest the field? | ✓ |
| **N**ovel | Does it add something new — a new population, method, variable, or theoretical perspective? | ✓ |
| **E**thical | Can you get ethics approval? Does it avoid harm to participants? | ✓ |
| **R**elevant | Does it contribute to theory, practice, or policy? | ✓ |

### Additional Qualities

- **Specific** — Not "What affects memory?" but "Does sleep deprivation affect word recall in young adults?"
- **Answerable** — You can actually collect data to answer it.
- **Grounded in literature** — You have read enough to know that this question has not already been definitively answered.
- **Theoretically meaningful** — The answer has implications for psychological theory.

## 4.8 Common Mistakes in Formulating Research Questions

1. **Too broad** — "What causes depression?" (This is a career-long question, not a single study.)

2. **Too narrow** — "Do left-handed female psychology students at Delhi University aged 20–22 prefer blue ink over black ink?" (This may not generalize to anyone.)

3. **Not testable** — "Is the unconscious mind real?" (This is a philosophical question, not a research question — at least not in this form.)

4. **Assuming causation in a correlational design** — "How does social media use cause depression?" (If your design is a cross-sectional survey, you cannot answer a causal question.)

5. **Double-barreled questions** — "Do sleep and exercise affect academic performance?" (This is two questions. Pick one, or design a study that examines both as separate predictors.)

6. **Ignoring operationalization** — Stating a question without thinking about how you will actually measure the constructs.

> **Pro Tip:** After formulating your question, try to answer these three follow-up questions:
> 1. What is my IV? How will I manipulate or identify it?
> 2. What is my DV? How will I measure it?
> 3. What confounds should I control?
>
> If you cannot answer all three, your question needs more work.

## 4.9 From Research Question to Hypothesis: A Worked Example

Let us walk through the complete process from broad interest to testable hypothesis.

**Step 1: Broad interest**
> "I am interested in mindfulness and cognitive performance."

**Step 2: Literature review**
> You read papers on mindfulness and attention (Jha et al., 2007), mindfulness and working memory (Mrazek et al., 2013), and mindfulness interventions in educational settings (Zenner et al., 2014). You find that most studies focus on adults, and few examine the effect of brief mindfulness interventions on executive function in Indian college students.

**Step 3: Research question**
> "Does a brief mindfulness intervention improve executive function in Indian college students?"

**Step 4: Operationalization**
- IV: Mindfulness intervention (a 10-minute guided mindfulness meditation, administered once)
- DV: Executive function (measured by the Stroop task — reaction time and accuracy on incongruent trials)
- Control: Active control group (a 10-minute relaxation audio)
- Participants: 80 undergraduate students, randomly assigned
- Covariates: Baseline stress levels (PSS), trait mindfulness (MAAS)

**Step 5: Hypothesis**
> "Participants who complete a brief mindfulness meditation will show faster reaction times and higher accuracy on Stroop incongruent trials compared to participants who complete a relaxation control task, controlling for baseline stress and trait mindfulness."

**Step 6: Null hypothesis**
> "There will be no difference in Stroop task performance between the mindfulness and relaxation groups."

This is a complete, well-formulated research question with a clear hypothesis. Every component is specified, measurable, and grounded in the literature.

## 4.10 Research Questions in Qualitative Research

Qualitative research questions look different from quantitative ones. They are typically:

- **Open-ended** — They do not predict outcomes.
- **Exploratory** — They seek to understand, not to test.
- **Process-oriented** — They ask "how" and "what" rather than "how much."

### Common Qualitative Question Starters

- "What is the lived experience of...?"
- "How do individuals make sense of...?"
- "What are the processes by which...?"
- "What meanings do participants assign to...?"

> *Example:* "How do Indian therapists experience and navigate the integration of AI tools (such as chatbots and diagnostic algorithms) into their clinical practice?"

This question is open-ended, exploratory, and seeks rich, contextual understanding. It would be answered through interviews, not surveys.

> **Pro Tip:** In qualitative research, your research question may evolve as you collect and analyze data. This is a feature, not a bug. Grounded theory, in particular, embraces the idea that the question sharpens as the theory emerges from data (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

## Key Takeaways

- A **broad interest** must be narrowed into a **specific, testable research question** through a funnel process: interest → literature review → gap → question.
- The **PICO/PICOT framework** structures your question by specifying Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, and Time.
- A **research question** asks; a **hypothesis** predicts. Use both in your paper.
- **Operationalization** transforms abstract concepts into measurable variables. Always ask: "How will I measure this?"
- **Independent variables** are manipulated or identified as predictors; **dependent variables** are measured as outcomes; **confounding variables** threaten validity; **mediators** explain the mechanism; **moderators** specify the conditions.
- Use the **FINER criteria** (Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant) to evaluate your research question.
- **Qualitative research questions** are open-ended, exploratory, and process-oriented.
- Common mistakes: too broad, too narrow, not testable, assuming causation, double-barreled, ignoring operationalization.
