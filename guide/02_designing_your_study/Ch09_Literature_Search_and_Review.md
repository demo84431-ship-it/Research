# Chapter 6: Literature Search and Review

## Opening

Before you design a study, write a hypothesis, or collect a single data point, you must understand what has already been done. The literature review is not a formality — it is the intellectual foundation of your research. A well-conducted literature review helps you identify gaps in knowledge, avoid duplicating existing work, refine your research question, and position your study within the broader scholarly conversation. This chapter teaches you how to search for academic literature systematically, evaluate the quality of what you find, and organize it for effective use. Whether you are preparing a thesis proposal, a journal article, or a UGC NET exam answer, these skills are essential.

---

## 6.1 Why the Literature Review Matters

The literature review serves multiple functions:

1. **Establishes context** — Shows readers where your work fits in the field
2. **Identifies gaps** — Reveals what is unknown or understudied
3. **Avoids duplication** — Ensures you are not repeating existing work
4. **Refines your question** — Helps you narrow and sharpen your focus
5. **Builds credibility** — Demonstrates that you have done your homework
6. **Informs methodology** — Shows you how others have studied the same topic

> *Example:* Before studying the impact of COVID-19 lockdowns on mental health in India, you would review existing studies on pandemic-related mental health, lockdown effects, and Indian population-specific findings. This review might reveal that most studies focused on urban populations, creating a gap for rural research.

---

## 6.2 Types of Literature Reviews

### 6.2.1 Narrative Review

A narrative review provides a broad overview of a topic, synthesizing findings from multiple studies. It is less structured than a systematic review and allows for interpretive commentary.

- **Strengths:** Flexible, covers broad topics, good for introductory sections
- **Weaknesses:** Subjective selection of studies, potential for bias
- **Use when:** Writing the introduction of your paper or exploring a new topic

### 6.2.2 Systematic Review

A systematic review follows a strict, pre-defined protocol to identify, evaluate, and synthesize all relevant studies on a specific question.

- **Strengths:** Comprehensive, reproducible, minimizes bias
- **Weaknesses:** Time-consuming, requires specific expertise
- **Use when:** You need a complete picture of the evidence on a specific question

### 6.2.3 Meta-Analysis

A meta-analysis is a systematic review that uses statistical methods to combine results from multiple studies, producing an overall effect size.

- **Strengths:** Increases statistical power, provides precise estimates
- **Weaknesses:** Depends on quality of included studies (garbage in, garbage out)
- **Use when:** Multiple studies have examined the same question with similar methods

### 6.2.4 Scoping Review

A scoping review maps the available evidence on a broad topic, identifying key concepts, gaps, and types of evidence.

- **Strengths:** Useful for emerging topics, identifies research gaps
- **Weaknesses:** Does not assess quality of evidence
- **Use when:** You are exploring a new or interdisciplinary topic

> **Pro Tip:** For your thesis or JRF proposal, a well-structured narrative review is usually sufficient. Save systematic reviews for when you have a very specific question and enough time to do them properly.

## 6.3 Where to Search

### 6.3.1 Academic Databases

| Database | Coverage | Access |
|----------|----------|--------|
| **PubMed** | Biomedical, psychology, health | Free |
| **PsycINFO** | Psychology-specific | Institutional access |
| **Google Scholar** | Multidisciplinary | Free |
| **Scopus** | Multidisciplinary, citation analysis | Institutional access |
| **Web of Science** | Multidisciplinary, high-impact journals | Institutional access |
| **JSTOR** | Social sciences, humanities | Institutional access |
| **ERIC** | Education research | Free |
| **Cochrane Library** | Systematic reviews, health | Free |
| **medRxiv/bioRxiv** | Preprints (not yet peer-reviewed) | Free |

### 6.3.2 Indian-Specific Resources

- **Shodhganga** — Indian PhD theses
- **Indian Journals (indianjournals.com)** — Indian academic journals
- **National Digital Library of India (ndl.iitkgp.ac.in)** — Indian academic resources

> *Example:* If you are studying stigma and mental health in India, search PubMed for "stigma mental health India" AND check Shodhganga for related PhD theses. This ensures you do not miss unpublished Indian research.

## 6.4 Search Strategies

### 6.4.1 Keywords and Boolean Operators

Effective searching requires the right combination of keywords and Boolean operators:

| Operator | Function | Example |
|----------|----------|---------|
| **AND** | Narrows search (both terms must appear) | "anxiety AND CBT" |
| **OR** | Broadens search (either term can appear) | "depression OR depressive disorder" |
| **NOT** | Excludes terms | "anxiety NOT social anxiety" |
| **" "** | Exact phrase | "cognitive behavioral therapy" |
| **\*** | Wildcard (truncation) | "psycholog\*" finds psychology, psychological, psychologist |
| **( )** | Groups terms | "(CBT OR DBT) AND depression" |

### 6.4.2 Building a Search Strategy

**Step 1:** Define your PICO question
> "Does mindfulness meditation reduce anxiety in college students?"

**Step 2:** Identify key concepts
> Mindfulness, meditation, anxiety, college students

**Step 3:** Generate synonyms and related terms
> Mindfulness: mindfulness-based stress reduction, MBSR, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, MBCT
> Meditation: mindfulness meditation, guided meditation, contemplative practice
> Anxiety: generalized anxiety, anxiety symptoms, anxiety disorder, GAD
> College students: university students, undergraduate students, higher education

**Step 4:** Combine with Boolean operators
> ("mindfulness" OR "meditation" OR "MBSR" OR "MBCT") AND ("anxiety" OR "generalized anxiety" OR "anxiety symptoms") AND ("college students" OR "university students" OR "undergraduate")

**Step 5:** Apply filters
> Date range (2015–2025), English language, peer-reviewed journals

> **Pro Tip:** Keep a search log documenting which databases you searched, which terms you used, and how many results each search returned. This is essential for systematic reviews and helpful for any literature review.

## 6.5 Evaluating Source Quality

Not all published papers are equally reliable. Use these criteria to evaluate what you find:

### 6.5.1 The CRAAP Test

| Criterion | Questions to Ask |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Currency** | When was it published? Is it current enough for your topic? |
| **Relevance** | Does it directly relate to your research question? |
| **Authority** | Who are the authors? What are their credentials? Where is it published? |
| **Accuracy** | Is the methodology sound? Are claims supported by data? |
| **Purpose** | Why was it written? Is there bias? Who funded it? |

### 6.5.2 Journal Quality Indicators

- **Impact Factor** — Higher is generally better (but varies by field)
- **Peer review** — Is the journal peer-reviewed? (Avoid predatory journals)
- **Indexing** — Is it indexed in PubMed, Scopus, or Web of Science?
- **Publisher** — Reputable publishers include Elsevier, Springer, Wiley, APA, SAGE

### 6.5.3 Identifying Predatory Journals

Predatory journals charge fees but provide little or no peer review. Warning signs:

- Unsolicited email invitations to publish
- Journal scope is too broad ("International Journal of Science, Technology, and Everything")
- No editorial board listed, or board members are unverifiable
- Journal is not indexed in major databases
- Website has grammatical errors and looks unprofessional

> **Pro Tip:** Use Beall's List (or its successors) to check for predatory journals. In India, the UGC maintains a list of approved journals — publishing in a journal not on this list may not count for career advancement.

## 6.6 Organizing Your Literature

### 6.6.1 Reference Managers

Use a reference manager to organize your sources. Recommended tools:

- **Zotero** (free, open-source) — Good for collecting papers from databases
- **Mendeley** (free) — Good for PDF management and annotation
- **EndNote** (paid) — Powerful, widely used in institutions

### 6.6.2 Literature Matrix

A literature matrix is a table that organizes key information from each source. Create one early and update it as you read.

| Authors | Year | Question | Method | Sample | Key Findings | Limitations | Relevance |
|---------|------|----------|--------|--------|-------------|-------------|-----------|
| Braun & Clarke | 2006 | How to do thematic analysis? | Methodological paper | N/A | Six-phase guide | Not empirical | Methodology |
| Smith et al. | 2021 | COVID-19 and anxiety? | Cross-sectional survey | 500 UK adults | 40% reported increased anxiety | Self-report bias | Directly relevant |

### 6.6.3 Note-Taking Strategies

- **Summary notes** — Write a 3–5 sentence summary of each paper in your own words
- **Direct quotes** — Use sparingly, always with page numbers
- **Your reactions** — Note how each paper relates to your work
- **Connections** — Draw links between papers (agreements, contradictions, extensions)

> **Pro Tip:** Never copy-paste from papers into your notes without quotation marks. It is too easy to accidentally plagiarize later. Always paraphrase in your own words.

## 6.7 Citing Sources

### Citation Styles in Psychology

| Style | Used By | Example (journal article) |
|-------|---------|--------------------------|
| **APA 7th** | Most psychology journals | Smith, J. (2021). Title of article. *Journal Name*, *15*(2), 123–145. https://doi.org/xxxxx |
| **Vancouver** | Medical journals | Smith J. Title of article. J Name. 2021;15(2):123-145. |
| **Harvard** | Some social science journals | Smith, J. (2021) 'Title of article', *Journal Name*, 15(2), pp. 123–145. |

In psychology, **APA style** is the standard. Learn it early — you will use it for every paper, thesis, and assignment.

### In-Text Citations

- **Parenthetical:** (Smith, 2021)
- **Narrative:** Smith (2021) found that...
- **Multiple authors:** (Smith & Jones, 2021) for two authors; (Smith et al., 2021) for three or more

## 6.8 Common Mistakes in Literature Reviews

1. **Listing instead of synthesizing** — Do not just summarize one paper after another. Organize by theme, not by author.
2. **Ignoring contradictory evidence** — Address studies that disagree with your hypothesis
3. **Over-relying on a single source** — Diversify your references
4. **Using outdated sources** — For most topics, prioritize studies from the last 10 years
5. **Not reading what you cite** — Never cite a paper based only on its abstract. Read the full text.
6. **Plagiarism** — Always paraphrase properly and cite your sources

> *Example:* Instead of "Smith (2021) found X. Jones (2020) found Y. Lee (2019) found Z," write: "Several studies have examined the relationship between social media and anxiety, with most finding a positive association (Smith, 2021; Jones, 2020; Lee, 2019), though the effect sizes vary considerably."

## Key Takeaways

- The literature review **establishes context**, **identifies gaps**, and **builds credibility** for your research.
- Use **academic databases** (PubMed, PsycINFO, Google Scholar) and **Boolean operators** for systematic searching.
- Evaluate sources using the **CRAAP test** — check currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, and purpose.
- Use a **reference manager** (Zotero, Mendeley) and a **literature matrix** to organize your sources.
- **APA 7th edition** is the standard citation style in psychology.
- **Synthesize**, do not just list. Organize by theme, address contradictions, and prioritize recent, high-quality sources.
- **Avoid predatory journals** and always read the full text before citing.
