# Chapter 2: Anatomy of a Research Paper

## Opening

A research paper is not a collection of independent sections glued together — it is a **narrative**. It tells the story of a scientific investigation, from the question that sparked it to the answers it produced. Every section serves a purpose, and each one connects logically to the next. Understanding this architecture is essential, whether you are writing your first empirical paper for a journal submission or preparing your M.A. dissertation. In this chapter, we will walk through the IMRAD structure (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion) that dominates empirical psychology, examine what each section does, how they relate to each other, and how real papers use this structure to communicate their findings effectively.

---

## 2.1 The IMRAD Structure: An Overview

IMRAD stands for:

- **I**ntroduction
- **M**ethods
- **R**esults
- **A**nd
- **D**iscussion

This structure emerged in the mid-20th century and is now the standard format for empirical papers in psychology and the broader sciences. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) and the American Psychological Association (APA) both endorse this format.

But a full research paper includes more than just these four sections. A typical psychology paper follows this order:

1. **Title**
2. **Abstract**
3. **Keywords**
4. **Introduction**
5. **Method** (with subsections: Participants, Materials/Measures, Procedure, Design)
6. **Results**
7. **Discussion**
8. **References**
9. **Appendices** (if applicable)

Some journals also require:
- **Author Note** — Acknowledgments, funding, conflicts of interest
- **Data Availability Statement** — Where readers can access your data
- **Supplementary Materials** — Additional analyses, stimuli, or protocols

Let us now examine each major section in detail.

---

## 2.2 The Title

The title is the first (and sometimes only) thing people see. A good title is **specific, informative, and concise**. It should tell the reader exactly what the paper is about.

**Bad title:** "A Study of Memory" (too vague)

**Good title:** "The Effect of Sleep Deprivation on Working Memory Performance in Young Adults: A Randomized Crossover Trial" (specific, includes variables, population, and design)

> *Real example:* Baddeley, Lewis, and Vallar (1984) published "Exploring the Articulatory Loop" — a concise, specific title that tells you exactly what the paper investigates.

> **Pro Tip:** Include your key variables and, if space allows, your design. Titles with specific information attract more citations. Avoid titles that are too clever or vague — reviewers and search engines both prefer clarity.

---

## 2.3 The Abstract

The abstract is a **150–250 word summary** of the entire paper. It is the most-read part of any paper — many researchers decide whether to read the full text based on the abstract alone.

A well-structured abstract includes:

1. **Background/Problem** (1–2 sentences): What gap or question motivated the study?
2. **Objective** (1 sentence): What did you aim to do?
3. **Method** (2–3 sentences): Who were your participants? What did you measure? What was your design?
4. **Results** (2–3 sentences): What were your key findings? Include effect sizes and p-values.
5. **Conclusion** (1–2 sentences): What do the findings mean? What are the implications?

> *Real example:* Hofmann, Sawyer, Witt, and Oh (2010) published a meta-analysis on mindfulness-based therapy for anxiety and depression. Their abstract succinctly stated the purpose ("to evaluate the effects"), method ("meta-analysis of 39 studies"), results ("effect size Hedges' g = 0.63 for anxiety, 0.59 for depression"), and conclusion ("mindfulness-based therapy is a promising intervention").

> **Pro Tip:** Write the abstract **last**, after the entire paper is complete. Many researchers make the mistake of writing it first, then it does not accurately reflect the final paper. Also, some journals use **structured abstracts** with explicit subheadings (Background, Method, Results, Conclusion) — check the journal's guidelines.

---

## 2.4 The Introduction

The introduction is the **argument for your study**. It moves from the general to the specific, like an inverted pyramid:

1. **Broad context** — What is the general topic area? Why does it matter?
2. **Literature review** — What do we already know? Summarize key findings and theories.
3. **The gap** — What is missing? What do we not yet know?
4. **The present study** — What did you do, and why?
5. **Hypotheses** — What did you predict?

This structure is sometimes called the **CARS model** (Creating a Research Space), developed by John Swales (1990). You establish a territory (the broad topic), establish a niche (the gap), and occupy that niche (your study).

> *Real example:* Consider a paper on the relationship between social media use and body image in adolescents. The introduction might:
>
> - Open with statistics on social media use among teenagers (broad context)
> - Review existing research on social media and mental health (literature review)
> - Note that most studies focus on adults or use cross-sectional designs (the gap)
> - State that the present study uses a longitudinal design with adolescents (the present study)
> - Predict that increased social media use will predict decreased body satisfaction over time (hypothesis)

> **Pro Tip:** The introduction should be a funnel, not a firehose. Do not try to review the entire literature on your topic. Focus on the studies most directly relevant to your specific question. A focused introduction of 3–5 pages is better than a sprawling 15-page literature dump.

### How the Introduction Connects to Other Sections

- The **gap** you identify in the introduction becomes the **rationale** for your method.
- Your **hypotheses** become the **tests** you run in the results section.
- Your **discussion** must return to the theories and findings you introduced.

---

## 2.5 The Method Section

The method section is the **blueprint** of your study. Its purpose is to provide enough detail that another researcher could **replicate** your study exactly. This is the standard of transparency in psychological research.

The method section typically has several subsections:

### 2.5.1 Participants

- **Who** were your participants?
- How many? What was the age range, gender distribution?
- How were they recruited? (e.g., convenience sampling, random sampling, university participant pool)
- Were they compensated? How?
- Inclusion/exclusion criteria

> *Example:* "A total of 120 undergraduate students (65 female, 55 male; M_age = 20.3, SD = 1.8) were recruited from the psychology participant pool at the University of Delhi. Participants received course credit for their participation. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders."

### 2.5.2 Materials and Measures

- What **instruments** did you use? (questionnaires, tasks, stimuli)
- For established measures: report the name, author, reliability (Cronbach's α), and a brief description.
- For novel measures: provide the full items or a sample, and describe how they were developed.
- For experimental stimuli: describe them precisely (e.g., "200 high-frequency and 200 low-frequency English words, matched for word length").

> *Example:* "Anxiety was measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006). The GAD-7 has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.92) and good test-retest reliability in Indian samples (α = 0.85; Ghanean et al., 2011)."

### 2.5.3 Procedure

- What did participants **actually do**, step by step?
- Describe the sequence of events in chronological order.
- Include instructions given to participants, timing, and any deception used (with justification).

> *Example:* "Upon arrival, participants provided written informed consent and completed a demographic questionnaire. They were then randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 60) or the control group (n = 60). The experimental group participated in a 4-week mindfulness program (2 sessions per week, 45 minutes each), while the control group attended an equal number of sessions on stress management education. Pre- and post-intervention anxiety was measured using the GAD-7."

### 2.5.4 Design

- State your research design explicitly (e.g., "a 2 × 3 mixed factorial design with one between-subjects factor (group: experimental vs. control) and one within-subjects factor (time: pre, post, follow-up)").
- Identify your independent, dependent, and control variables.

> **Pro Tip:** The method section is where many student papers fall short. Vague descriptions like "participants were tested" are not enough. Write as if the reader is going to replicate your study tomorrow and needs every detail. Also, use past tense throughout — you are describing what *was* done, not what *should be* done.

---

## 2.6 The Results Section

The results section presents your **findings without interpretation**. It answers the question: "What did the data show?"

Key principles:

1. **Report in the order of your hypotheses.** If you stated three hypotheses in the introduction, address them in the same order.
2. **Use the active or passive voice consistently.** APA 7th edition encourages active voice ("We analyzed the data using...").
3. **Report effect sizes and confidence intervals**, not just p-values. APA 7th edition requires this.
4. **Use tables and figures** to present complex data clearly.
5. **Do not interpret** — save that for the discussion.

### What to Report

For a typical quantitative study:
- Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies)
- Assumption checks (normality, homogeneity of variance)
- Inferential statistics (t-tests, ANOVA, regression, chi-square)
- Effect sizes (Cohen's d, η², r)
- Confidence intervals
- Post-hoc tests (if applicable)

> *Example:* "An independent samples t-test revealed that the experimental group (M = 8.2, SD = 3.1) reported significantly lower anxiety scores than the control group (M = 12.7, SD = 3.8) at post-intervention, t(118) = 7.23, p < .001, d = 1.30, 95% CI [−5.78, −3.22]. This represents a large effect according to Cohen's (1988) conventions."

### Common Mistakes in the Results Section

- **Interpreting results** — "This shows that mindfulness is effective" belongs in the discussion, not here.
- **Omitting non-significant results** — Report all planned analyses, whether significant or not. Cherry-picking significant results is a form of researcher bias.
- **Reporting only p-values** — A p-value tells you whether an effect is statistically significant, but not how large or meaningful it is. Always include effect sizes.
- **Repeating numbers from tables in the text** — Use the text to highlight key findings, not to restate every number.

> **Pro Tip:** Think of the results section as presenting evidence to a jury. You lay out the facts — the numbers, the tests, the patterns. The jury (your readers) can see the evidence for themselves. The argument about what those facts *mean* comes later, in the discussion.

---

## 2.7 The Discussion Section

The discussion is where you **interpret your findings** and connect them to the broader literature. This is often the most challenging section to write, because it requires you to think deeply about what your results mean.

A strong discussion follows this structure:

1. **Summary of key findings** (1 paragraph) — Restate your main results in plain language.
2. **Interpretation** — What do the results mean? How do they relate to your hypotheses?
3. **Comparison with existing literature** — How do your findings fit with (or contradict) previous research?
4. **Theoretical implications** — What do your findings mean for the theories you discussed in the introduction?
5. **Practical implications** — Are there real-world applications? (Especially for applied research.)
6. **Limitations** — What are the weaknesses of your study? Be honest but not self-destructive.
7. **Future directions** — What should be studied next?
8. **Conclusion** — A strong final paragraph that leaves the reader with a clear takeaway.

> *Real example:* In their study on working memory, Baddeley et al. (1984) used the discussion to explain how their findings (that articulatory suppression abolished the word-length effect) supported the existence of a phonological loop within working memory. They connected their specific data to the broader theoretical model, explained why alternative accounts were less convincing, and suggested future experiments to test the model further.

> **Pro Tip:** The discussion is where you demonstrate your expertise. Do not just say "our results are consistent with previous research" — explain *why* they are consistent, and what that consistency means theoretically. A strong discussion elevates a paper from descriptive to insightful.

---

## 2.8 The References Section

Every source you cite in the paper must appear in the references, and vice versa. Psychology papers use **APA style** (7th edition).

Key APA reference examples:

**Journal article:**
> Baddeley, A. D., & Hitch, G. (1974). Working memory. In G. H. Bower (Ed.), *The psychology of learning and motivation* (Vol. 8, pp. 47–89). Academic Press.

**Book:**
> Braun, V., & Clarke, V. (2006). Using thematic analysis in psychology. *Qualitative Research in Psychology, 3*(2), 77–101.

**Book chapter:**
> Creswell, J. W., & Creswell, J. D. (2018). *Research design: Qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods approaches* (5th ed.). SAGE.

> **Pro Tip:** Use a reference manager from day one. Zotero (free), Mendeley (free), or EndNote will save you hundreds of hours. Do not format references manually — it is a waste of time and prone to errors. Most reference managers have browser plugins that capture citation information directly from journal websites.

---

## 2.9 Other Important Components

### 2.9.1 Keywords

Below the abstract, list 4–6 keywords that help others find your paper in databases. Choose terms that are specific and commonly used in your field.

> **Pro Tip:** Check what keywords similar papers in your target journal use. Also, search your keywords on PubMed and Google Scholar — if they return relevant results, they are good choices.

### 2.9.2 Tables and Figures

- **Tables** present numerical data (means, correlations, regression coefficients).
- **Figures** present visual data (graphs, charts, diagrams, images of stimuli).
- Each table/figure should be interpretable on its own — include clear titles, labels, and notes.
- Reference every table/figure in the text (e.g., "As shown in Table 1...").

### 2.9.3 Appendices

Use appendices for material that is too detailed for the main text but necessary for replication:
- Full questionnaire items
- Stimuli lists
- Detailed statistical output
- Supplementary analyses

## 2.10 A Real Paper's Structure: Walkthrough

Let us walk through the structure of a real, highly cited psychology paper to see how these sections work in practice.

**Paper:** Hofmann, S. G., Sawyer, A. T., Witt, A. A., & Oh, D. (2010). The effect of mindfulness-based therapy on anxiety and depression: A meta-analytic review. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 78*(2), 169–183.

| Section | What It Does |
|---------|-------------|
| **Title** | Specifies the intervention (mindfulness-based therapy), outcomes (anxiety and depression), and method (meta-analytic review) |
| **Abstract** | Summarizes purpose, method (39 studies, 1,140 participants), results (Hedges' g = 0.63 for anxiety, 0.59 for depression), and conclusion |
| **Introduction** | Defines mindfulness, reviews the growing literature, identifies the gap (no comprehensive meta-analysis), states the purpose |
| **Method** | Describes search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, coding procedures, statistical approach (random-effects model) |
| **Results** | Reports overall effect sizes, moderator analyses (type of disorder, type of mindfulness intervention), heterogeneity statistics |
| **Discussion** | Interprets findings, compares with previous reviews, discusses mechanisms, acknowledges limitations (small sample sizes, publication bias), suggests future directions |
| **References** | 60+ references in APA format |

Notice how each section flows logically from the previous one. The introduction raises questions that the method answers. The method produces data that the results present. The results need interpretation that the discussion provides. It is a coherent narrative, not a disconnected list.

## 2.11 How Sections Connect: The Thread

Think of a research paper as having a **thread** that runs through every section:

| Section | The Thread |
|---------|-----------|
| **Introduction** | "Here is what we do not know and why it matters" |
| **Method** | "Here is how we found out" |
| **Results** | "Here is what we found" |
| **Discussion** | "Here is what it means" |

Every hypothesis you raise in the introduction must be addressed in the results. Every limitation you acknowledge in the discussion should relate to choices you made in the method. Every theory you cite in the introduction should be revisited in the discussion.

When you write, constantly check: **Does this paragraph connect to the thread?** If a paragraph in your introduction does not help build the case for your study, cut it. If a result does not address a hypothesis, move it to supplementary materials. The thread keeps the paper focused and coherent.

> **Pro Tip:** After writing a draft, create an outline that lists the main point of each paragraph. Then read the outline — does it flow logically? If not, rearrange. Many papers suffer not from bad writing but from bad organization.

---

## 2.12 Variations on the Standard Structure

Not all psychology papers follow the IMRAD format exactly:

- **Review papers** replace Methods/Results/Discussion with thematic or chronological sections.
- **Case studies** may follow a modified structure (Introduction, Case Presentation, Discussion).
- **Qualitative papers** often replace Results with "Findings" or "Themes" and include a detailed reflexivity section.
- **Theoretical papers** may have no empirical data at all, focusing on argumentation and theory development.
- **Registered Reports** include a Stage 1 (Introduction + Method, reviewed before data collection) and Stage 2 (Results + Discussion, reviewed after).

Regardless of the variation, the underlying logic remains: **state the question, explain your approach, present the evidence, interpret the meaning.**

---

## Key Takeaways

- The **IMRAD structure** (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) is the standard format for empirical psychology papers.
- The **title** should be specific and informative; the **abstract** should summarize the entire paper in 150–250 words.
- The **introduction** is an inverted pyramid: broad context → literature → gap → your study → hypotheses.
- The **method** section must be detailed enough for replication. Include participants, materials, procedure, and design.
- The **results** section presents findings without interpretation. Report effect sizes, confidence intervals, and all planned analyses.
- The **discussion** interprets findings, connects to theory, acknowledges limitations, and suggests future directions.
- A **thread** connects all sections: every hypothesis raised must be addressed, every limitation acknowledged must relate to methodological choices.
- Use a **reference manager** from day one. APA 7th edition is the standard for psychology.
