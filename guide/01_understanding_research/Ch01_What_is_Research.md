# Chapter 1: What Is Research?

## Opening

You already know more about research than you think. Every time you searched for the best study technique before an exam, compared reviews before buying a phone, or asked a friend which restaurant to try — you were conducting informal research. You identified a question, gathered information, evaluated it, and drew a conclusion. Formal research in psychology follows the same logic, but with a critical difference: it demands rigor, transparency, and systematic methods so that others can evaluate, replicate, and build upon your findings. This chapter lays the groundwork for everything that follows. By the end, you will understand the different types of research, how the scientific method operates in psychology, and why these distinctions matter when you sit down to write your own paper.

---

## 1.1 Defining Research

Research is a **systematic process of inquiry** designed to discover, interpret, or revise facts, theories, and applications. The key word here is *systematic*. Unlike casual observation, research follows a structured plan — you define a question, choose a method, collect data, analyze it, and report your findings in a way that allows others to scrutinize your work.

In psychology, research serves two broad purposes:

- **To understand** — How does memory work? Why do people conform? What happens in the brain during a panic attack?
- **To improve** — Can a new therapy reduce symptoms of depression? Does a mindfulness intervention help students perform better on exams?

These two purposes map onto a distinction you will encounter repeatedly: **basic research** versus **applied research**.

---

## 1.2 Basic vs. Applied Research

**Basic research** (also called fundamental or pure research) seeks to expand knowledge for its own sake. It asks questions about the nature of human behavior, cognition, and emotion without an immediate practical goal.

> *Example:* Baddeley and Hitch (1974) proposed the working memory model — a theory describing how short-term memory operates through multiple subsystems (the phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, and central executive). At the time, there was no immediate clinical application. The goal was to understand how the mind temporarily holds and manipulates information. Decades later, this model became foundational for understanding ADHD, learning disabilities, and cognitive aging.

**Applied research** aims to solve a specific, real-world problem. It takes existing knowledge and tests whether it works in practice.

> *Example:* Linehan (1993) developed Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) specifically to treat Borderline Personality Disorder, a condition with extremely high suicide rates. Her research was not about understanding personality in the abstract — it was about saving lives. Randomized controlled trials showed DBT significantly reduced suicidal behavior, self-harm, and hospitalization.

The line between basic and applied is often blurry. A single study can serve both purposes. Cognitive neuroscience research on attention (basic) might eventually inform interventions for children with ADHD (applied). What matters is that you recognize *where your own research question falls*, because it shapes your methodology, your audience, and even where you submit your paper.

> **Pro Tip:** When writing your introduction, make the relevance of your research explicit. If your work is basic, explain the theoretical gap. If applied, state the practical problem. Reviewers and examiners want to know *why this matters*.

## 1.3 Types of Research by Methodology

The most fundamental classification in psychology research divides studies by their **methodological approach**. Let us walk through the major types.

### 1.3.1 Empirical Research

Empirical research involves the **direct collection and analysis of data**. You observe, measure, experiment, or survey. This is the backbone of psychology — most papers you read in journals like *Psychological Science*, *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, or *Cognition* are empirical studies.

An empirical paper reports original data. It has participants, a procedure, results (often with statistical tests), and a discussion interpreting those results.

> *Example:* Ebbinghaus (1885) conducted the first systematic empirical study of memory. He memorized lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself at various intervals, discovering the "forgetting curve" — the exponential decline of memory over time. This was empirical because he collected data on his own recall performance under controlled conditions.

Empirical research can be further subdivided by its **data type** — which brings us to the next major distinction.

### 1.3.2 Quantitative Research

Quantitative research deals in **numbers**. It measures variables, counts occurrences, and uses statistical analysis to test hypotheses. In psychology, this is the dominant paradigm.

Common quantitative methods include:
- **Experiments** — Manipulating an independent variable to observe its effect on a dependent variable (e.g., testing whether sleep deprivation impairs reaction time)
- **Surveys/Questionnaires** — Collecting self-report data from large samples (e.g., measuring anxiety levels across different age groups using the GAD-7)
- **Correlational studies** — Examining relationships between variables without manipulation (e.g., the relationship between social media use and self-esteem)
- **Psychometric studies** — Developing and validating measurement tools (e.g., creating a new scale to measure AI anxiety)

> *Example:* In a study on the Stroop effect, participants name the ink color of color words (e.g., the word "RED" printed in blue ink). Reaction times are measured in milliseconds. The data is numerical, and you can run paired t-tests or repeated-measures ANOVA to compare congruent vs. incongruent conditions.

### 1.3.3 Qualitative Research

Qualitative research deals in **words, meanings, and experiences**. It asks *how* and *why* rather than *how much*. This approach is particularly valuable when you want to understand a phenomenon from the perspective of the people experiencing it — something that numbers alone cannot capture.

Common qualitative methods include:
- **Interviews** — Semi-structured or unstructured conversations with participants
- **Focus groups** — Group discussions guided by a facilitator
- **Thematic analysis** — Identifying patterns and themes in textual data
- **Grounded theory** — Building theory inductively from data
- **Phenomenology** — Exploring the lived experience of a phenomenon
- **Case studies** — In-depth examination of a single individual or small group

> *Example:* Braun and Clarke (2006) published one of the most cited papers in qualitative research — their six-phase guide to thematic analysis. Their work did not involve statistics or experiments. Instead, they systematically described how researchers should identify, analyze, and report patterns (themes) within qualitative data. This paper has been cited over 100,000 times, demonstrating that qualitative research is valued and impactful.

> *Example:* Smith, Flowers, and Larkin (2009) developed Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA), a method for understanding how individuals make sense of major life experiences. A clinical psychologist using IPA might interview survivors of trauma to understand their recovery process — not to count symptoms, but to capture the richness of their experience.

### 1.3.4 Mixed Methods Research

Mixed methods research **combines quantitative and qualitative approaches** within a single study or program of research. The logic is that each method compensates for the limitations of the other.

Common mixed-methods designs include:
- **Convergent parallel** — Collecting quantitative and qualitative data simultaneously, then merging results
- **Explanatory sequential** — Collecting quantitative data first, then using qualitative data to explain the findings
- **Exploratory sequential** — Starting with qualitative exploration, then using those findings to design a quantitative study

> *Example:* You might conduct a randomized controlled trial (quantitative) showing that a mindfulness intervention reduces test anxiety in college students. Then you interview a subset of participants (qualitative) to understand *how* they experienced the intervention — what changed in their thinking, what challenges they faced, what they found helpful. The numbers tell you *whether* it worked; the interviews tell you *why* and *how*.

> **Pro Tip:** Mixed methods research is increasingly valued in psychology, especially in applied and clinical fields. If you are planning a thesis or dissertation, consider whether a mixed-methods design could strengthen your work. Reviewers appreciate the depth that comes from combining approaches.

### 1.3.5 Review Research

Not all research papers collect new data. **Review papers** synthesize, evaluate, and organize existing research on a topic. They are enormously valuable because they help the field see the forest, not just the trees.

Types of review research include:
- **Narrative reviews** — Summarizing the literature on a topic in a structured essay format
- **Systematic reviews** — Using a rigorous, pre-defined protocol to find and evaluate all relevant studies on a specific question
- **Meta-analyses** — Statistically combining results from multiple studies to calculate an overall effect size
- **Scoping reviews** — Mapping the breadth of research on a broad topic to identify gaps

> *Example:* Cuijpers, Karyotaki, Reijnders, and Eberhard (2018) conducted a meta-analysis of 367 randomized controlled trials examining the efficacy of psychotherapy for depression. By pooling data across studies, they could estimate the overall effect size (Cohen's d = 0.75) with far more precision than any single study. This is the power of meta-analysis — it turns many small signals into one clear message.

> *Example:* A systematic review by Carl et al. (2020) examined the efficacy of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) for anxiety disorders across 101 randomized controlled trials. The review followed the PRISMA guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses), ensuring transparency and replicability in how studies were selected and evaluated.

## 1.4 Types of Research by Purpose

Beyond methodology, research can be classified by its **purpose** or **goal**.

### 1.4.1 Descriptive Research

Descriptive research aims to systematically describe a phenomenon, population, or situation. It answers the question *what is happening?* without necessarily explaining why.

> *Example:* A survey of 2,000 Indian college students measuring the prevalence of smartphone addiction using the Smartphone Addiction Scale (SAS). The study does not test whether smartphone addiction *causes* anything — it simply documents how widespread it is.

### 1.4.2 Exploratory Research

Exploratory research investigates a topic that is not well understood. It is often the first step in a new area, generating hypotheses rather than testing them.

> *Example:* In the early 2000s, researchers began exploring whether playing violent video games increased aggressive behavior. Early studies were exploratory — they examined correlations and generated hypotheses that later, more rigorous experimental studies would test.

### 1.4.3 Explanatory Research

Explanatory research seeks to explain *why* something happens. It tests causal hypotheses, typically through experiments.

> *Example:* Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven, and Tice (1998) conducted a series of experiments to test their "ego depletion" hypothesis — that self-control draws on a limited resource. Participants who resisted cookies (depleting self-control) subsequently gave up on puzzles faster than those who did not resist. This was explanatory research because it tested a causal mechanism.

### 1.4.4 Predictive Research

Predictive research uses known variables to forecast future outcomes.

> *Example:* Using machine learning to predict which patients with depression will respond to a specific antidepressant based on baseline EEG patterns and demographic variables.

## 1.5 The Scientific Method in Psychology

The scientific method is not a rigid recipe — it is a flexible framework for building reliable knowledge. In psychology, it typically follows these steps:

### Step 1: Observe and Ask a Question

Research begins with curiosity. You notice something interesting — a pattern, an inconsistency, a gap in the literature.

> *You notice that your friends who meditate seem less anxious during exams. You wonder: Does meditation actually reduce test anxiety, or are naturally calm people more likely to meditate?*

### Step 2: Review the Literature

Before designing a study, you must understand what is already known. This prevents you from reinventing the wheel and helps you identify gaps.

> *You search PubMed and find several studies on mindfulness-based interventions for anxiety (Hofmann et al., 2010), but few specifically examine test anxiety in Indian college students.*

### Step 3: Formulate a Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a **testable prediction** about the relationship between variables.

> *"College students who participate in a 4-week mindfulness program will show greater reductions in test anxiety compared to a control group."*

### Step 4: Design the Study

You choose your methodology — experimental or correlational? Quantitative or qualitative? What is your sample? What measures will you use?

> *You design a randomized controlled trial with 60 college students, using the Westside Test Anxiety Scale as your primary measure.*

### Step 5: Collect Data

You recruit participants, run your intervention, and collect your data according to your pre-registered protocol.

### Step 6: Analyze Data

You run your statistical tests — perhaps an ANCOVA controlling for baseline anxiety levels.

### Step 7: Interpret and Report

You discuss what your findings mean, how they relate to existing literature, what the limitations are, and what should be studied next.

> **Pro Tip:** The scientific method is iterative, not linear. Your results often raise new questions, leading you back to step 1. A good researcher is comfortable with this cycle.

## 1.6 The Role of Theory in Psychology Research

Research does not happen in a theoretical vacuum. In psychology, theories provide the framework within which you formulate hypotheses and interpret results.

> *Example:* If you are studying memory, you might draw on Baddeley's working memory model or Craik and Lockhart's (1972) levels of processing framework. If you are studying anxiety, you might draw on Beck's cognitive theory or Barlow's triple vulnerability model.

A strong research paper connects its findings to existing theory. Your results either **support**, **extend**, **refine**, or **challenge** a theoretical position. This is what makes a paper contribute to the field — not just the data, but the theoretical implications.

> **Pro Tip:** When writing your discussion section, always ask: "What do my findings mean for theory?" This is often the difference between a paper that gets published and one that gets rejected.

## 1.7 Paradigms and Worldviews in Research

Behind every research study lies a set of assumptions about the nature of reality (ontology) and how we can know it (epistemology). These assumptions form research **paradigms**.

- **Positivism** — Reality exists objectively and can be measured. This underpins most quantitative research.
- **Post-positivism** — Reality exists but can only be imperfectly understood. This is the dominant paradigm in psychology today — we use rigorous methods but acknowledge that all knowledge is provisional.
- **Constructivism/Interpretivism** — Reality is socially constructed. This underpins most qualitative research.
- **Pragmatism** — The best method is the one that answers the question. This underpins mixed methods research.

> *Example:* A positivist might say "IQ is a measurable, objective trait." A constructivist might say "IQ is a socially constructed concept that reflects cultural values about intelligence." Neither is inherently right or wrong — they represent different philosophical positions that lead to different research approaches.

---

## 1.8 Why These Distinctions Matter for You

As a researcher preparing for JRF and beyond, understanding these distinctions is not academic trivia — it is practical knowledge:

1. **For your thesis:** Your methodology chapter must justify your approach. Knowing whether your study is experimental, correlational, or qualitative shapes your entire design.

2. **For journal submissions:** Journals have methodological preferences. *Qualitative Psychology* will not publish a quantitative survey. *Journal of Experimental Psychology* will not publish a narrative review.

3. **For UGC NET/JRF:** Expect questions on research methodology. Understanding the difference between basic and applied research, or between a quasi-experiment and a true experiment, is exam-relevant.

4. **For reading papers:** Classifying a paper by type helps you evaluate it. An exploratory qualitative study should not be criticized for not having a large sample — that is not its purpose.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Research** is a systematic process of inquiry that follows a structured, transparent methodology.
- **Basic research** expands knowledge; **applied research** solves practical problems. The line between them is often blurry.
- **Empirical research** collects original data; **review research** synthesizes existing findings.
- **Quantitative** methods use numbers and statistics; **qualitative** methods use words and meanings; **mixed methods** combine both.
- The **scientific method** in psychology is iterative: observe, review, hypothesize, design, collect, analyze, interpret, repeat.
- **Theories** provide the framework for research. Good research connects its findings to theoretical implications.
- Research **paradigms** (positivism, constructivism, pragmatism) reflect underlying philosophical assumptions that shape methodology.
