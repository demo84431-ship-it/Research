Abstract

Yogic meditation, involving focused awareness, is gaining scientific attention for its impact on brain function and neurochemical balance. It enhances autonomic control and influences neurotransmitters related to emotional and cognitive health.

This narrative review explored scientific studies on the effects of yogic meditation on EEG patterns and neurotransmitter levels. 21 peer-reviewed articles were selected through a structured search of PubMed, PubMed Central, and Google Scholar.

The findings indicate that yogic meditation promotes coherent brainwave activity and modulates neurotransmitters, such as gamma-aminobutyric acid, thereby reducing anxiety, improving emotional regulation, and providing pain relief and autonomic stability.

The studies literature suggest that yogic meditation may enhance brain function and emotional well-being, but these findings remain preliminary and require more well-designed confirmation.

Keywords:
 Meditation, Yoga, Brainwave, Electroencephalography, Neurotransmitters

1. Introduction

Meditation (
dhyana
) is part of 
Ashtanga
 yoga, which can be considered part of the mind-body medicine that deals with mental health [
1
]. According to Sage Patanjali, meditation represents one of the yogic techniques to achieve peace of mind, considered one of the main goals of human existence [
2

3

4
,
5

6
,
7

The electroencephalogram (EEG) was introduced nearly 100 years ago and is still a method of choice for many research questions, including the study of brain activity [
8
]. Of note, meditation induces changes in brain activity as documented by EEG, particularly in the alpha and theta bands [
9
]. These bands typically appear when an individual is relaxed but awake [
10
].

Neurotransmitters are key players in modulating and regulating behaviour and anxiety [
[11]
, 
[12]
, 
[13]
]. They represent the target of the available pharmacological treatments for mental disorders [
14

15

Various health benefits of meditation are narrated in one of the classical compendiums of 
Hatha Yoga
 - 
Gheranda Samhita
- but there is a lack of comprehensive literature on scientific study in this subject matter. Therefore, the present review was undertaken to consolidate the scientific evidence on the biological basis of yogic meditation, with a focus on the effects of this technique on EEG and neurotransmitters.

2. Methodology

2.1. Literature search strategy

A narrative literature review was conducted using online databases, including PubMed, PubMed Central, and Google Scholar. The aim was to identify research studies exploring the relationship between yogic meditation, EEG activity, and neurotransmitter modulation. The search covered publications from 1976 to 2024. The keywords and Boolean operators were applied: “Meditation OR Dhyana” AND (“EEG” OR Brain Waves”) AND (“Neurotransmitters OR “Brain Chemicals” OR “Dopamine” OR “GABA” OR “Serotonin”). This structured search approach combined both traditional and modern research evidence.

2.2. Study selection criteria

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and experimental studies published in English and involving human participants were included in this review. Studies without abstracts or with inaccessible full texts were excluded. An initial pool of 527 records was screened; after applying inclusion and exclusion criteria and removing duplicates, 21 studies met the eligibility requirements. The flow diagram (
Figure_1
) illustrates the systematic and screening process. These selected studies specifically assessed the neurophysiological and biochemical effects of meditation, with a focus on EEG activity and neurotransmitter modulation.

2.3. Types of meditation techniques reviewed

The reviewed studies assessed various yogic meditation techniques, including Yoga Nidra, Transcendental Meditation, Mindfulness Meditation, 
Vipassana
 Meditation, Triarchic Body-Pathway Relaxation Technique, Amrita Meditation, Cyclic Meditation, and Acem Meditation. Most studies demonstrated increased alpha, theta, and gamma wave activity, along with modulation of serotonin, dopamine, noradrenaline, and GABA levels, reflecting improved neural integration and emotional regulation.

3. Results

The synthesis of 21 studies revealed consistent evidence that meditation practice influences brain wave activity and neurotransmitter modulation. Most studies reported increased alpha and theta waves, reflecting enhanced relaxation and focused attention, along with elevated levels of dopamine, GABA, and serotonin, indicating improved emotional balance and stress regulation.

3.1. Yoga 
Nidra

Swami Satyananda Saraswati introduced the Yoga Nidra method to promote relaxation for the body and Mind. Yoga Nidra, known as psychic sleep, differs from regular sleep; during its practice, the practitioner feels awareness, and during the Yoga Nidra practice, the practitioner performs in a supine pose (Shavasana).

The practice of Yoga Nidra begins with preparation, which includes adopting a ‘corpse pose’. This is followed by setting a personal Sankalp (resolution). The practitioner then moves through a rotation of consciousness, focusing on different body parts and breath awareness, where the breath is visualized, flowing in and out of the body. After this, the practitioner visualises various images with closed eyes, followed by another resolution (
Sankalp
). The session concludes by focusing on external sounds in the surroundings, maintaining body and mind awareness [
42
].

3.1.1. EEG

An 11c-raclopride positron emission tomography study revealed that Yoga Nidra meditation reduced raclopride binding, signifying increased (electroencephalogram) EEG theta activity. Participants reported a reduced desire for activity and increased activation of imagination senses [
15
].

3.1.2. Neurotransmitters

The study highlighted an increase in endogenous dopamine release during Yoga Nidra meditation, along with the suppression of cortico-striatal glutamatergic transmission [
15
].

3.2. Transcendental Meditation

Maharishi Mahesh Yogi introduced Transcendental Meditation (TM) in the mid-1950s. Transcendence is sought as a state of pure consciousness free from cognitive impediments. This practice involves silently repeating mantras for 15–20 min, which promotes concentration, self-awareness, and stress reduction. Various changes in electroencephalogram (EEG) patterns have been observed during this practice and over time while practitioners become more familiar with the deeper level of consciousness. Of note, this technique was used to improve different clinical conditions, including psychiatric [
43
] and cardiometabolic disorders [
44
].

3.2.1. EEG

Research shows that TM falls into the “transcendental category”, characterised by increased frontal alpha1 power and coherence and elevated blood flow in the frontal brain region [
45
].

3.2.2. Neurotransmitters

A study showed that TM increases serotonin metabolism and reduces norepinephrine and epinephrine levels, as shown by a decrease in the concentration of metabolite vanillylmandelic acid [
41
]. Another study noted that TM practice also increases in functionally high-affinity beta-adrenergic receptors [
46
].

3.3. Mindfulness meditation

Mindfulness Meditation, a revisitation of a meditative technique provided by Theravada Buddhism, involves purposefully paying attention to thoughts and observing them in a gentle and non-judgemental manner. It emphasizes focusing on the present moment rather than reinforcing reactivity tied to past and future events. Research suggests this practice helps reduce ruminative thinking and emotional reactivity [
47
].

3.3.1. EEG

In a study by Davidson and collaborators [
38
], the authors observed that an 8-week mindfulness meditation program applied to healthy employees significantly increased left-brain activation, which was associated with enhanced humoral immunity.

3.3.2. Neurotransmitters

A study conducted on 16 participants revealed, through the use of the psychological Well-being Scale, that mindfulness meditation is associated with increased grey matter density in brain regions linked to the serotonin, cholinergic, and norepinephrine systems, which play a key role in mental health [
48
].

3.4. Vipassana meditation

Vipassana means seeing things as they are. This meditation technique is part of the Buddhist tradition. It helps to counteract one of the factors Buddhism believes is associated with human suffering, namely incorrect vision. Furthermore, this practice encourages practitioners to focus on the present moment, reducing regret and anticipatory anxiety [
19
].

3.4.1. EEG

A study showed that Vipassana Meditation increased theta phase consistency and early-event-related alpha synchronization in response to stimuli. Additionally, reactivity to distraction decreased [
35
].

3.4.2. Neurotransmitters

Elevated levels of melatonin and serotonin were observed in long-term meditators, along with a significant reduction in stress levels among practitioners [
25
].

3.5. TriArchic relaxation technique

The TriArchic Relaxation Technique, inspired by Buddhist principles, is a Chinese mindfulness exercise that enhances body awareness and thoughts from three perspectives: front, back, and both sides. It can be practised in a sitting or lying down position [
20
].

3.5.1. EEG

Chan and co-authors conducted a randomized trial to investigate the effect of Triarchic Body-pathway Relaxation (TBPR) Technique. The study found that TBPR led to increased activation in the brain's left hemisphere, which is associated with positive thoughts. Additionally, the technique significantly enhanced internal attention, as evidenced by increased midline theta power [
20
].

3.6. Amrita Meditation

Amrita (immortality) meditation technique, designed by 
Mata Amritanandamayi Devi
, is tailored for the modern generation. This practice combines yogic postures, relaxation, and meditation for durations of 8,2 and 13 min, respectively, followed by staying in silence for 5 min [
21
].

3.6.1. Neurotransmitters

A study investigating the effect of 
Amrita
 Meditation found that practising the IAM (Integrated Amrita Meditation) Technique for one week led to elevated plasma Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) levels, along with a reduction in stress and anxiety in participants [
49
].

3.7. Cyclic Meditation

Cyclic Meditation is a practice that integrates 
mantra
 chanting with isometric muscle contraction. It involves chanting 
mantras
 from 
Mandukya Upanisad
 followed by the performance of isometric muscle contraction through asanas such as 
Tadasana, Ardhakatichakrasana, Shashankasana
, and 
Ustrasana,
 concluding with relaxation techniques and 
om chanting
 [
28
].

3.7.1. EEG

In a study, 24 college students were randomised to practice Cyclic Meditation or supine rest. The group on Cyclic Meditation showed enhanced awareness and attention. Furthermore, the authors observed an increase in positive emotions, indicated by elevated gamma wave activity, while the control group exhibited delta wave activity, reflecting a state of dullness [
28
].

3.8. Acem Meditation

Acem Meditation is a non-directive meditation technique that requires no specific focus of attention. It is practiced with a free mental attitude similar to mindfulness meditation, where there is no attempted correction or control over unnecessary thoughts. Instead, a free flow of thoughts is encouraged, accompanied by the repetition of a sound that lacks linguistic or emotional meaning.

3.8.1. EEG

In a study conducted by Lagopoulos and co-authors (3), electroencephalogram (EEG) changes were noted, indicating that 
Acem Meditation
 significantly alters theta and alpha electroencephalogram (EEG) patterns more than regular relaxation. The study found increased theta and alpha power during the meditation condition compared to the resting condition.

4. Discussion

The reviewed studies demonstrated that meditation produces consistent neurophysiological and biochemical effects, supporting its role as an integrative mind-body practice. Techniques such as Transcendental Meditation, Yoga Nidra, Vipassana, and Mindfulness were found to enhance alpha and theta EEG activity, indicating improved relaxation, focused attention, and emotional regulation [
3
,
15
,
29
]. These EEG change patterns correspond to a shift toward parasympathetic activation and reduced cortical arousal.

Physiologically, yogic meditation is observed to modulate brain networks and neural circuitry, particularly by reducing activity in the default mode network, thereby improving attention and emotional control [
50
]. It also promotes neuroplasticity, strengthening connections in regions such as the prefrontal cortex, insula, and hippocampus [
51
,
52
]. These neural adaptations support enhanced autonomic balance, reduced HPA-axis activity, and a more stable regulation of key neurotransmitters such as GABA and serotonin, collectively contributing to the observed improvements in alpha-theta brainwave patterns and emotional resilience [
[53]
, 
[54]
, 
[55]
, 
[56]
, 
[57]
].

However, variations in findings across studies may be attributed to differences in sample sizes, participant characteristics, and control conditions. For instance, open-label studies often reported more potent effects than controlled designs, possibly reflecting expectancy bias or differences in meditation experience.

Biochemical evidence indicates increased levels of serotonin, dopamine, and GABA, and decreased levels of norepinephrine and cortisol, suggesting that meditation promotes emotional stability and stress resilience [
41
,
23
,
39
].

Nonetheless, inconsistencies among studies could stem from variations in meditation duration, frequency and the use of biochemical assays. Increased frontal coherence in EEG analyses further suggests enhanced attentional control and neural integration. Further increases in frontal coherence in EEG studies indicate better attentional control and neural integration [
20
,
28
].

In interpreting these findings, it is also important to acknowledge that the present review is conducted as a systematic review, following a structured approach to identify and synthesise available evidence. However, the scope of the literature search was limited, which may have introduced methodological constraints, including section bias, information bias, and the potential omission of relevant studies not captured through the selected search strategy. These limitations may influence the comprehensiveness and robustness of the conclusions drawn. Therefore, while the results provide meaningful insights into the neurophysiological and biochemical effects of meditation, they should be interpreted with caution, and future reviews should consider broader search strategies to enhance coverage and reduce potential bias.

5. Conclusion

Overall, the literature on meditation studies suggests that meditation may enhance neural efficiency and biochemical balance, potentially contributing to improved stress regulation and cognitive well-being. Future research should adopt standardised protocols, larger sample sizes, and multimodal assessments (EEG, neuroimaging, and biochemical measures) to address current inconsistencies better and improve the reproducibility of findings related to meditation.
