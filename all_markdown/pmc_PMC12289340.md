Abstract

Purpose:

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare oral naltrexone (NTX) and extended-release naltrexone (XR-NTX) for treating substance use disorders (SUDs).

Methods:

A comprehensive search of multiple databases (MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, APA PsycInfo, and Scopus) was conducted on October 1, 2024, with no date or language restrictions. Inclusion criteria were adults with SUDs, interventions with XR-NTX, comparisons with oral NTX, and outcomes on treatment persistence/relapse, hospitalizations, and ED visits. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies were included. Odds ratios were calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel random-effects model.

Results:

Of the 1,116 studies screened, 27 were selected for full-text review, 15 met the inclusion criteria (3 open-label RCTs [n=340] and 12 retrospective studies [n=18,695]) focusing on alcohol use disorder (AUD) and opioid use disorder (OUD), and 7 were included in the meta-analysis. We found no other SUD where the two interventions were compared. Treatment persistence was significantly higher with XR-NTX compared to oral NTX at both 3 months (OR 1.43 [95% CI: 1.04–1.96], p = 0.03) and 6 months (OR 1.96 [95% CI: 1.37–2.81], p = 0.0002). No significant differences were observed in healthcare utilization, including inpatient admissions (OR 0.69 [95% CI: 0.30–1.61], p = 0.39) or ED visits (OR 1.17 [95% CI: 0.61–2.26], p = 0.63).

Conclusions:

Patients with AUD and OUD receiving XR-NTX stay in treatment longer than those receiving oral NTX. However, hospitalization and ED visit rates did not differ. Findings are limited due to a small number of RCTs.

Keywords:
 Naltrexone, Oral naltrexone, Extended-Release injectable naltrexone, opioid use disorder, alcohol use disorder

1. Introduction

Substance use disorders (SUDs) represent a public health challenge with a significant impact on individuals, families, and communities. Individuals with SUDs often experience physical or mental health challenges, along with clinically significant functional impairment (
Volkow and Blanco, 2023
). In the United States (US) alone, the economic burden of SUDs is substantial, with costs associated with healthcare, criminal justice involvement, and lost productivity reaching hundreds of billions of dollars annually (
Sacks et al., 2015
). According to the 
2022 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH)
, 48.7 million people aged 12 and older (17.3%) had a SUD in the past 12 months (SAMHSA). Less than one-quarter (24.0% or 13.1 million individuals) of those aged 12 and older who identified as requiring substance use treatment in the past year actually received it (SAMHSA). As a result, a substantial number of individuals remain untreated.

Treatment options for SUDs include psychosocial interventions and pharmacological approaches (
Volkow and Blanco, 2023
). The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved several medications for SUD, including Disulfiram, Acamprosate, oral Naltrexone (NTX), and extended-release Naltrexone (XR-NTX) for alcohol use disorder (AUD) (
Fairbanks et al., 2020
), and methadone, buprenorphine, and XR-NTX for opioid use disorder (OUD) (
Lu et al., 2024
). NTX and XR-NTX have been investigated across a wide spectrum of SUDs, including studies on amphetamine, cocaine, cannabis, and tobacco use disorders (
Aboujaoude and Salame, 2016
; 
Haney et al., 2015
; 
Jayaram-Lindstrom et al., 2008
; 
King et al., 2012
; 
Modesto-Lowe and Van Kirk, 2002
; 
Runarsdottir et al., 2017
); however, they are not FDA-approved for any of these SUDs. Among the FDA-approved medications for SUDs, NTX stands out as a significant option due to its favorable efficacy, side effect profile, safety, and preliminary efficacy data across transdiagnostic SUDs.

NTX is a competitive opioid receptor antagonist with high affinity for the mu-opioid receptor and lower affinity for kappa and delta opioid receptors (
Weerts et al., 2008
). By competitively binding to mu-opioid receptors, naltrexone is believed to block the euphoric effects produced by opioids and alcohol (via endogenous opioid release) thereby, reducing cravings, and lowering the risk of relapse (
Bell and Strang, 2020
; 
Littleton and Zieglgänsberger, 2003
). The XR formulation of NTX offers the advantage of sustained release, typically administered once monthly, ensuring consistent therapeutic plasma levels and potentially improving treatment adherence (
Lee et al., 2016
). This makes it an attractive option for individuals with SUDs who struggle with medication adherence. In contrast, oral NTX provides similar therapeutic benefits but requires daily adherence, which may pose challenges for some patients. However, oral NTX is significantly cheaper than XR-NTX. Despite this distinction, both formulations have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing substance use and promoting abstinence across various substances, making them valuable components of comprehensive SUD treatment strategies (
Gastfriend, 2011
). Understanding the relative real-world benefit, safety profiles, and practical considerations of XR and oral NTX is essential for optimizing treatment outcomes and addressing the diverse needs of individuals with SUDs.

This comprehensive systematic review aimed to compare XR-NTX versus oral NTX in the management of any SUDs, with a focus on treatment persistance, health care utilization, safety profiles, and practical considerations in clinical practice.

2. Methods

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (
Page et al., 2021
). The study protocol was registered with the open science framework 
https://osf.io/x4pmk
.

2.1. Data Sources and Search Strategies

A comprehensive search of several databases was performed on October 1, 2024. No date or language limits were applied to the search. Databases searched (and their content coverage dates) were Ovid MEDLINE(R) (1946+ including epub ahead of print, in-process, and other non indexed citations), Ovid Embase (1974+), Ovid Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (1991+), Ovid Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (2005+), APA PsycInfo (1987+), and Scopus via Elsevier (1970+). The search strategies were designed and conducted by a medical librarian (L.H) with input from the study investigators. Controlled vocabulary supplemented with keywords was used. The actual strategies listing all search terms used and how they are combined is available in the 
supplemental material
.

2.2. Study Selection

Three reviewers (AE, OM, and AM) independently screened the titles and abstracts of studies meeting the inclusion criteria, ensuring that each study was assessed by at least two reviewers. Our search strategy aimed to capture comparative studies of oral and XR-NTX across all SUDs. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Population: adult (≥18 years) patients with a diagnosis of a SUD; (2) Intervention: XR-NTX; (3) Control: oral NTX; and (4) Outcome: reported data on treatment persistence, healthcare utilizations including inpatient hospitalizations or emergency department (ED) visits, and time to relapse. Studies were selected regardless of language. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (blinded or open-label) and observational studies, while excluding case series, case reports, and reviews, as well as studies with very small sample sizes (n &lt; 10). Relevant articles were documented in an online Excel spreadsheet and used for screening titles, abstracts, and full-text reviews. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved by consensus.

2.3. Data Collection

Data from the included studies were independently extracted by two reviewers (AE and OM) using a standardized data extraction form. We collected data on study characteristics (author, year, country, study design, sample size, and demographic characteristics of study participants), intervention settings, outcome measures, and follow-up details. The primary outcomes included metrics assessing persistence on treatment during the study duration, healthcare utilization rates, and adverse events. Persistence was measured using medication possession ratios (MPR), with high persistence defined as an MPR ≥80% (
Stewart et al., 2021
; 
Walker et al., 2019
), and outcomes were analyzed at 3-month and 6-month intervals. Secondary outcomes, such as rates of ED visits and inpatient admissions, were extracted where available. Additional data included participant characteristics and study-level variables.

2.4. Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were systematically assessed using established tools appropriate for the study designs. The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2.0) tool was employed for the primary outcomes of the RCTs (
Sterne et al., 2019
), and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) (
Wells G.A., 2000
) was utilized for the observational studies. For the RCTs, the RoB2 tool evaluated biases across five domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process, (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (3) bias due to missing outcome data, (4) bias in measurement of the outcome, and (5) bias in selection of the reported result. Each variable categorized as high risk, low risk, or some concern. For the observational studies, the NOS assessed the quality based on selection of the study groups, comparability of the groups, and ascertainment of the outcome. Each study was independently assessed by two reviewers (AS) and (AE) for the risk of bias assessment.

2.5. Statistical Analyses

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviations (SD). For the primary meta-analysis, data from RCTs and observational studies were combined. The effect sizes for categorical outcomes—high persistence, hospitalization, and ED visits—were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test, reported as Chi
2
 values with degrees of freedom (df), and quantified with the I
2
 statistic. Tau
2
 was calculated to estimate between-study variance. A random-effects model based on the DerSimonian and Laird method was used, with results displayed in forest plots. An I
2
 value &gt; 50% indicated significant heterogeneity. Pooled effects were evaluated using the Z-test, with p-values provided for statistical significance.

Sensitivity analyses were planned by excluding studies with a high risk of bias to assess the robustness of results. As only one RCT met the meta-analysis criteria, a sensitivity analysis was performed by removing the RCT data from the overall meta-analysis. Subgroup analyses stratified data between patients with AUD and OUD.

All analyses were conducted using RevMan version 5.4, with statistical significance set at P &lt; 0.05.

2

Excluding the RCT by Sullivan et al., findings comparing XR-NTX to oral NTX for persistence remained consistent at 3 and 6 months. At 3 months, the OR decreased slightly (1.43 to 1.39), the p-value shifted to 0.07, and I
2

2

3.3. Outcomes of the 3 open label trials

Busch et al. (
Busch et al., 2017
) conducted an open label pilot RCT to evaluate the feasibility of using oral NTX or XR-NTX for hospitalized veterans with AUD, focusing on treatment engagement and alcohol consumption post-discharge. Fifty-four veterans were randomized to receive either 50 mg oral NTX or a 380 mg XR-NTX injection before discharge, with follow-ups at 14 and 45 days. Thirty-five participants (64.8%) completed the study, with 77.8% attending all follow-ups. No significant differences were found between groups in adherence, engagement, or alcohol consumption (p&gt;0.05), but the median number of drinks significantly decreased from 128 pre-hospitalization to zero at follow-ups (p&lt;0.001) for both groups. Medication adherence was similar (62% oral, 61% XR).

Sullivan et al. (
Sullivan et al., 2019
) conducted an open-label RCT comparing XR-NTX and oral NTX outcomes in patients with OUD post-inpatient withdrawal. Sixty participants were randomized to XR-NTX (N=28) or oral NTX (N=32) for 24 weeks, with weekly behavioral therapy. XR-NTX showed significantly higher treatment retention at 6 months (57.1%) compared to oral NTX (28.1%) (HR = 2.18, p = 0.03). However, the median proportion of opioid-positive urine tests did not differ significantly (2.4% for XR-NTX vs. 8.8% for oral NTX; p=0.57).

Malone et al. (
Malone et al., 2019
) conducted an open-label RCT comparing XR-NTX and daily oral NTX for treating AUD in primary care. The primary outcome was achieving a “Good Clinical Outcome,” defined as abstinence or moderate drinking. The study enrolled 237 participants (XR-NTX: n = 117, oral NTX: n = 120), with significant baseline alcohol use (mean 10.3 drinks/day for XR-NTX, 9.1 for oral NTX). While a slightly higher percentage of the XR-NTX group achieved the “Good Clinical Outcome” (29% vs. 23%), the difference was not statistically significant.

3.4. Outcomes of the Retrospective studies

Mark et al. (
Mark et al., 2010
) compared healthcare utilization in patients with AUD treated with XR-NTX (n = 295) versus oral NTX (n = 2064). XR-NTX was associated with fewer inpatient detoxification days (224 vs. 552 per 1000 patients, p = 0.003) and lower detoxification costs (USD 0.60 million vs. USD 1.48 million, p&lt;0.01). While XR-NTX showed fewer alcoholism-related inpatient days (137 vs. 229 per 1000 patients) and lower related costs (USD 0.38 million vs. USD 0.64 million), these differences were not significant (p = 0.24). No significant differences were seen in alcoholism-related admissions (2.3% vs. 3.1%, p = 0.38), non-alcoholism-related inpatient days (869 vs. 589 per 1000 patients, p=0.33), or alcoholism-related ED visits (65 vs. 57 per 1000 patients, p = 0.71).

Baser et al. (
Baser et al., 2011a
) conducted a retrospective claims analysis comparing oral NTX and XR-NTX for alcohol dependence among 2391 oral NTX patients and 661 XR-NTX patients. Results showed that 21% of XR-NTX patients achieved a continuous MPR &gt; 0.8 versus 11% for oral NTX (p &lt; 0.0001). XR-NTX was associated with fewer alcohol-related (82 vs. 184 per 1000 patients, p &lt; 0.0001) and non-alcohol-related inpatient admissions (109 vs. 205 per 1000 patients, p &lt; 0.0001), as well as fewer detoxification and rehabilitation admissions (43 vs. 76 per 1000 patients, p = 0.0039). Although XR-NTX incurred higher medication costs, total healthcare costs were comparable ($6757 vs. $6595, p=0.64).

Baser et al.(
Baser et al., 2011b
) performed a retrospective claims analysis for opioid dependence among 845 oral NTX patients and 156 XR-NTX patients. They found that 21% of XR-NTX patients achieved a continuous MPR &gt; 0.8 compared to 8% of oral NTX patients (p &lt; 0.0001). XR-NTX was associated with fewer opioid-related (93 vs. 145 per 1000 patients, p = 0.005) and non-opioid-related inpatient admissions (234 vs. 387 per 1000 patients, p=0.027), although detoxification and rehabilitation admissions did not differ significantly (69 vs. 84 per 1000 patients, p=0.61). While XR-NTX led to higher medication costs, total healthcare costs were similar ($8582 vs. $8903, p=0.867), likely due to fewer hospitalizations, which resulted in comparable overall costs.

Bryson et al.(
Bryson et al., 2011
) conducted a cohort study of Aetna beneficiaries, comparing 211 patients on XR-NTX with 1,408 on oral NTX. They found a hazard ratio for treatment discontinuation of 0.79 (95% CI, 0.70–0.89; p &lt; 0.01) for XR-NTX versus oral NTX. Medication adherence at three months was 30% for XR-NTX versus 20% for oral NTX, and at six months, 15% versus 12%. The XR-NTX group had higher ED visits and hospitalization rates before treatment but showed a greater reduction in ED visits post-treatment (−0.23 vs. −0.16). Despite higher medication costs, XR-NTX’s lower non-pharmacy healthcare costs resulted in similar total costs between groups.

Harris et al. (
Harris et al., 2012
) conducted an analysis of oral NTX and XR-NTX utilization and persistence within the Veterans Health Administration. The analysis of time to first gap (TTG) included 8,560 patients initiating oral naltrexone and 356 patients initiating XR-NTX. Among patients initiating treatment, XR-NTX exhibited greater persistence, with 20.2% of patients maintaining continuous medication possession at 90 days compared to 17.9% for oral NTX; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.13). The mean proportion of days covered over 180 days was similar between XR-NTX (44.7%) and oral NTX (42.2%). Overall, the study did not find statistically significant differences in persistence between oral NTX and XR-NTX.

Crits-Christoph et al. (
Crits-Christoph et al., 2015
) conducted a retrospective analysis among criminal justice-involved individuals with AUD or OUD to compare XR-NTX and oral NTX outcomes. The study included 156 patients receiving XR-NTX and 45 receiving oral NTX. XR-NTX was associated with a significantly longer adjusted median duration of care (95 vs. 59 days, p = 0.025) and higher abstinence rates at discharge (OR = 4.51; 95% CI, 1.29–15.75; p = 0.018), with 67.2% abstinent compared to 35.3% for oral NTX. No statistically significant differences were observed in self-help participation, employment at discharge, or arrests within 30 days.

Crits-Christoph et al. (
Crits-Christoph et al., 2016
) compared XR-NTX and oral NTX in patients with AUD and OUD. For AUD (299 XR-NTX vs. 170 oral NTX), XR-NTX improved composite outcomes (abstinence, self-help participation, employment, arrests) significantly (Cohen’s d = 0.19; p = 0.048), though abstinence rates at discharge (80.4% vs. 76.5%; p = 0.675) and median treatment duration (91 vs. 83 days) showed no significant differences. For OUD (154 XR-NTX vs. 86 oral NTX), XR-NTX showed significantly better composite outcomes (Cohen’s d = 0.45; p = 0.006) and a nonsignificant trend for higher discharge abstinence rates (63.3% vs. 40.7%; p = .057). Median treatment durations (53 vs. 48 days) were also similar.

Beatty et al. (
Beatty and Stock, 2017
) compared healthcare utilization in 79 veterans with AUD treated with either oral NTX (n=65) or XR-NTX (n=14). The primary outcome was the 90-day alcohol-related hospital admissions per patient, with secondary outcomes at 30 and 180 days. Oral NTX recipients had fewer 30-day admissions (0.05 vs. 0.36, p &lt; 0.01) and fewer 6-month admissions (0.32 vs. 1.14, p &lt; .01). Although 90-day admissions were also lower in the oral NTX group (0.17 vs. 0.64, p = .06), this difference approached but did not reach statistical significance. The study concluded that oral NTX was linked to lower healthcare utilization, while acknowledging that patients on XR-NTX may have had more severe AUD.

Walker et al. (
Walker et al., 2019
) conducted a retrospective chart review of Veterans with AUD and found that among 592 subjects, 551 received oral NTX and 41 received XR-NTX. Mean adherence was 49.8% for oral NTX versus 54.6% for XR-NTX (p = 0.3). Additionally, 22.7% of oral NTX treatment courses reached 80% adherence compared to 24.4% of XR-NTX (p = 0.8). While there was no statistically significant difference, the authors suggest that adherence to oral NTX may have been overestimated, implying XR-NTX might be more reliably adhered to in practice.

Leighty et al. (
Leighty and Ansara, 2019
) conducted a retrospective chart review of 32 patients, 16 received oral NTX and 16 received XR-NTX. The median time to relapse was significantly longer for XR-NTX (150.5 days) compared to oral NTX (50.5 days; p &lt; .01). In contrast, the medication possession ratio (MPR) did not significantly differ between the two groups (p = 0.47).

Stewart et al. (2021)
 conducted a retrospective chart review comparing adherence rates between oral NTX and XR-NTX at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months using the portion of days covered (PDC) model. XR-NTX showed significantly higher adherence rates at all time points: 68.37% vs. 48.64% at 3 months (p = 0.006), 57.47% vs. 33.88% at 6 months (p = 0.003), 49.75% vs. 22.69% at 9 months (p = 0.003), and 45.07% vs. 19.43% at 12 months (p = 0.023). However, alcohol-related readmission rates at one year were not significantly different between the groups (p = 0.472).

Anderson et al. (
Anderson et al., 2021
) evaluated NTX use in the ED among 59 adult patients with moderate to severe AUD. Of these, 41 received oral NTX and 18 received XR-NTX. Overall, 15.3% of patients attended formal addiction treatment within 30 days after discharge. Those receiving XR-NTX had a higher follow-up rate (27.8%) compared to those on oral NTX (9.8%).

3.5. Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

The results presented in 
Figure 4

Sullivan et al., 2019
) achieved a more favorable risk of bias rating in the “Measurement of the outcome” domain because of its rigorous monitoring methods, including urine toxicology testing, standardized relapse criteria, and treatment monitors. In contrast, the Busch (
Busch et al., 2017
) and Malone (
Malone et al., 2019
) studies exhibited “some concerns” in this domain due to their reliance on self-reported adherence and less objective or consistent outcome monitoring methods. Consequently, the Sullivan study was rated as having an overall low risk of bias, whereas the Busch and Malone studies were rated as having “some concerns”. The observational studies generally performed well on the NOS as presented in 
Table 2
, reflecting strong selection methodologies and reliable outcome assessments. However, two studies were identified to have moderate risk of bias. This indicates that while most of the observational studies were methodologically sound, some may still harbor unaccounted-for biases that could impact their conclusions.

4. Discussion

4.1. Summary and Interpretation of Findings

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis compared oral NTX and XR-NTX in patients with SUDs. We found studies comparing the two interventions only in OUD and AUD. The findings indicate that patients with AUD and OUD receiving XR-NTX had greater treatment persistence at both 3- and 6-months post-initiation. However, inpatient hospitalization and ED visit rates were similar between the two groups. Detoxification admission rates and durations were qualitatively evaluated, revealing inconsistent evidence that does not conclusively favor either treatment group. These findings align with the meta-analysis by Hartung et al. (
Hartung et al., 2014
), which also found significantly improved persistence at six months but no consistent statistically significant differences in healthcare utilization, including inpatient admissions, ED visits or detoxification sessions.

Persistence is a critical metric in evaluating SUD treatments (
Klein and Seppala, 2019
; 
Kranzler et al., 2008
). Higher persistence is often associated with improved clinical outcomes, reduced healthcare costs, and fewer adverse events (
Liao et al., 2023
). Metrics like medication possession ratios, time to discontinuation, and adherence rates are commonly used to assess persistence. By enhancing adherence, XR-NTX may help optimize clinical outcomes and resource utilization, a critical consideration in SUD management. However, the current evidence, particularly from RCTs, is limited and does not demonstrate a clear reduction in hospitalization or ED rates.

Another important factor to consider between XR-NTX and oral NTX is cost; overall, the treatment expenses appear comparable (
Baser et al., 2011b
; 
Baser et al., 2011a
; 
Bryson et al., 2011
). This finding is particularly noteworthy given the significantly higher upfront cost of XR-NTX. Mark et al. (
Mark et al., 2010
) highlighted an even more compelling finding, demonstrating that, despite similar rates of healthcare utilization in certain categories, the shorter duration of each healthcare encounter for XR-NTX patients significantly offset its higher medication cost. This reduction in utilization duration ultimately resulted in an overall cost that was lower than that of oral NTX, despite the higher upfront cost of XR-NTX. These findings highlight the potential for XR-NTX to offer broader clinical and economic benefits that remain underexplored. Further robust studies, particularly RCTs, are needed to assess whether XR-NTX can demonstrate statistically significant advantages in reducing healthcare utilization, relapse rates, and the overall burden of disease while improving quality of life. Such evidence would provide valuable guidance for policymakers and healthcare providers in optimizing SUD management strategies and resource allocation. While we acknowledge the high value of RCTs in establishing comparative effectiveness, we also recognize that these trials can introduce conditions that might differ from routine clinical practice. For example, frequent follow-ups, consent procedures and enhanced compliance monitoring may favor highly motivated patients, potentially influencing adherence and limiting external validity. In contrast, observational studies and real-world clinical data offer meaningful insights into everyday practice (
De Sousa and De Sousa, 2004
; 
Magneberg, 1998
; 
Mark et al., 2010
). Future research should integrate the strengths of both RCTs and observational studies to ensure that findings are robust, clinically relevant and applicable to routine care. The FDA recently approved the first generic version of XR-NTX on July 6, 2023. This development is anticipated to further narrow the cost gap between the two formulations, potentially increasing interest and accessibility for the XR formulation (
FDA, 2023
). In real-world settings, patient preferences and financial factors, such as insurance coverage, may influence individual decisions regarding medication use.

Despite the consistently better persistence on treatment reported in the studies, Muthulingam et al. (
Muthulingam et al., 2023
) conducted a survey analysis that found injections and implants were less preferred by patients despite their convenience. This underscores the complexity of patient decision-making, where preferences are not solely driven by clinical convenience or outcomes. While healthcare professionals may prioritize metrics such as mortality or treatment adherence, these factors did not appear to significantly influence patient choices(
Muthulingam et al., 2023
). Understanding and addressing patient preferences, including their concerns about the invasiveness or long-term commitment associated with injectables is critical for optimizing patient acceptance, enhancing treatment adherence, and ultimately improving clinical outcomes. (
Martin et al., 2024
).

4.2. Limitations

We conducted a comprehensive systematic review but identified studies comparing oral NTX and XR-NTX only for AUD and OUD. While both have been studied in stimulant use disorder (amphetamine dependance) with varying results, they have not been directly compared in this population (
Jayaram-Lindstrom et al., 2008
; 
Runarsdottir et al., 2017
). Future studies are needed to investigate these two interventions in other SUDs. One clear limitation of our study is the relative lack of RCTs on this specific topic, which meant that we had to rely mostly on data from non-randomized studies. While observational studies provide valuable, real-world insights, especially regarding adherence-related outcomes, they cannot fully replace RCTs, which remain the gold standard for minimizing bias. For instance, receiving an XR-NTX injection in a clinical setting might naturally encourage better adherence compared to oral NTX, which relies more on individual discipline. Although this reliance on non-randomized data is not ideal, it reflects the current state of the evidence, and combining findings from different study designs allows us to build a more comprehensive understanding of these two interventions. The variability in the definitions and measurement methods for key outcomes, such as persistence, adherence, and relapse, significantly complicates cross-study comparisons and limits the ability to draw consistent and robust conclusions. A common limitation reported is that studies calculated adherence based on the availability of oral medication determined by refills, leading to a likely overestimation of adherence to oral medications in most studies, as medications are collected, but may remain unused. Another common limitation is that the XR-NTX sample size was significantly smaller than that of oral NTX in nearly all of the studies. In addition, patients considered appropriate for XR-NTX may have more severe AUD and be more prone to relapse, which is why they were trialed on XR-NTX initially (
Beatty and Stock, 2017
). Some studies did not report or did not sufficiently account for the severity of the SUD between the two groups of patients and characteristics such as years of problematic abuse, psychiatric and medical co-morbidities and number of previous treatments. These variables may have influenced treatment outcomes and therefore have a potential to be a confounding factor in our results.

Many of the included studies also did not report on the abstinence status of the patients prior to beginning NTX treatment, affecting the findings of the included papers. Furthermore, most of the included studies did not report on adverse effects experienced by the oral NTX and XR-NTX groups. Only two out of the 15 studies (
Malone et al., 2019
; 
Sullivan et al., 2019
). It was not always clear whether the reported effects were related to the drug administered. Additionally, due to the relatively small sample sizes, no robust conclusions can be drawn regarding adverse events based on the studies included in our systematic review. Although many studies evaluating either oral NTX or XR-NTX individually include safety data, it is particularly important for studies directly comparing both formulations to report adverse events. Since such studies involve comparable patient populations, they offer a more reliable basis for assessing differences in side effect profiles. The randomized studies included were limited by the lack of double blinding, which is a challenging issue to address in study design due to the inherent differences in formulation types and other ethical considerations, and they faced significant challenges in recruiting a sufficient number of participants. A significantly greater number of studies have focused on AUD compared to OUD, highlighting the need for further research to better explore the effect of the different formulations on OUD.

5. Conclusion

This systematic review showed higher treatment persistence with XR-NTX for SUD (AUD and OUD) compared to oral NTX. However, healthcare utilization rates, including hospitalizations and ED visits, showed no significant differences. This review is limited by the small number of available RCTs. Additional RCTs are needed to directly assess differences in clinical outcomes, including abstinence rates, reductions in heavy drinking days or substance use and relapse prevention, to provide patients and physicians with more comprehensive evidence for informed decision-making.
