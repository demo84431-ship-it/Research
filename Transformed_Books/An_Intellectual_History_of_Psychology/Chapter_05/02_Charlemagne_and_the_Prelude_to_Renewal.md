# Module 02 — Charlemagne and the Prelude to Renewal

## Chapter 5: Scholastic Psychology — The Authority of Aristotle

---

Picture the year 800 AD. A man kneels in St. Peter's Basilica in Rome. He is tall — unusually tall for his era, well over six feet — with a thick mustache and the bearing of someone who has spent more of his life on horseback than on a throne. Pope Leo III places a crown on his head and declares him Emperor of the Romans. The man rises. He is Charles, king of the Franks, and from this moment he will be called Carolus Magnus — Charlemagne, Charles the Great.

What happened in that basilica was not merely a religious ceremony. It was a calculated political move by a leader who understood something most of his contemporaries did not: the future would be built on institutions, not just armies. The coronation symbolized the rebirth of the Western world, organized around principles shared by both temporal and spiritual authority. It created at least the possibility of European unification — not against a foreign army, but against chaos itself. And against that chaos, Charlemagne would spend the next fourteen years building something that would outlast his empire by centuries: a system of education.

---

## A King Who Built Schools

Charlemagne served as king of the Franks for thirty-two years (768–800) and, until his death in 814, as Emperor of the Holy Roman Empire. You have probably heard of the **Holy Roman Empire** — the political entity that claimed to revive the unity and glory of ancient Rome under Christian auspices. What made Charlemagne's version unusual was not the imperial title itself (other rulers had claimed it) but what he did with the power it gave him.

He was devoted to learning. This is not a polite biographical footnote. It was a deliberate policy choice at a time when literacy was rare, books were hand-copied treasures, and most European rulers cared more about land than about letters. Charlemagne imported Alcuin — a brilliant English scholar from the cathedral school at York — to establish a **palace school** at Aachen. The palace school was exactly what it sounds like: a school attached to the royal court, where the king's children, promising young clergy, and even Charlemagne himself were taught.

Alcuin's curriculum was built around the **Trivium** — the three foundational liberal arts of grammar, rhetoric, and logic — along with the four mathematical disciplines of the Quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy). Together, these seven liberal arts formed the intellectual backbone of medieval education for the next five hundred years. They were not arbitrary. Grammar gave you the tools to read and write. Rhetoric taught you to argue persuasively. Logic — derived largely from Aristotle — taught you to think rigorously. The Trivium was, in essence, a training program for the mind.

Charlemagne did not stop at his own court. He issued decrees encouraging bishops to establish schools within churches and abbeys across his empire. These ecclesiastical schools, and the curriculum Alcuin introduced, were the direct ancestors of the medieval university — an institution that would emerge in the twelfth century and transform European intellectual life permanently.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Consider the difference between a ruler who builds palaces and one who builds schools. What does Charlemagne's investment in education reveal about his understanding of power? Could a leader today make a similar choice — and would the results take as long to appear?

## Restoring a Civilization

Robinson describes Charlemagne's achievement in striking terms: he "restored respectability to learning, cutting a seam in the heavy curtain that had darkened European scholarship for four hundred years." That curtain — the centuries of political fragmentation, economic collapse, and cultural contraction following Rome's fall — had not eliminated knowledge, but it had made knowledge seem irrelevant to survival. When your city might be sacked next year, Latin grammar feels like a luxury.

Charlemagne changed that calculation. He restored stability to the economy. He provided safety across a vast territory — from the Ebro River in Spain to southern Denmark. He planted Christianity's banner across Europe, not just as a spiritual mission but as a unifying political framework. And in doing so, he created what Robinson calls "a climate congenial to their antagonist: reason."

This is a subtle but critical point. Charlemagne did not eliminate fear or magic — he legislated against both, but legislation alone cannot change the way people think. What he did was create the conditions under which reason could compete. Schools need safety. Scholars need patronage. Libraries need walls. The palace school and the network of ecclesiastical institutions Charlemagne promoted provided all three.

To the extent that the modern Western world retains Greco-Roman institutions — its legal systems, its educational structures, its philosophical vocabulary — Charlemagne can be said to have been a maker of the modern Western world. Not because he invented anything new, but because he preserved and propagated what had nearly been lost.

## The Twelfth-Century Renaissance

Here is something that may surprise you: there was a Renaissance before the Renaissance. The twelfth century produced an intellectual revival that, "in purely intellectual respects, compared favorably with its more vaunted successor" — the famous Italian Renaissance of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

The most visible signs of this revival are the Gothic cathedrals: Paris, Chartres, Amiens, Laon, Beauvais, Rheims, LeMans, Tours, Orleans, Mont Saint-Michel, Canterbury, Oxford, Prague, Cologne — nearly all appearing in less than two hundred years. These were not just buildings. They were statements of confidence, engineered with mathematical precision, designed to flood interior spaces with light. They represented a civilization that believed, once again, that human reason could reach toward the divine.

But the cathedrals were only the most dramatic expression of something deeper. The twelfth century saw:

- A revival of classical Latin as a language of serious scholarship
- A resumption of philosophical inquiry that went beyond mere commentary on scripture
- And most significantly, the passage of Arabic translations of Aristotle's works from Saracen Spain into Christian Europe

This last development — the transmission of Aristotle through Arabic intermediaries — would reshape Western thought for the next three hundred years. It deserves its own story.

---

## Avicenna and the Return of Aristotle

The major figure in this intellectual transmission was **Avicenna** (980–1037), known in the Islamic world as Ibn Sina. Avicenna was a Persian polymath — a physician, philosopher, and logician whose works circulated throughout the Islamic world long before they reached Christian Europe. His Islamic rendition of Aristotelian thought reacquainted the Western philosophical community with a far wider range of Aristotle's work than had previously been available.

When Avicenna's commentaries arrived in Europe, they posed urgent questions for thirteenth-century Christian theologians. Aristotle's metaphysical arguments for the eternity of the world directly contradicted the Book of Genesis, which described a definite moment of creation. Aristotle's ethical system was built on virtue and habit, with no concept of sin — the central concern of Christian moral theology. These were not minor disagreements. They were fundamental conflicts between two of the most powerful intellectual systems ever constructed, and resolving them would consume the energies of Europe's greatest minds for over a century.

But Avicenna was not merely a conduit for Aristotle. He was an original thinker who anticipated psychological questions that would not be seriously addressed again until the seventeenth century.

---

## The Flying Man

Imagine this: you are suspended in empty space. You are blindfolded. You cannot see, hear, touch, taste, or smell anything. All your senses are completely blocked from external stimuli. You have no awareness of your own body — its position, its weight, its limbs. You are, in effect, a disembodied point of consciousness floating in a void.

Now the question: do you still exist?

Avicenna said yes. He argued that even in this extreme scenario — stripped of every sensory input, every bodily sensation — you retain a vivid awareness of yourself. You know that you exist. This is not derived from your senses, because your senses have been eliminated. It is not derived from your body, because you have no bodily awareness. It is derived from the very nature of being a rational, conscious entity.

This is Avicenna's famous **"flying man"** thought experiment. It is remarkably similar to René Descartes' *cogito ergo sum* — "I think, therefore I am" — which would not appear until 1637, more than six hundred years later. Descartes' version is better known, but Avicenna's argument arrived first, and it arrived through a different philosophical tradition.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Try the flying man experiment yourself. Close your eyes. Block your ears. Try to imagine having no sensation of your body at all — no feeling of your hands, no sense of gravity, nothing. Do you still feel that "you" exist? Where does that feeling come from, if not from your body or your senses?

Avicenna went further. In the Sixth Treatise of his *Deliverance*, he argued that the basis of all rational concepts must be immaterial. Here is his reasoning: your senses respond to particular things — *this* tree, *that* tree. But your rational faculty does something your senses cannot do. It abstracts the intelligible form of "tree" and forms a universal concept — the idea of *tree* as such, not any particular tree. This universal concept cannot be a material thing, because abstractions cannot be materially represented. They cannot be physical objects that enter into causal relations with your physical sense organs.

If the rational faculty operates with immaterial concepts, then the rational faculty itself must be immaterial. And if the rational faculty — the soul — is immaterial, then it is not subject to the decay and dissolution that governs material things. The soul is immortal.

This was a powerful argument, and it would echo through centuries of Western philosophy. The concept of **universals** — general ideas like "tree," "justice," "beauty" — became one of the most hotly debated questions in medieval thought. Are universals real things that exist independently of particular objects? Or are they just names we attach to groups of similar things? This debate, which you will encounter in Module 03, shaped the entire trajectory of Western epistemology.

## From Avicenna to the Philosopher

The return of Aristotle did not happen overnight. It was "slowly and haltingly but then quickly and continuously." The authority of Aristotle challenged and overtook the neo-Platonism that had dominated Christian thought since Augustine.

The progression went something like this:

- **St. Anselm** (1033–1109) initiated the philosophic revival. His major work, *Faith Seeking Understanding*, argued that perception and reason had legitimate roles in the Christian life. But his intellectual authority was Augustine, not Aristotle.
- **Peter Lombard's** *Four Books of Sentences* proclaimed reason's position in faith. Still Augustine.
- **Peter Abailard** (1079–1142) made the decisive shift. He placed the greatest emphasis on Aristotle, calling him **"our prince."** For the next two centuries, Aristotle would be known simply as **"the Philosopher"** — the thinker whose authority was so vast that he needed no other name.

The revival cannot be reduced to a single cause. Charlemagne's contribution was sustained by King Otto's German mini-renaissance in the tenth century. Rome was secure enough to serve as a center for grammar and law. By 1085, the Christian reconquest of Spain had reached Toledo; by 1118, Saragossa. The First Crusade (1096) was a mere expression of this revival, not an antecedent to it. By the end of the twelfth century, Europe resembled Athens under Alexander: war and discord giving way to order, security, and growing prosperity.

The stage was set. Aristotle's works — preserved, translated, and transmitted by Islamic scholars — were now available to Christian Europe. The question was what Christian Europe would do with them. The answer would define the next three centuries of intellectual history, and it would produce some of the most sophisticated psychological thinking the Western world had yet seen.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** The knowledge that transformed medieval Europe arrived through a different civilization — Arabic-speaking scholars who had preserved and extended Greek thought. What does this tell you about the relationship between cultural exchange and intellectual progress? Can you think of a modern parallel, where ideas from one tradition have revitalized another?

## A Bridge to What Comes Next

You now have the infrastructure: a king who built schools, a curriculum that trained minds in logic and rhetoric, a network of ecclesiastical institutions that would grow into universities, and — through the mediation of Islamic scholars — the recovered works of Aristotle, the thinker who would dominate European thought for the next three hundred years.

But having Aristotle's texts and understanding them are two very different things. The medieval thinkers who wrestled with Aristotle's ideas faced a problem that was not merely intellectual but theological: how do you reconcile a pagan philosopher's account of the soul, the world, and the good life with the teachings of the Christian Church? The answers they produced — and the fierce debates those answers provoked — are the subject of the modules ahead.
