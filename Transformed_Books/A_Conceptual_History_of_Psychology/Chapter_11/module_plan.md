## Module Plan (5 Modules)

### Module 1: Logical Positivism and the Neobehaviorist Dream
**Source lines:** 1–400 | **Source words:** ~4,000 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Watson's positivism vs. neobehaviorism — inductivism vs. hypothetico-deductive | Opening |
| 2 | Logical positivism — Vienna Circle, verification principle, formal vs. factual propositions | Logical positivism |
| 3 | Operationism — Bridgman, operational definition, "created a Frankenstein" | Operationism |
| 4 | Intervening variables — logical vs. causal, instrumentalist vs. realist | Neobehaviorist theory |
| 5 | Scientific empiricism — correspondence rules, physicalism, Hempel | Scientific empiricism |

**Narrative arc:** Watson's behaviorism is too simple → neobehaviorists want real theories → they adopt logical positivism from the Vienna Circle → the verification principle: meaning = method of verification → operationism: concepts must be linked to measurements → intervening variables bridge stimuli and responses → but are they just logical devices or real causes?

**Key terms to define:** logical positivism, verification principle, operationism, intervening variable, hypothetical construct, scientific empiricism

**Research anchors:**
- Koch, S. (1964). "Psychology and the Human Enterprise" — critique of logical positivism's influence
- Smith, L. D. (1986). *Behaviorism and Logical Positivism* — on the relationship between behaviorism and logical positivism

### Module 2: Tolman and Hull — Two Visions of Scientific Psychology
**Source lines:** 400–851 | **Source words:** ~4,500 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Tolman — purposive behaviorism, cognitive maps, sign-Gestalt, MIT to Berkeley | Tolman |
| 2 | Tolman's intervening variables — mental processes as causes, hypothetical constructs | Tolman theory |
| 3 | Hull — Newtonian system, drive reduction, habit strength, mathematical laws | Hull |
| 4 | Hull's system — postulates and theorems, deduction of predictions, reaction potential | Hull theory |
| 5 | Tolman vs. Hull — cognition vs. drive, purposive vs. mechanistic | Comparison |

**Narrative arc:** Tolman reads James and Koffka → he develops purposive behaviorism: behavior is goal-directed → he treats cognitive maps as real causes → Hull reads Newton and Watson → he builds a mathematical system of learning → drive reduction, habit strength, reaction potential → the two behaviorists represent two visions: one cognitive, one mechanistic.

**Key terms to define:** purposive behaviorism, cognitive maps, sign-Gestalt, intervening variables, hypothetico-deductive system, drive reduction, habit strength, reaction potential

**Research anchors:**
- Tolman, E. C. (1948). "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men" — foundational paper
- Hull, C. L. (1943). *Principles of Behavior* — Hull's system

### Module 3: Skinner's Box — Radical Behaviorism
**Source lines:** 852–1111 | **Source words:** ~2,500 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Skinner biography — failed writer, Watson/Pavlov inspiration, Harvard | Skinner |
| 2 | Operant conditioning — emitted vs. elicited behavior, Skinner box, reinforcement | Skinner theory |
| 3 | Schedules of reinforcement — fixed/variable interval/ratio, shaping | Skinner methods |

**Narrative arc:** Skinner fails at writing → reads Watson and Pavlov → develops operant conditioning → the Skinner box → schedules of reinforcement → shaping behavior through progressive approximation → he rejects all theories about internal states as "explanatory fictions" → he tries to explain language as operant behavior → Chomsky will demolish this.

### Module 4: The Walls Close In — Problems of Behaviorism
**Source lines:** 1112–1583 | **Source words:** ~7,500 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | WWII and professionalization — APA reorganization, clinical psychology, postwar expansion | WWII |
| 2 | Chomsky's critique — language, poverty of stimulus, innate grammar, species difference | Chomsky |
| 3 | Brelands' instinctual drift — raccoon "washing," biological constraints on learning | Brelands |
| 4 | Garcia's taste aversion — single-trial learning, long delay, violation of contiguity | Garcia |
| 5 | Lashley's serial ordering — central cognitive processes, hierarchically organized control | Lashley |

**Narrative arc:** WWII transforms psychology → APA reorganizes with 18 divisions → but behaviorism faces mounting problems → Chomsky demolishes Skinner's account of language → the Brelands show that instinctual drift overrides conditioning → Garcia shows that taste aversion violates contiguity and frequency → Lashley argues that complex behavior requires central cognitive control → the behaviorist edifice cracks.

**Key terms to define:** instinctual drift, conditioned taste aversion, poverty of the stimulus, serial ordering, central cognitive processes

**Research anchors:**
- Chomsky, N. (1959). Review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* — the most famous critique in psychology
- Breland, K., & Breland, M. (1961). "The Misbehavior of Organisms" — on instinctual drift

### Module 5: The Eve of Revolution — From Behaviorism to Cognition
**Source lines:** 1584–1601 + synthesis | **Source words:** ~300 + synthesis | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Hixon Symposium (1948) — Lashley, von Neumann, McCulloch, brain as computer | Hixon |
| 2 | Rescorla-Wagner — cognitive conditioning, anticipated vs. actual reinforcement | Rescorla |
| 3 | Consciousness and conditioning — Greenspoon effect, Dulany's awareness requirement | Consciousness |
| 4 | Dartmouth Conference (1950) — neobehaviorist theories condemned as inconsistent | Dartmouth |
| 5 | MIT Symposium (1956) — cognitive revolution launched, Miller, Bruner, Newell & Simon | MIT |

**Narrative arc:** At the 1948 Hixon Symposium, Lashley argues that complex behavior requires central cognitive control → von Neumann draws parallels between brain and computer → Rescorla and Wagner show that conditioning is cognitive, not automatic → consciousness may be necessary for some conditioning → at the 1950 Dartmouth Conference, neobehaviorist theories are condemned → at the 1956 MIT Symposium, the cognitive revolution is launched → behaviorism is not dead, but it is no longer alone.

**Key terms to define:** Hixon Symposium, cognitive conditioning, awareness in conditioning, cognitive revolution

**Research anchors:**
- Rescorla, R. A. (1988). "Pavlovian Conditioning: It's Not What You Think It Is" — redefining conditioning
- Miller, G. A. (1956). "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" — cognitive revolution landmark
