## Module Plan (5 Modules)

### Module 1: The Social Animal — European Roots of Social Psychology
**Source lines:** 1–300 | **Source words:** ~3,000 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Holism vs. individualism — Durkheim (social minds), Hobbes (individual pursuit of pleasure) | Opening |
| 2 | Völkerpsychologie — Humboldt, Lazarus, Wundt (language, myth, custom as social products) | European origins |
| 3 | Crowd psychology — Le Bon (suggestibility, irrationality), Tarde (imitation), social contagion | Le Bon, Tarde |
| 4 | Durkheim vs. Weber — social facts vs. social action, imitation vs. social orientation | Durkheim, Weber |
| 5 | Early American social psychology — Ross (social influence), McDougall (instincts), Triplett (competition) | American origins |

**Narrative arc:** Aristotle says humans are social animals → Enlightenment individualism challenges this → European theorists (Humboldt, Durkheim, Weber) recognize social dimensions of psychology → Le Bon and Tarde study crowd behavior → Durkheim distinguishes social from individual psychology → American psychologists (Ross, McDougall) establish social psychology as a discipline.

**Key terms to define:** Völkerpsychologie, crowd psychology, social facts, social action, social influence, instinct

**Research anchors:**
- Le Bon, G. (1895/1896). *The Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind* — foundational crowd psychology
- Durkheim, É. (1895/1982). *The Rules of Sociological Method* — social facts as the subject matter of sociology

### Module 2: The Laboratory of the Social — Experimental Social Psychology
**Source lines:** 300–600 | **Source words:** ~3,000 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | James's social selves — multiple selves, different roles, social identity | James |
| 2 | McDougall vs. Ross — instincts vs. social influence, nature vs. nurture in social behavior | McDougall, Ross |
| 3 | Triplett's experiment (1898) — competition and performance, first social psychology experiment | Triplett |
| 4 | Allport's experimental social psychology — social facilitation, group effects, laboratory methods | Allport |
| 5 | Sherif's autokinetic effect — social norms, conformity, the creation of group norms | Sherif |

**Narrative arc:** James describes the multiple social selves → McDougall and Ross debate whether social behavior is instinctive or learned → Triplett runs the first social psychology experiment (competition in cycling) → Allport establishes experimental social psychology in the laboratory → Sherif demonstrates that social norms are created through group interaction.

**Key terms to define:** social self, social facilitation, autokinetic effect, social norm, conformity, experimental social psychology

**Research anchors:**
- Triplett, N. (1898). The dynamogenic factors in pacemaking and competition. *American Journal of Psychology*, *9*(4), 507–533.
- Sherif, M. (1936). *The Psychology of Social Norms* — foundational study of social norms

### Module 3: Conformity, Obedience, and Attitudes — The Classic Experiments
**Source lines:** 600–923 | **Source words:** ~3,000 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Asch's conformity experiments — line judgment, majority influence, dissent | Asch |
| 2 | Milgram's obedience experiments — authority, electric shocks, situational power | Milgram |
| 3 | Attitude measurement — Thurstone (equal-appearing intervals), Likert (summated ratings) | Thurstone, Likert |
| 4 | Group dynamics — Lewin (field theory, group decision, leadership styles) | Lewin |
| 5 | Cognitive dissonance — Festinger (1957), inconsistency, attitude change, forced compliance | Festinger |

**Narrative arc:** Sherif shows that people conform to group norms → Asch demonstrates conformity even when the group is clearly wrong → Milgram shows that ordinary people will obey authority even when it causes harm → Thurstone and Likert develop methods for measuring attitudes → Lewin studies group dynamics and leadership → Festinger proposes that inconsistency between beliefs and behavior drives attitude change.

**Key terms to define:** conformity, obedience, attitude, cognitive dissonance, group dynamics, field theory

**Research anchors:**
- Asch, S. E. (1951). Effects of group pressure upon the modification and distortion of judgments. In H. Guetzkow (Ed.), *Groups, Leadership, and Men*.
- Milgram, S. (1963). Behavioral study of obedience. *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*, *67*(4), 371–378.
- Festinger, L. (1957). *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*.

### Module 4: Growing Minds — The Origins of Developmental Psychology
**Source lines:** 924–1100 | **Source words:** ~2,000 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Preformationism vs. epigenesis — is the mind preformed or does it develop? | Philosophical origins |
| 2 | Hall — adolescence, recapitulation theory, genetic psychology, child study movement | Hall |
| 3 | Watson — behaviorist child development, conditioning infants, "Little Albert" | Watson |
| 4 | Gesell — maturation, developmental norms, systematic observation | Gesell |
| 5 | Piaget — cognitive development, stages (sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete, formal), assimilation and accommodation | Piaget |

**Narrative arc:** Philosophers debate whether the mind is preformed or develops → Hall establishes developmental psychology through the child study movement → Watson applies behaviorism to child development → Gesell studies maturation through systematic observation → Piaget develops the most influential theory of cognitive development, arguing that children actively construct their understanding of the world through stages.

**Key terms to define:** preformationism, epigenesis, recapitulation, maturation, cognitive development, assimilation, accommodation, stage theory

**Research anchors:**
- Piaget, J. (1952). *The Origins of Intelligence in Children*. New York: International Universities Press.
- Hall, G. S. (1904). *Adolescence: Its Psychology and Its Relations to Physiology, Anthropology, Sociology, Sex, Crime, Religion, and Education*.

### Module 5: Attachment, Culture, and the Developing Self
**Source lines:** 1100–1190 + synthesis | **Source words:** ~1,500 + synthesis | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Vygotsky — zone of proximal development, social constructivism, scaffolding, private speech | Vygotsky |
| 2 | Bowlby — attachment theory, maternal deprivation, secure base, internal working model | Bowlby |
| 3 | Erikson — psychosocial development, eight stages, identity crisis, generativity vs. stagnation | Erikson |
| 4 | Cross-cultural development — Bronfenbrenner (ecological systems), cultural variation in parenting | Bronfenbrenner |
| 5 | Open questions — nature vs. nurture, continuity vs. stages, universal vs. culturally specific development | Synthesis |

**Narrative arc:** Vygotsky argues that cognitive development is fundamentally social → Bowlby shows that early attachment relationships shape lifelong development → Erikson extends development across the entire lifespan → Bronfenbrenner shows that development is embedded in ecological systems → the question of whether development is universal or culturally specific remains open.

**Key terms to define:** zone of proximal development, scaffolding, attachment, secure base, psychosocial development, ecological systems

**Research anchors:**
- Bowlby, J. (1969). *Attachment and Loss, Vol. 1: Attachment*. New York: Basic Books.
- Vygotsky, L. S. (1934/1986). *Thought and Language*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.
