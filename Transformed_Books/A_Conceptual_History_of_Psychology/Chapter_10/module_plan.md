## Module Plan (5 Modules)

### Module 1: The Functional Revolution — Psychology Learns to Be Useful
**Source lines:** 1–580 | **Source words:** ~8,800 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Turn to applied psychology — PhD oversupply, public demand, teaching overload, western universities | Opening |
| 2 | Angell — province of functional psychology, utilities of consciousness, adaptive behavior | Angell |
| 3 | Carr — functional psychology as American psychology, adaptive behavior, Völkerpsychologie | Carr |
| 4 | Thorndike — trial-and-error learning, puzzle boxes, law of effect, connectionism | Thorndike |
| 5 | Woodworth — S-O-R model, stimulus-organism-response, rejection of schools | Woodworth |

**Narrative arc:** PhDs flood the market → psychologists turn to applied work to survive → Angell defines functional psychology: what the mind *does*, not what it *is* → Carr says functional psychology *is* American psychology → Thorndike puts cats in puzzle boxes and discovers the law of effect → Woodworth proposes S-O-R: between stimulus and response, there is an organism.

**Key terms to define:** functional psychology, adaptive behavior, law of effect, law of exercise, connectionism, S-O-R model

**Research anchors:**
- Dewey, J. (1896). "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology" — foundational functionalist paper
- Joncich, G. (1968). *The Sane Positivist: A Biography of Edward L. Thorndike*

### Module 2: Watson's Manifesto — The Birth of Behaviorism
**Source lines:** 580–1200 | **Source words:** ~9,500 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Early behaviorism — new realists (Holt, Perry), Meyer, Weiss, McDougall | Background |
| 2 | Watson — "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" (1913), three reasons against introspection | Watson manifesto |
| 3 | Cognition as motor response — inner speech, laryngeal thought, Watson's prediction falsified | Watson theory |
| 4 | Pavlov — conditioned reflexes, extinction, spontaneous recovery, experimental neurosis | Pavlov |
| 5 | Little Albert — fear conditioning, ethical concerns, Watson's environmentalism | Watson experiments |

**Narrative arc:** The conceptual foundations of behaviorism are laid by philosophers → Watson publishes his manifesto in 1913: study behavior, not consciousness → his three reasons: animal psychology doesn't need consciousness, introspection has failed, applied psychology doesn't need it → he identifies thought with laryngeal movement → Pavlov shows how reflexes can be conditioned → Watson and Rayner condition fear in Little Albert → behaviorism becomes a movement.

**Key terms to define:** behaviorism, conditioned reflex, unconditioned reflex, conditioned stimulus, unconditioned stimulus, extinction, Little Albert

**Research anchors:**
- Watson, J. B. (1913). "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" — the behaviorist manifesto
- Harris, B. (1979). "Whatever Happened to Little Albert?" — on the ethics of the experiment

### Module 3: Thorndike's Cats, Pavlov's Dogs — The Science of Learning
**Source lines:** 1200–1500 | **Source words:** ~5,000 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Thorndike's puzzle boxes — cats escape, incremental learning, no insight | Thorndike |
| 2 | Law of effect — behaviors stamped in/out by consequences, satisfaction/discomfort | Thorndike |
| 3 | Pavlov's conditioned reflexes — metronome (not bell), extinction, spontaneous recovery | Pavlov |
| 4 | Bechterev — motor reflexes, objective psychology, association of movements | Bechterev |
| 5 | Classical vs. instrumental conditioning — Konorski & Miller, two distinct forms of learning | Learning types |

**Narrative arc:** Thorndike puts cats in puzzle boxes → they learn through trial and error, not insight → successful responses are "stamped in" by satisfaction → Pavlov pairs metronomes with food and discovers conditioned salivation → he also discovers extinction, spontaneous recovery, and experimental neurosis → Bechterev develops an objective psychology of motor reflexes → Konorski and Miller distinguish two types of learning.

**Key terms to define:** trial-and-error learning, law of effect, law of exercise, connectionism, conditioned reflex, extinction, spontaneous recovery, classical conditioning, instrumental conditioning

**Research anchors:**
- Thorndike, E. L. (1911). *Animal Intelligence* — foundational learning theory
- Todes, D. P. (2014). *Ivan Pavlov: A Russian Life in Science* — definitive biography

### Module 4: Neobehaviorism — Tolman, Hull, and the Mental Return
**Source lines:** 1500–1800 | **Source words:** ~5,000 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Tolman — purposive behaviorism, cognitive maps, intervening variables | Tolman |
| 2 | Tolman's cognitive maps — rats learn spatial layouts, place vs. response learning | Tolman |
| 3 | Hull — hypothetico-deductive system, drive reduction, habit strength | Hull |
| 4 | Hull's system — mathematical learning theory, primary/secondary drives, reinforcement | Hull |
| 5 | Watson vs. Tolman vs. Hull — three visions of behaviorism compared | Comparison |

**Narrative arc:** Watson says no mentalistic concepts → Tolman disagrees: you can study behavior AND use mentalistic explanations → he shows rats learn cognitive maps, not just stimulus-response bonds → Hull builds a mathematical system of learning based on drive reduction → the three behaviorisms represent three different visions of what psychology should be.

**Key terms to define:** purposive behaviorism, cognitive maps, intervening variables, hypothetico-deductive system, drive reduction, habit strength

**Research anchors:**
- Tolman, E. C. (1948). "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men" — foundational cognitive behaviorism
- Hull, C. L. (1943). *Principles of Behavior* — Hull's system

### Module 5: The Measure of Intelligence — Testing, Immigration, and Sterilization
**Source lines:** 1800–2307 | **Source words:** ~8,400 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Binet-Simon test — mental age, practical identification of struggling students | Binet |
| 2 | Goddard — Kallikak study, feeblemindedness as inherited, Ellis Island testing | Goddard |
| 3 | Terman — Stanford-Binet, IQ, "gifted" children, hereditarian assumptions | Terman |
| 4 | Army Testing Project — group tests, Yerkes, 1.7 million recruits tested, public impact | Army testing |
| 5 | Immigration quotas & sterilization — National Origins Act, Buck v. Bell, Brigham's recantation | Eugenics |

**Narrative arc:** Binet designs a test to identify struggling students in Paris → Goddard imports it to America and turns it into a tool for identifying the "feebleminded" → Terman creates the Stanford-Binet and normalizes IQ testing → the Army tests 1.7 million recruits in World War I → the results are used to justify immigration quotas and forced sterilization → Brigham later recants his racist conclusions → the Nazis take eugenics to its logical extreme.

**Key terms to define:** mental age, intelligence quotient (IQ), feeblemindedness, Army Testing Project, National Origins Act, eugenics, sterilization

**Research anchors:**
- Gould, S. J. (1981). *The Mismeasure of Man* — critique of intelligence testing
- Zenderland, L. (1998). *Measuring Minds: Henry Herbert Goddard and the Origins of American Intelligence Testing*
