# The Way of Ideas — Locke, Berkeley, and the Problem of the External World
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

You are sitting in a garden in Oxford, England, in the summer of 1688. A friend reaches into a basket and pulls out a cherry. She holds it up. "What do you see?" she asks.

You describe it: red, round, glossy, about the size of a marble. She asks you to close your eyes. You do. She places the cherry in your hand. "Now what do you feel?" You describe the sensation: smooth, cool, slightly soft. She asks you to bite it. You do. The juice floods your mouth — sweet, sharp, faintly tart.

"Very well," she says. "You've described what you saw, what you felt, and what you tasted. But answer me this: which of those is the cherry *itself*? Is the redness the cherry? Is the roundness? Is the tartness? Take away any one of them — take away the color, take away the taste — and is it still a cherry?"

You open your eyes and look at the cherry again. It seems obvious that the cherry is the thing you are holding. But your friend has just shown you that every single thing you can say about the cherry is something you *experienced*, not something you *know* about the cherry as it exists in the world, independent of your senses. And that raises a question that will haunt psychology for the next three centuries: if everything you know comes through your senses, how do you know that the world as you experience it is anything like the world as it actually is?

John Locke thought he had the answer. A young Irish bishop named George Berkeley was about to show that he didn't.

## The World in Two Halves

Locke was not a man who liked to speculate. He had watched the English Civil War tear his country apart, had been exiled to Holland for backing the wrong political side, and had spent decades studying medicine, philosophy, and natural science alongside his friend Isaac Newton. When he finally published his masterwork, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, in 1690, he made his intentions plain: he wanted to clear away the "rubbish" that blocked the path to genuine knowledge. The rubbish, in his view, was the belief that human beings are born with ideas already installed in their minds.

Everything we know, Locke argued, comes from experience. That was his central claim. But he needed to explain *how* experience builds up into the rich, complex world you navigate every day. His answer drew directly on the Newtonian model of physics: just as all material objects are composed of tiny, indivisible corpuscles, all knowledge is composed of **simple ideas** — the basic atoms of thought. These simple ideas are "perfectly distinct" from each other: the idea of red is not the idea of sweet, which is not the idea of smooth. From these building blocks, your mind assembles **complex ideas** — ideas of apples, of friends, of justice — through operations like comparison, combination, and abstraction.

So far, so Newtonian. But Locke was about to draw a distinction that would split the perceived world into two halves — and create a problem that would take 200 years to even begin to solve.

### Primary Qualities: The Real World

Look at the cherry on the table. Now imagine stripping away everything that depends on *you* — your eyes, your tongue, your skin. What remains? According to Locke, what remains are the **primary qualities**: shape, size, motion, number, and solidity. These are the properties that belong to the cherry itself, whether or not anyone is there to perceive them.

Locke was emphatic about this. He wrote that "the particular bulk, number, figure, and motion of the parts of fire, or snow, are really in them, whether any one's senses perceive them or no." A table has a rectangular shape whether you are looking at it or not. A stone has a certain mass whether you are holding it or not. Primary qualities are the furniture of the objective world — the world as Newton's physics described it.

### Secondary Qualities: The World as You Experience It

But what about the cherry's redness? Its sweetness? Its coolness? These, Locke argued, are **secondary qualities** — and they work differently. The redness is not *in* the cherry the way its shape is. Instead, the cherry has the *power* to produce the sensation of redness in you, thanks to the arrangement of its tiny corpuscles. The sweetness is not *in* the cherry; the cherry has the power to produce the sensation of sweetness on your tongue.

Locke compared secondary qualities to sickness. Manna — a kind of bread — can make one person feel sick and another feel fine. The sickness is not in the manna; it is an effect produced in the person. Likewise, the redness is not in the cherry; it is an effect produced in your mind by the cherry's interaction with your eyes. Secondary qualities, Locke said, are "nothing in the objects themselves but powers to produce various sensations in us."

This was a bold move. It meant that the world you experience — the colorful, fragrant, flavorful world of everyday life — is, in a sense, a *product* of your mind's interaction with a much plainer, more mathematical reality. The real world is all shape and motion. The world you live in is a painted overlay, generated by your senses.

### The Resemblance Problem

Locke believed he could still give you reason to trust your experience. Your ideas of primary qualities — your mental picture of the cherry's roundness — actually *resemble* the cherry's real shape. They are, as Locke put it, "resemblances" of the properties out there in the world. Your idea of the cherry's shape is like a picture of the cherry's shape. It may not be perfect, but it is a faithful copy.

But here is where things get uncomfortable. How do you *check* whether your idea of roundness resembles the cherry's actual roundness? To make that comparison, you would need to access the cherry's shape *independently* of your idea of it — to see the cherry as it is, apart from your perception of it. And that is precisely what you cannot do. Every time you look at the cherry, you are having *another idea*. You are comparing one idea with another idea. You never get outside the circle of your own experience to compare your idea with the thing itself.

Locke himself seemed to sense the trouble. He admitted that the underlying *substance* — the mysterious "something" that holds the cherry's properties together — is something "we have no clear distinct idea at all." He had opened a door, and someone was about to walk through it.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Pick up any object near you — a pen, a phone, a book. You can see its shape and color. But can you distinguish which of those qualities belongs to the object *itself* and which is produced by your senses? Try to find a single quality you can access without going through your own perception. What does that mean for your confidence about the "real" world?

## Looking Inward: The Invention of Introspection

Locke did not only have a theory about how you know the external world. He had a theory about how you know your own mind — and it was, for its time, just as radical.

Before Locke, people did not generally think of their mental lives as something you could *inspect*. The word **consciousness** existed, but it originally meant "shared knowledge" — the Latin *con-* ("with") plus *scio* ("I know"). It was a social concept, not a psychological one. The idea that your mind is something you can look *into* — that you have an inner sense that monitors your own thoughts and feelings — was a 17th-century invention. The English phrase "be conscious of" first appears in 1620. The noun "consciousness" in its modern, inner-experience sense dates from 1678. "Self-consciousness" shows up in 1690, the very year Locke published his *Essay*. The term "introspection" — from the Latin *intro* ("within") plus *specio* ("I look") — appeared in France and England toward the end of the same century.

Locke treated consciousness as a form of **introspection** — an inner perception, a kind of mental eye that watches what is happening inside you. And he made an extraordinary claim: this inner perception is *infallible*. You cannot be wrong about what you are currently experiencing. When you see, hear, smell, taste, feel, meditate, or will anything, you *know* that you do. "It being impossible for anyone to perceive, without perceiving, that he does perceive," Locke wrote.

His justification was, frankly, weak by modern standards. He argued that it is "hard to conceive, that any thing should think, and not be conscious of it," and that "to be happy or miserable without being conscious of it, seems to me utterly inconsistent and impossible." But it is not hard to conceive of at all. You do dozens of things every day — driving a familiar route, typing on a keyboard, brushing your teeth — without being consciously aware of the fine-grained details of your mental activity. Later psychologists would build entire theories around the existence of **unconscious mental processes**, challenging Locke's claim head-on.

Yet Locke's picture of the mind as transparent to itself — of consciousness as a spotlight that illuminates every mental event — proved enormously influential. It remained the default assumption of psychologists and philosophers well into the 20th century. Introspection became the primary method of early experimental psychology. If the mind is transparent, all you need to do to understand it is look inward and report what you find. It took decades for researchers to discover that this method had serious limits.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Try to introspect right now. Close your eyes for ten seconds and notice what is happening in your mind. Did you catch *everything*? Did you notice the background hum of your body, the faint train of associations running beneath your main thought, the emotions you were not deliberately attending to? If you missed some of these, what does that tell you about whether consciousness is really a perfect mirror of your mental life?

## The Association of Ideas: How Accidental Connections Shape Your Life

Locke was an optimist about human nature. He believed that most of the cruelty, superstition, and irrationality he saw around him was the product of bad upbringing, not bad character. Give a child a good education, he argued, and you would produce a rational, virtuous adult. It was a view that would later find echoes in the behaviorism of John B. Watson and B. F. Skinner, who similarly believed that environment, not inheritance, shapes the person.

But Locke was honest enough to notice that education sometimes goes wrong — and he put his finger on a mechanism that explains why. In a passage that would prove prophetic for psychology, he distinguished between two kinds of connections between ideas. Some ideas, he said, have a "natural correspondence and connection one with another" — reason traces these connections and holds them together. But there is another kind of connection, one that is "wholly owing to chance or custom." Ideas that have no natural relationship get linked together simply because they happen to occur at the same time or in the same place. Once linked, they are "very hard to separate."

He gave an example that will resonate with anyone who has watched a child struggle in school: "Many children, imputing the pain they endured at school to their books they were corrected for, so join these ideas together, that a book becomes their aversion, and they are never reconciled to the study and use of them all their lives after." The child does not *decide* to hate books. The association forms automatically, through the accident of co-occurrence. And it persists, shaping behavior for decades.

### Vain Terrors and the Frog

Locke saw these accidental associations as the source of many "vain terrors" — irrational fears that grip people without reason. A child hears ghost stories in the dark, and the darkness becomes permanently linked with terror. An adult develops a phobia of a perfectly harmless animal after a single frightening encounter. The fear is not rational, but it is not random either. It is the product of an **association of ideas** — a mental connection forged by contiguity rather than by logic.

And here Locke made a move that would prove remarkably prescient. He described a method for *unlearning* these fears — a method that anticipated, by more than 250 years, a technique that would become one of the most effective treatments in modern psychotherapy.

Imagine your child, Locke wrote, shrieking and running away at the sight of a frog. Do not force the child to touch the frog. Instead, start at a distance. Let another person catch the frog and place it far away. Accustom the child first to *look* at the frog, then to see it leap, without emotion. Then let the child touch it lightly while someone else holds it. Gradually, step by step, bring the child closer, "till he can come to handle it as confidently as a butterfly, or a sparrow."

The key, Locke insisted, is patience: "care be taken, that you go not too fast, and push not the child on to a new degree of assurance, till he be thoroughly confirm'd in the former."

In 1958, the South African psychiatrist Joseph Wolpe formalized this exact technique and called it **systematic desensitization**. The principle is identical: expose the patient to the feared stimulus in gradually increasing intensity, while ensuring they remain relaxed at each stage. A 2019 review published in *Frontiers in Psychiatry* confirmed that exposure-based therapies remain among the most effective treatments for anxiety disorders. Locke had described the logic of the treatment 260 years before Wolpe gave it a name.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Think of something you feel uneasy about — maybe a food you refuse to eat, a place you avoid, or a situation that makes you anxious. Can you trace the feeling back to a specific experience? Did the unease form through a logical process, or did it attach itself to something through the accident of timing or context? If you wanted to weaken the association, what would Locke's method suggest?

## Berkeley's Bomb: An Idea Can Be Like Nothing but an Idea

In 1709, a 24-year-old Irish clergyman named George Berkeley published a slim book on vision. The next year, he published a philosophical treatise that would shake the foundations of everything Locke had built. Berkeley had read Locke carefully. He agreed with almost all of Locke's premises. And then he used those premises to demolish Locke's conclusions.

The argument was devastating in its simplicity. Locke had said that our ideas of primary qualities *resemble* the real properties of objects out there in the world. Your idea of a table's rectangular shape is a mental picture that corresponds to the table's actual shape. Berkeley's response was blunt: "An idea can be like nothing but an idea."

Think about what resemblance requires. For one thing to resemble another, you need to compare them. You hold up your photograph and look at your face in the mirror. You compare the painting with the landscape it depicts. But to compare your *idea* of the table's shape with the table's *actual* shape, you would need to access the table's shape independently of your idea of it. You would need to step outside your own experience. And that, Locke had already agreed, you cannot do. Every time you perceive the table, you are having another idea. You are stuck inside the circle.

Berkeley drove the point home. If you cannot compare your ideas with the things they supposedly represent, you have no reason to believe that *any* of your ideas — not just of secondary qualities, but of primary qualities too — resemble anything outside your mind. The distinction between primary and secondary qualities collapses. Shape is no more "in" the object than color is. Both are ideas. Both exist only in the perceiving mind.

### The Cherry Unravels

Berkeley illustrated his argument with an example that cuts to the heart of the matter. Consider a cherry. You see its redness, feel its softness, taste its tartness. Now, Berkeley asked, what is the cherry *apart from* these sensations?

"Take away the sensations of softness, moisture, redness, tartness, and you take away the cherry," he wrote. The cherry is not some mysterious substance *behind* its appearances, holding them together like an invisible frame. The cherry *is* its appearances. It is "nothing but a congeries of sensible impressions, or ideas perceived by various senses." The mind unites them into a single thing — gives them one name, "cherry" — because they are "observed to attend each other." But there is no hidden entity beneath the bundle.

This view is called **idealism** — the doctrine that only minds and their ideas exist. There is no **material substance**, no inert stuff "out there" that causes your perceptions. The cherry is a bundle of sensations. The table is a bundle of sensations. The entire physical world is a structured pattern of ideas, held together not by material reality but by the regularity with which certain ideas appear together in experience.

Was Berkeley crazy? He did not think so. He pointed out that he was simply following Locke's own principles to their logical conclusion. If all knowledge comes from experience, and experience consists of ideas, then all you ever have access to is ideas. You cannot leap from ideas to a material world behind them. The very concept of material substance — the Newtonian substratum of corpuscles and forces — is, Berkeley argued, *meaningless*, because no one can form a sense impression of it. You can see the cherry. You can taste it. You cannot perceive "material substance." So what does the word even refer to?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Berkeley argued that a cherry is just a bundle of sensations — redness, softness, tartness — held together by the habit of experiencing them together. Try this: imagine a cherry without its color. Now without its taste. Now without its texture. What is left? Is there anything remaining that you can point to as "the cherry itself"? Or does the cherry dissolve when you remove its perceptible qualities?

## The Mystery of Distance: What the Eyes Cannot Tell You

Berkeley did not only attack Locke's theory of knowledge. He also tackled a problem in visual perception that would occupy psychologists for the next two centuries: how do you perceive *distance*?

The question seems trivial until you think about it. When you look at a tree across a field, you "see" it as being far away. But what exactly are your eyes detecting? Distance, Berkeley pointed out, is a line extending outward from your eye to the object. It projects onto your retina as a single point — the same point, whether the object is one meter away or one hundred meters away. Your eyes, considered purely as optical instruments, cannot distinguish near from far. The image on your retina is flat. Yet you experience the world in depth.

The standard answer, championed by Descartes and his followers, was that the brain performs an unconscious geometric calculation. When you look at an object, your two eyes converge at slightly different angles. The brain, they argued, computes the distance from the angle of convergence — using the same trigonometry you learned (or didn't learn) in school.

Berkeley rejected this outright. His objection was simple and devastating: nobody *experiences* these angles or calculations. "I appeal to anyone's experience," he wrote, "whether upon sight of an object he computes its distance by the bigness of the angle made by the meeting of the two optic axes?" The answer, obviously, is no. You do not feel your eyes converging. You do not sense the angle of a triangle formed by imaginary lines. You just *see* the tree as far away. If the process is unconscious and unobservable, Berkeley argued, then positing it is not an explanation — it is a fiction.

### Distance as Learned Association

So how *do* you perceive distance? Berkeley's answer was that you do not perceive it directly at all. You *learn* to estimate it through experience. As a baby, you reach for objects and discover that some require more movement to touch than others. Over time, you learn that certain visual cues — objects appearing smaller, looking fainter, requiring a broader focus of the eyes — correlate with the amount of bodily effort needed to reach them. Distance perception, Berkeley argued, is not a direct sensory experience. It is an inference, built on the association between visual impressions and tactile impressions accumulated over a lifetime of reaching, walking, and touching.

"Looking at an object I perceive a certain visible figure and color, with some degree of faintness and other circumstances," Berkeley wrote, "which from what I have formerly observed, determine me to think, that if I advance forward so many paces or miles, I shall be affected with such and such ideas of touch."

This was a radical claim. It meant that the rich, three-dimensional world you experience is not something your eyes *give* you. It is something your mind *constructs* — assembling depth from fragments of visual and tactile experience, stitching together a coherent world from raw sensory data that, on its own, is flat and featureless. Modern neuroscience has largely confirmed this: your brain does not passively receive the world. It actively builds a model of it, using past experience to fill in what the senses leave out.

### The Conceptual Gap

But Berkeley's account, brilliant as it was, contained a problem that he never fully resolved — and it is a problem that strikes at the heart of all empiricist psychology.

If distance cannot be seen, and cannot be touched either (you do not *feel* distance the way you feel pressure or temperature), then where does your *concept* of distance come from? Berkeley said you learn to associate visual cues with the tactile experience of moving through space. But that association already *presupposes* that you have the concept of distance — that you understand what it means for one thing to be farther away than another. The association does not *create* the concept. It *uses* the concept.

Think of it this way: a correlation between visual faintness and the effort of reaching only tells you "these two things go together." It does not, by itself, tell you "this object is 10 meters away." To make that judgment, you need the *idea* of distance as a measurable extension in space. And that idea, Berkeley's own principles suggest, cannot be assembled from visual and tactile impressions alone.

This gap in Berkeley's reasoning pointed toward a conclusion that neither Locke nor Berkeley was willing to accept: that some concepts may be **innate** — built into the mind before experience begins. The Scottish philosopher Thomas Reid and the German philosopher Immanuel Kant would later argue exactly this: that concepts like distance, shape, substance, and causality are part of our mental endowment, not products of sensory association. The mind, they suggested, comes equipped with structures that organize experience. Without those structures, experience itself would be impossible.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Berkeley argued that you learn to perceive distance through the association of visual and tactile experience. But to form that association, you already need to *have* the concept of distance. Where did that concept come from? Can you build a concept entirely from sensory impressions, or does the mind need to bring something *to* the experience in order to make sense of it? What does this imply about the limits of empiricism?

---

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Locke opened the door to the "way of ideas" — the view that all knowledge passes through the filter of our own mental representations. Berkeley walked through that door and discovered something unsettling: once you are inside the circle of ideas, you cannot get out. You cannot compare your ideas with reality, because every attempt to do so produces more ideas. The cherry dissolves into a bundle of sensations. Distance dissolves into a learned inference. The material world dissolves into a pattern of perceptions held together by habit and God's benevolence. And yet — and this is the twist that will drive the next module — if the empiricists are right that all ideas come from experience, where do the concepts *come from* that allow us to make sense of experience in the first place? That question will push empiricism to its breaking point.

Berkeley's legacy extends well beyond 18th-century philosophy. In *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979), the psychologist James J. Gibson argued that Berkeley was right about one thing — perception is not a passive reception of sense-data — but wrong about another. Gibson claimed that the environment itself contains structured information (what he called "affordances") that the perceiver picks up directly, without needing to construct mental representations. Gibson's direct realism was, in effect, a modern answer to Berkeley's idealism: you do not need to build a cherry out of sensations, because the cherry is already there, offering itself to perception.

## Speaking of...

**The phone that isn't there.** You reach for your pocket and feel a phantom vibration — your phone buzzing, even though it is sitting on the table across the room. Your brain has formed an association between the sensation of your leg and the expectation of a notification. The vibration is not in your leg. It is not in the phone. It is in the association — a mental connection forged by repetition, just like the ones Locke described.

**The acquired taste.** A colleague in São Paulo tells you she hated *feijoada* as a child — the heavy black bean stew was too rich, too salty, too much. Now she craves it. The dish has not changed. Her sensory receptors have not changed. What changed is the *association*: the taste became linked with family gatherings, with Saturday afternoons, with comfort. The flavor itself is a bundle of sensations. The enjoyment is the bundle plus meaning — an accidental connection that became natural through repetition.

**The cured phobia.** A friend in Seoul developed a severe fear of elevators after being trapped in one during an earthquake at age twelve. A therapist helped her using exactly the method Locke described: first looking at an elevator from a distance, then standing near one with the doors open, then stepping in for a few seconds, then riding one floor. Each step was small. Each step waited until the previous one felt safe. The fear did not vanish because the elevator changed. It weakened because the association between "elevator" and "terror" was gradually replaced by a new association: "elevator" and "nothing bad happened."

---

Locke gave psychology its first systematic account of how the mind builds knowledge from experience. Berkeley showed that this account, taken seriously, leads to a world made entirely of ideas — and to the unsettling conclusion that you can never check whether those ideas match anything outside your mind. But both Locke and Berkeley still believed that the mind works in a relatively orderly way: ideas come in, get combined, and produce knowledge. They believed in the power of reason to trace natural connections between ideas.

David Hume was about to take a hammer to that belief. If all knowledge comes from experience, he asked, then where does your idea of *causation* come from? You have never *seen* one event cause another. You have only seen one event follow another — again and again, until your mind forms a habit of expectation. Causation, Hume argued, is not a law of nature you discover. It is a feeling you develop. And if that is true, then the entire edifice of scientific knowledge — including Newton's laws — rests not on reason, but on habit. The implications were so alarming that Hume himself called them "philosophical melancholy and delirium."
