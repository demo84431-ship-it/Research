## Module Plan (5 Modules)

### Module 1: Newton's Shadow — How Physics Colonized Psychology
**Source lines:** 1–250 | **Source words:** ~5,300 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | The Enlightenment — confidence in reason, progress, and the applicability of science to human affairs | Opening |
| 2 | Newton's paradigm — universality, atomism, determinism, explanatory reduction as implicit criteria for science | Newtonian Science |
| 3 | How psychologists adopted Newton's features as requirements — universal theories, invariant human nature, mechanistic laws | Newtonian Science |
| 4 | Locke as Newton's "under-labourer" — the biographical context (Shaftesbury, exile, Boyle) | Locke biography |
| 5 | Psychological and meaning empiricism — all ideas from experience, words derive meaning from ideas of experience | Locke's empiricism |

**Narrative arc:** The Enlightenment's optimism → Newton becomes the gold standard for all science → psychologists treat Newton's features as checkboxes → Locke befriends Newton and volunteers to "clear the rubbish" → every idea comes from experience, every meaningful word points to an experience.

**Key terms to define:** Enlightenment, universality, atomism, determinism, explanatory reduction, psychological empiricism, meaning empiricism, simple ideas, complex ideas

### Module 2: The Way of Ideas — Locke, Berkeley, and the Problem of the External World
**Source lines:** 251–680 | **Source words:** ~6,800 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Primary and secondary qualities — shape/size/color/taste, ideas as pictures, the resemblance problem | Locke's qualities |
| 2 | Consciousness as inner perception — mental states are transparent, introspection as the method | Locke's consciousness |
| 3 | The association of ideas — natural vs accidental connections, vain terrors, the frog desensitization | Locke's association |
| 4 | Berkeley's idealism — "an idea can be like nothing but an idea," cherry = bundle of sensations, rejection of material substance | Berkeley's idealism |
| 5 | Berkeley on distance perception — not seen but inferred, tactile-visual association, the innate concepts objection | Berkeley's distance |

**Narrative arc:** Locke splits the world into primary and secondary qualities → says we can know both → Berkeley demolishes this: you can never compare your idea with the thing-in-itself → a cherry is just a bundle of sensations → even distance isn't directly seen, it's learned → but wait, where does the concept of distance come from in the first place?

**Key terms to define:** primary qualities, secondary qualities, introspection, association of ideas, idealism, material substance, constant conjunction, innate concepts

### Module 3: The Skeptic's Toolkit — Hume and the Machinery of the Mind
**Source lines:** 681–1150 | **Source words:** ~10,000 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Hume's impressions and ideas — faint images, psychological atomism, meaning empiricism ("from what impression?") | Hume's foundations |
| 2 | Hume's Fork — relations of ideas vs matters of fact, "commit it then to the flames" | Hume's epistemology |
| 3 | Causality as constant conjunction — no impression of power, necessary connection as internal feeling, two definitions of causality | Hume's causality |
| 4 | The empiricist conception of causal explanation — science as description of correlation, Molière's "dormative virtue," critique of this view | Hume's influence |
| 5 | Hartley's neurological associationism — vibrations and vibratiuncles, classical/instrumental conditioning anticipations, habit formation | Hartley's neurology |

**Narrative arc:** Hume pushes empiricism to its logical limit → "from what impression is that idea derived?" → causality isn't observed, it's felt → science just describes correlations (Molière laughs) → but practicing scientists say causality is real, grounded in mechanisms → Hartley tries to ground association in the brain itself → vibrations become vibratiuncles → habits form through neural fusion → the blueprint for behaviorism is drawn.

**Key terms to define:** impressions, ideas, Hume's Fork, constant conjunction, necessary connection, empiricist conception of science, vibrations, vibratiuncles, synchronous association, successive association, classical conditioning, instrumental conditioning

### Module 4: Revolutions of the Mind — French Sensationalism and the Critical Responses
**Source lines:** 1151–1600 | **Source words:** ~9,600 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Condillac's statue — one sense modality, sensation transformed, mental faculties as products of pleasure/pain | French sensationalism |
| 2 | Helvetius and the idéologues — radical environmentalism, education as the key to progress, Cabanis's guillotine experiments | French applied psychology |
| 3 | Thomas Reid and common sense — direct realism, innate faculties, critique of the "way of ideas" | Scottish realism |
| 4 | Leibniz — petites perceptions, apperception (active organization), innate ideas as veins in marble | Rationalist reaction |
| 5 | Kant — categories, synthetic a priori knowledge, concepts as schemas not images, "thoughts without content are empty" | Kantian critique |

**Narrative arc:** Condillac imagines a statue with only smell → all mental faculties emerge from sensation → Helvetius says you can engineer genius through education → the French Revolution embraces this → Reid says nonsense: you perceive the world directly, not through ideas → Leibniz says some perceptions are too faint for consciousness → Kant delivers the masterstroke: the mind actively structures experience, and some knowledge is built-in, not learned.

**Key terms to define:** sensationalism, sensation transformed, idéologues, direct realism, innate faculties, common sense, petites perceptions, apperception, categories, synthetic a priori, transcendental idealism, schemas

### Module 5: The Road Not Taken — Vico, Romanticism, and the Question of a Human Science
**Source lines:** 1601–1750 | **Source words:** ~3,200 | **Concepts:** 5

| # | Concept | Source |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | Vico's New Science — we know our own creations better than nature, cultural and historical variation in psychology | Vico's human science |
| 2 | The Naturwissenschaften vs Geisteswissenschaften distinction — causal explanation (erklären) vs interpretive understanding (verstehen) | Dilthey's distinction |
| 3 | Rousseau and the noble savage — civilization corrupts, natural education in Émile | Romanticism |
| 4 | Goethe, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche — limits of reason, the irrational, creativity, self-transformation | Romanticism |
| 5 | Hegel's dialectic — the social mind, progress through contradiction, the road to Marx | Hegel + toward science |

**Narrative arc:** Vico says something radical: we understand ourselves better than we understand nature → Dilthey later formalizes this: natural sciences explain, human sciences understand → Rousseau says civilization corrupts the noble savage → Goethe says some things transcend science → Nietzsche hymns the irrational → Hegel sees history as a dialectical march toward freedom → but his "social mind" concept has dark consequences → and yet, despite all these objections, scientific psychology arrives anyway.

**Key terms to define:** New Science, cultural variation, erklären, verstehen, noble savage, dialectic, social mind, romanticism
