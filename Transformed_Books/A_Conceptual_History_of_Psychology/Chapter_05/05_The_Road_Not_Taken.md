# Chapter 05 · Module 5
# The Road Not Taken — Vico, Romanticism, and the Question of a Human Science
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In the summer of 1725, a broke, half-blind Neapolitan professor published a book that almost nobody read. Giambattista Vico had lost his bid for a better chair at the University of Naples. His teeth were falling out. His children kept dying. He was, by any reasonable measure, a failure. And yet the book he produced — *The New Science* — contained an idea so radical that psychology is still trying to absorb it three centuries later.

Vico's claim was deceptively simple: we understand ourselves better than we understand nature. When Newton described gravity, he was explaining something he could observe but never fully comprehend — the inner workings of a universe he did not create. But when you explain why you lied to a friend, or why a particular song makes you cry, or why a family in Kyoto grieves differently from a family in Lagos, you are explaining something you *made*. Human culture, human psychology, human institutions — these are our creations. And a creator, Vico argued, always knows its creations better than an outsider can know the natural world.

This was a direct challenge to everything the Newtonian program stood for. The Enlightenment said: apply the methods of physics to human beings, and you will discover universal laws. Vico said: you are looking in the wrong direction. The laws of human psychology are not like the laws of gravity. They change across cultures. They shift across centuries. And the only way to understand them is from the inside — through empathy, imagination, and historical understanding, not through measurement and mechanism.

It was a road not taken. Scientific psychology chose Newton over Vico. But the questions Vico raised never went away. They came back, again and again, in different guises — in Romanticism, in philosophy, in the social sciences — and they are still unresolved today. What kind of science can you make out of something that keeps changing?

## The Man Who Said We Know Ourselves Better

Giambattista Vico (1668–1744) spent his entire career at the University of Naples, teaching rhetoric — a subject considered far less important than philosophy or natural science. He was passed over for promotion repeatedly. His books sold poorly. He died in obscurity. Yet his *New Science* (1725, revised 1730 and 1744) anticipated arguments that would not become mainstream for another two hundred years.

Vico started from a principle he borrowed from Aristotle: the governing principles of any entity are best known to its creator. God created nature, so only God can fully understand nature. Humans created culture — language, law, religion, customs, art — so humans can understand culture from the inside. We have direct knowledge of our own psychological states through introspection and empathetic insight into others. We have only indirect knowledge of the material world through our theoretical models of it.

This sounds abstract, but the implication is concrete. When a psychologist in London designs an experiment on decision-making, she is studying a phenomenon that she herself participates in. She knows what it feels like to choose, to hesitate, to regret. When a physicist studies quarks, he has no such insider knowledge. The psychologist's subject matter is, in principle, more accessible to her than the physicist's subject matter is to the physicist.

Vico also rejected the Newtonian assumption that human nature is universal and invariant. He argued that the fundamental principles of human psychology are developed products of culture and history — and just as diverse. People in ancient Rome did not think like people in 18th-century Naples. People in China did not feel like people in Europe. This created a problem: how can you understand the psychology of someone whose entire "web of belief" is different from yours? Vico suggested you could make some progress by focusing on what appear to be the few cultural and historical invariants of human life — birth, sex, and death — and working outward from there.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Consider how differently people in different cultures experience grief. In some traditions, mourning is loud and public. In others, it is quiet and private. In some, the dead are celebrated. In others, they are feared. If the basic emotion of loss is universal, why does it look so different from place to place? What would Vico say?

## Explaining vs. Understanding

Vico's ideas were largely ignored in his own century. But they found echoes in later thinkers. Johann Gottfried Herder (1744–1803) restated Vico's basic principles in his *Ideas Towards a Philosophy of History* (1784–1791). Kant addressed cultural variation in his *Anthropology From a Pragmatic Point of View* (1797). John Stuart Mill argued that associationist psychology needed a supplementary "science of character" — an ethology — that could account for individual and cultural differences. Even Wundt, the founder of experimental psychology, devoted the second half of his career to *Völkerpsychologie* (1900–1920) — the psychology of peoples — which he treated as a necessary complement to laboratory science.

But the most influential formalization of Vico's distinction came from the German philosopher Wilhelm Dilthey (1833–1911). Dilthey argued that there is a fundamental difference between the natural sciences and the human sciences — not just in subject matter, but in method and purpose.

The natural sciences (*Naturwissenschaften*) aim at causal explanation — what Dilthey called **erklären**. When a physicist explains why a ball falls, she identifies the cause (gravity) and predicts the effect (acceleration at 9.8 m/s²). The explanation is impersonal. It does not matter who the physicist is, where she lives, or what she believes. The ball falls the same way for everyone.

The human sciences (*Geisteswissenschaften*) aim at interpretive understanding — what Dilthey called **verstehen**. When a historian explains why the French Revolution happened, or when a psychologist explains why a teenager in Seoul feels alienated, she is not just identifying causes. She is interpreting meanings. She is trying to understand what the events *meant* to the people involved. This kind of understanding is not impersonal. It requires empathy, imagination, and cultural knowledge. It requires getting inside someone else's head.

The distinction between **erklären** and **verstehen** became foundational for the philosophy of social science. It raised a question that has never been fully answered: can psychology be both? Can it explain human behavior in terms of causes *and* understand it in terms of meanings? Or are these two fundamentally different enterprises that cannot be combined?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Imagine you are trying to understand why a friend quit a high-paying job to become a music teacher. You could explain it in terms of causes — childhood experiences, personality traits, brain chemistry. You could also understand it in terms of meaning — what the decision *meant* to your friend, what values it expressed, what story they were telling about their life. Are these the same explanation? Are they compatible? Which one tells you more?

## The Noble Savage and the Corruption of Civilization

While Vico was challenging the universality of Newtonian science from within the academic tradition, a very different kind of challenge was emerging from the salons of Paris. Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) did not argue that human psychology varies across cultures. He argued that civilization itself is the problem.

In 1749, the Dijon Academy announced an essay competition on the question of whether the revival of the sciences and arts had improved morals. Rousseau entered and won — with the stunning argument that they had not. Human beings in their natural state, he claimed, are "noble savages" — naturally good, spontaneous, uncorrupted by the artificial demands of civilized life. It is civilization that makes us miserable. Reason, science, social hierarchy, private property — these are not progress. They are disease.

Rousseau developed this argument in *The Social Contract* (1762) and, more practically, in *Émile* (1762) — a treatise on education that reads like a novel. The ideal education, Rousseau argued, is one in which the child's natural inclinations are not suppressed but nurtured. Do not force the child to memorize facts. Do not train the child to sit still and obey. Instead, follow the child's natural curiosity. Let experience be the teacher. Let nature take its course.

This was not just a theory of education. It was a theory of human nature. Rousseau believed that the spontaneous emotional life of the child is more authentic — more real, more valuable — than the rational, controlled, artificial life demanded by society. He commended the spontaneity of emotion over the sterility of reason. He was, in the language of the time, a Romantic.

The implications for psychology were profound. If civilization corrupts, then the Newtonian project — applying rational science to human behavior — is not just misguided. It is part of the disease. The more you analyze, the more you distort. The more you systematize, the more you lose.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Rousseau argued that a child left to explore freely would develop into a healthier, happier adult than one subjected to rigorous formal education. Modern educational psychology has produced evidence on both sides. Think about your own education — were the moments of greatest learning the ones that were most structured, or the ones that were least?

## Beyond Reason: Goethe, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche

Rousseau was not alone. The Romantic movement — a broad cultural shift that began in the late 18th century and ran through the 19th — shared his suspicion of reason and science. But the Romantics were not anti-intellectual. They were after something bigger. They believed that certain aspects of human experience — creativity, passion, the capacity for self-transformation — cannot be captured by any science, no matter how sophisticated.

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1749–1832) is best known as a poet and dramatist, but he was also a serious scientist who made genuine contributions to botany and optics. Goethe was not opposed to science. He was opposed to the claim that science is *sufficient*. He believed that certain human attributes — the creative imagination, the capacity to transform yourself through passionate engagement with life — transcend scientific understanding. You cannot put a number on the experience of standing before a mountain range and feeling yourself change. You cannot reduce the creative act to a formula. Some things, Goethe insisted, you can only know by living them.

Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) took this further. He focused on the irrational dimensions of the human will — the blind, driving forces that underlie all conscious thought. According to Schopenhauer, the will is driven by fundamental needs that are continually frustrated. You want something, you get it, you are briefly satisfied, and then you want something else. Life is a cycle of desire and disappointment, and reason is just a servant of the will — a tool that the will uses to get what it wants. Reason does not govern the will. The will governs reason.

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) hymned the irrational, passionate, and impulsive side of human nature with even greater intensity. Nietzsche celebrated what he called the "Dionysian" — the wild, ecstatic, boundary-dissolving side of human experience — against the "Apollonian" — the orderly, rational, controlled side. He argued that the great figures of history were not rational planners but passionate creators who imposed their vision on the world through sheer force of will. The "will to power" — the drive to create, to overcome, to become more than you are — was, for Nietzsche, the fundamental psychological force.

These thinkers did not reject science because they were ignorant of it. They rejected it because they believed it was *limited*. It could describe the surface of human experience but not its depths. It could measure behavior but not meaning. It could explain reflexes but not creativity.

Sigmund Freud later developed his own account of the conflict between the emotional and impulsive side of human nature and the rational and repressive side — the id versus the superego, the pleasure principle versus the reality principle. Freud's debt to the Romantics is often overlooked, but it is enormous.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Think about the most creative moment of your life — a solution to a problem that came out of nowhere, a piece of art or writing that seemed to flow through you rather than from you. Was that moment rational? Could it have been predicted, measured, or replicated in a laboratory? If not, what does that tell you about the limits of psychological science?

## Hegel's March of History

The Romantic vision of human history as a progressive, purposive journey — a spiritual quest — reached its most ambitious expression in the work of Georg Wilhelm Hegel (1770–1831). Hegel was not a Romantic in the literary sense. He was a systematic philosopher. But he shared the Romantics' conviction that history is going somewhere — that it is not a random sequence of events but a meaningful process of development.

Hegel represented human history as a progress toward freedom. The human mind, he argued, develops through all possible forms of experience to attain knowledge of itself and the world as it truly is — what Hegel called the **Absolute**. This is why his philosophy is often called **Absolute idealism**: the claim that reality is ultimately mental, and that history is the process by which mind comes to know itself fully.

Hegel's account of how this process works is called the **dialectic**. The dialectic operates through contradiction. At each stage of history, a particular form of life or thought develops internal tensions — contradictions that it cannot resolve within its own terms. These contradictions drive the transition to a new, more comprehensive form that preserves what was valuable in the previous stage while overcoming its limitations. This new form then develops its own contradictions, and the process continues. Thesis, antithesis, synthesis — though Hegel himself rarely used these terms.

Hegel also recognized something genuinely important about the social dimensions of mind. He argued that an individual's feelings of self-respect are grounded in the respect of others. You cannot feel like a person unless other people treat you like a person. Your psychological life is not self-contained. It is oriented toward the represented thoughts and emotions of other people in your social group. This insight — that the mind is fundamentally social — was ahead of its time.

But Hegel took this insight in a dangerous direction. He elevated the social nature of mind into an ontological doctrine about the **social mind** — a collective mentality that belongs to social groups, states, and nations as entities, irreducible to the mentality of their individual members. States and nations, in Hegel's view, are not just collections of individuals. They have minds of their own. And the individual's highest duty is to subordinate personal judgment to the collective will.

This mystical conception of the state promoted unquestioning obedience to authority. It had, as the source text notes, "disastrous consequences for later German history." Hegel's notion of historical progress as a dialectical resolution of contradictions also inspired Karl Marx (1818–1883), who replaced Hegel's idealism with materialism but kept the dialectical engine. For Marx, history is driven not by the development of ideas but by conflicts between economic classes. The contradictions are material, not mental. But the mechanism — thesis, antithesis, synthesis — is pure Hegel.

## And Yet, Psychology Arrived Anyway

Here is the irony. Every one of the objections raised in this chapter — Vico's cultural relativism, Dilthey's distinction between explaining and understanding, Rousseau's distrust of reason, the Romantics' celebration of the irrational, Hegel's social dialectic — was a challenge to the Newtonian program in psychology. Each one argued, in its own way, that you cannot study human beings the way you study falling bodies. Human beings are too culturally variable, too meaning-driven, too irrational, too social, too historical.

And yet, scientific psychology arrived anyway. By the 1880s, it was an established academic discipline in Germany and America. It did not emerge directly from the tradition of Hartley and the associationists — that tradition had been sidetracked by accusations of materialism and absorbed into the safer framework of Scottish common sense psychology. Instead, it grew out of 19th-century advances in neuroanatomy, physiology, and evolutionary theory.

But even these new psychologists were cautious about the material basis of mind. Many remained committed to dualism or neutral parallelism. They measured reaction times and mapped sensory thresholds, but they were careful not to claim too much about what their measurements *meant*. The philosophical challenges raised by Vico, Dilthey, and the Romantics were not answered. They were set aside.

In a landmark 2010 study published in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine, and Ara Norenzayan surveyed psychological research from around the world and reached a striking conclusion: the overwhelming majority of published studies used participants from WEIRD societies — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic. And these WEIRD populations turned out to be statistical outliers on many psychological measures — from visual perception to moral reasoning to cooperation. Vico had argued in 1725 that human psychology varies across cultures. Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan confirmed it — with data. The road Vico pointed to three centuries ago is the road psychology is still trying to find.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** If most psychological research is conducted on WEIRD populations — and if those populations are statistical outliers — what does that mean for the "universal laws" of psychology that the Newtonian tradition promised? Can you have a universal science of a species that varies this much?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The history of psychology is not a story of steady progress toward truth. It is a story of roads taken and roads not taken. The Newtonian road — universal laws, causal explanation, the methods of physics applied to human beings — was taken, and it produced extraordinary results. But the road Vico pointed to — cultural variation, interpretive understanding, the recognition that human beings are historical creatures who change across time and place — was not taken, and its absence left a gap that psychology is still struggling to fill. The most honest conclusion is not that one road was right and the other wrong, but that both are needed. A complete psychology would explain *and* understand. It would measure *and* interpret. It would be a natural science *and* a human science. Whether that is possible remains the open question.

## Speaking of Roads Not Taken...

- A psychologist in Bangalore designs a study on "universal" facial expressions of emotion. She finds that her participants — raised in a culture where emotional display is carefully modulated — do not match the patterns reported in studies conducted in American universities. She is discovering, firsthand, what Vico argued three centuries ago: psychology is not as universal as it claims to be.

- A teacher in Nairobi reads Rousseau's *Émile* and decides to let her students choose their own projects for a semester. The students who were previously disengaged come alive. The students who thrived on structure flounder. Rousseau's ideal education works beautifully for some children and terribly for others — which is exactly what you would expect if human nature is not as uniform as the Enlightenment assumed.

- A software engineer in Seoul spends a weekend doing nothing but hiking, painting, and reading poetry. Her colleagues think she is wasting time. She feels more creative and focused on Monday than she has in months. Goethe would have understood: some forms of knowledge cannot be acquired through analysis. They require immersion.

---

The 19th century was not finished with psychology. In fact, it was just getting started. While philosophers debated whether psychology could be a science, physiologists were quietly building the tools that would make it one. In Germany, a new generation of researchers was mapping the nervous system with unprecedented precision — tracing nerve pathways, localizing brain functions, measuring the speed of neural transmission. They were not asking whether psychology should emulate physics. They were asking something more practical: what can the body tell us about the mind? The answers they found would transform psychology forever. But they would also raise a new set of questions — about the relationship between brain and consciousness, between physiology and experience — that remain unanswered to this day.
