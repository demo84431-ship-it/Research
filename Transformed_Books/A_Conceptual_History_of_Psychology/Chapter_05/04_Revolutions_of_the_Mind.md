# Revolutions of the Mind — French Sensationalism and the Critical Responses

Imagine you are a statue. You have no eyes, no ears, no hands. You possess only a nose, and through that nose, the world begins. At first, there is nothing — just a faint whiff of something. Lavender, maybe. The sensation occupies your entire awareness because it is all you have. You do not know what "lavender" means. You do not know that smells come from objects, that the world contains things separate from yourself. There is only this: a scent, floating through the void of your consciousness.

Then the scent changes. Rose replaces lavender. And in that moment, something remarkable happens — you notice the difference. You compare. You remember the lavender that came before. A mind, it seems, is being born from nothing more than the contrast between two smells.

This was the thought experiment the French philosopher Étienne Bonnot de Condillac proposed in 1754. He wanted to prove that every human faculty — memory, attention, judgment, even the sense of self — could be built from sensation alone, with no need for innate ideas or divine programming. It was a radical claim, and it launched a revolution in how Europeans understood the mind. But here is the question Condillac could never quite answer: if all knowledge comes from sensation, where does the *knower* come from? Who is doing the smelling?

## The Statue That Thought

Condillac admired John Locke deeply — he translated Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* into French — but he thought Locke had not gone far enough. Locke had admitted that the mind possessed certain innate capacities, powers like reflection that operated independently of sensation. Condillac wanted to strip those away. In his *Treatise on the Sensations* (1754), he argued that every mental faculty is really just **sensation transformed** — sensation that has been reshaped by pleasure, pain, attention, and memory into something that *looks* like a separate capacity but is not.

His statue experiment was designed to prove this. By giving the statue only smell — the simplest sense, he believed — Condillac showed how a being could develop attention (the scent grabs you), memory (you recall the previous scent), comparison (you notice the difference), desire (you prefer one scent to another), and eventually imagination and reflection. Pleasure and pain were the engines. A pleasant smell draws the statue's attention; a painful one repels it. From these two responses, the entire architecture of the mind unfolds.

This was **sensationalism** in its purest form: the doctrine that all knowledge and all mental life derive from sensation. Condillac did not deny the existence of the soul — he was a former Catholic priest — but he made the soul entirely dependent on sensation for its cognitive operations. Without sensation, the soul is dormant. With sensation, it wakes up and begins to build a world.

### Where Do Objects Come From?

There was a problem Condillac wrestled with, and it is worth pausing on. If you only have smell, how do you ever come to believe that there are *things* out there producing the smells? Condillac argued that ideas of external objects actually require touch, not smell. When the statue finally gains the sense of touch, it discovers resistance — something pushes back — and from that experience of pushing and being pushed, it constructs the idea of an external world.

This is a surprisingly modern intuition. Developmental psychologists have shown that infants learn about object permanence partly through grasping and manipulating things. But it also reveals the limits of pure sensationalism. The statue does not just passively receive smells; it *reaches out* to touch. Where did that reaching come from? Condillac said it came from desire, which came from pleasure, which came from sensation. But critics noticed something circular in the argument: the statue seems to need a self before it can have the experiences that supposedly create the self.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Consider how you first learned that the world contains separate objects. Did you figure it out through seeing, touching, smelling — or did something in you already *expect* the world to be made of things? What would you lose if you had to build that expectation from scratch?

## Engineering Genius

If Condillac was the theorist of French sensationalism, Claude Helvétius was its evangelist. A wealthy tax collector and salon host, Helvétius took Condillac's insights and pushed them into politics. His 1758 book *Essays on the Mind* argued a breathtaking thesis: if all knowledge comes from experience, and all behavior is shaped by the pursuit of pleasure and avoidance of pain, then you can engineer virtually anything through education and legislation. Genius, virtue, scientific brilliance — none of these are gifts of nature. They are products of environment.

The implications were explosive. If genius is environmental, then the existing aristocracy has no inherent claim to power. If virtue is learned, then bad societies produce bad people, and reforming society reforms humanity. Helvétius declared that the primary obstacle to progress was religious dogma, which kept people obedient to authority rather than reason. The faculty of the Sorbonne condemned and burned his book. But the ideas caught fire anyway.

After the French Revolution, a group of thinkers called the **idéologues** — literally, "scientists of ideas" — seized on this program. Antoine Destutt de Tracy coined the very word *ideology* and argued that understanding how ideas form was the key to remaking society. Pierre Jean George Cabanis, a physician, provided the neurophysiological backbone. He famously declared that the brain "secretes thought as the stomach secretes gastric juices," reducing the mind to an organ.

### The Guillotine and the Spinal Cord

Cabanis did not just theorize. He experimented — in the grim circumstances of post-revolutionary France. Studying victims of the guillotine, he observed that severed heads appeared to show brief responses to stimuli. From this and related observations, he concluded that while the brain houses consciousness, will, and rationality, many sensory-motor reflexes are governed by the spinal cord. This was not entirely new — earlier researchers like Robert Whytt had proposed similar ideas — but Cabanis gave it a systematic, materialist framing.

Cabanis also made a programmatic claim that looks remarkably prescient: he argued that the study of abnormality and sociality, alongside development and physiology, is essential to a proper understanding of human psychology. In other words, you need clinical psychology, social psychology, developmental psychology, and neuroscience all working together. It would take another century for the discipline to catch up.

The idéologues reshaped French education and law in the early Republic. They also influenced Thomas Jefferson, who corresponded with Destutt de Tracy and shared the optimistic belief that rational education could perfect society. But Napoleon banned their work — he had no interest in a populace trained to think for itself.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Helvétius believed you could create genius through education. Modern debates about nature versus nurture echo this claim. Think of someone you consider exceptionally talented — a musician, athlete, scientist. How much of their ability do you think came from innate endowment versus environment and training? Could the right education produce ten more like them?

## The Scottish Rebellion

Not everyone was seduced by the sensationalist vision. In Aberdeen, Scotland, a Presbyterian minister-turned-philosopher named Thomas Reid looked at the edifice of empiricist psychology and declared it rotten from the foundation.

Reid's objection was precise and devastating. Locke, Berkeley, and Hume had all started from the same premise: we experience sensations — colors, sounds, smells, tastes — and from these we build up our knowledge of the world. But Reid saw that this path led to a cliff. If all you ever directly experience are your own sensations, how do you ever get to the external world? Locke had treated knowledge of physical bodies as a "doubtful inference." Berkeley had concluded that physical bodies do not exist at all — only ideas. Hume had admitted he could not rationally justify his belief in the external world and simply surrendered to custom and habit.

Reid thought this was philosophical madness, and he said so. His 1764 *Enquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense* launched a counter-revolution. The problem, Reid argued, was not with the external world. The problem was with the "way of ideas" — the assumption that we perceive ideas rather than things.

### Direct Realism: You See the Tree, Not the Idea of a Tree

Reid advocated **direct realism**: the claim that we perceive physical bodies and their properties directly, without the mediation of atomistic sense impressions. When you see an apple, you see the apple. You do not see a collection of color patches and then infer the apple. The perception of the apple *includes* a belief in its existence, its shape, its distance. That belief is not derived from comparing ideas; it is part of the perception itself.

Reid drew careful distinctions that the empiricists had blurred. Physical stimulation (light hitting your retina), sensation (the experience of color), and perception (seeing an apple) are three different things. You cannot build perception out of sensation any more than you can build a house out of the smell of sawdust. Each involves a different kind of mental act.

Reid called the powers that make direct perception possible **innate faculties** — part of the constitutional "common sense" of humanity. These faculties, he believed, were bestowed by a benevolent God and were ideally suited to their purpose. "When I perceive a tree before me," Reid wrote, "my faculty of seeing gives me not only a notion or simple apprehension of a tree, but a belief of its existence, and of its figure, distance and magnitude."

### Common Sense as Psychology

Reid's **common sense** psychology was not just a philosophical argument. It was a research program. He catalogued the innate faculties — cognitive, moral, religious — that ground human mental life and argued that a proper psychology should describe these powers rather than try to explain them mechanistically. He rejected Hartley's associationist neurophysiology as a "fallacious tract" and denied the possibility of a science of psychology modeled on Newtonian mechanics.

His students carried the program forward. Dugald Stewart promoted a chastened version of associationism at Edinburgh, stripped of its materialist trappings. William Hamilton reaffirmed the directness of perception while acknowledging that it is "conditioned" by the nature of the perceiver. And the Scottish school spread across the Atlantic. John Witherspoon, president of what became Princeton, introduced common sense psychology to American higher education. For most of the nineteenth century, American college students learned their psychology from Scottish textbooks.

Reid's influence was enormous, but his argument also left a nagging question. If God guarantees that our innate faculties are reliable, what happens when you remove the theological scaffolding? Reid's common sense worked beautifully as long as you believed in a benevolent designer. Without that belief, you need a different account of why the mind's built-in tendencies should track the truth. Modern cognitive science has found some of those tendencies — but also found that many of them are systematically biased.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Reid insisted that you perceive the world directly, not through a veil of ideas. But consider optical illusions — the Müller-Lyer illusion, for instance, where equal lines look different lengths. In those cases, your perception seems to *deceive* you. Does this undermine direct realism, or can a direct realist explain illusions without retreating to the "way of ideas"?

## Shadows in the Basement

While the Scots defended common sense, a German polymath was quietly laying the groundwork for a very different revolution. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — mathematician, diplomat, logician, and inventor of calculus (independently of Newton, and possibly first) — had written a response to Locke's *Essay* that sat unpublished for six decades. Leibniz completed his *New Essays on the Understanding* in 1704, the year Locke died, and delayed publication out of respect. It appeared only in 1765, and when it did, it changed the terms of the debate.

Where Condillac complained that Locke had granted too many innate capacities, Leibniz complained he had granted too few. Locke had neglected, Leibniz argued, our abstract knowledge of mathematics, logic, and science — knowledge of number, space, time, substance, and causality that does not seem to come from sensation alone. In a beautiful metaphor, Leibniz compared innate ideas to the veins of marble in a block of stone:

> Hercules would be innate in it, in a way, even though labor would be required to expose the veins and to polish them into clarity, removing everything that prevents their being seen. This is how ideas and truths are innate in us — as inclinations, dispositions, tendencies, or natural potentialities.

The marble metaphor captures something the empiricists missed. The idea is not fully formed and waiting to be uncovered, like a statue hidden under a cloth. Nor is it carved from nothing by experience. It is a *pattern* in the raw material — a disposition that experience can develop but cannot create from scratch.

### Petites Perceptions: The Mind Below the Surface

Leibniz made another contribution that would prove even more consequential for psychology. He argued that the mind processes far more sensory information than we are consciously aware of. He called these unconscious sensory registrations **petites perceptions** — tiny perceptions that individually never rise to the level of consciousness but collectively shape our experience.

His favorite example was the ocean. When you hear the roar of a wave crashing on the shore, that roar is composed of the sounds of thousands of individual water droplets. You cannot hear any single droplet. Yet those tiny sounds are registered by your perceptual system, and their combined effect is the thundering wave you consciously experience. The individual droplet is below your **sensory threshold**; the wave is above it.

This was a radical idea. For Locke, Berkeley, and Hume, perception was consciousness — what you are aware of is what you perceive. Leibniz shattered that equation. Most of what goes on in your mind, he insisted, happens in the dark.

The concept of petites perceptions also implied something else: perception is not passive. You do not simply receive sensations and assemble them. Your mind *actively organizes* the flood of tiny perceptions into a unified conscious experience. Leibniz called this active organization **apperception**. It is the mind reaching down into the basement of unconscious processing and pulling up a coherent picture of the world.

This idea — that perception involves active organization rather than passive assembly — would eventually flower into Gestalt psychology in the twentieth century. The notion of a sensory threshold would become central to psychophysics, the scientific measurement of the relationship between physical stimuli and psychological experience. Leibniz was writing in the early 1700s. The science would not catch up for two hundred years.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Right now, you are reading these words. But what else is your mind processing? The pressure of the chair against your body? The ambient sounds in the room? The slight tension in your shoulders? According to Leibniz, all of these are registering as petites perceptions, shaping your experience even though you were not aware of them until this moment. How much of your mental life do you think happens below the threshold of consciousness?

## The Copernican Revolution of the Mind

In 1781, a reclusive professor in Königsberg, Prussia, published a book that would reshape philosophy forever. Immanuel Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* did something no one had done before: it found a way to honor both the empiricists and the rationalists without fully agreeing with either.

Kant had been raised on the rationalist philosophy of Christian Wolff, which emphasized innate ideas and the power of reason. Then he read David Hume, who argued that our concept of causation — the idea that one event *necessarily* produces another — is nothing but a habit of association built from repeated experience. Hume's argument, Kant later confessed, woke him from his "dogmatic slumbers." If Hume was right, then the foundations of science — which depend on causal laws — were built on sand.

Kant's solution was audacious. He agreed with the empiricists that all our knowledge *begins* with experience. But he denied that all knowledge *derives* from experience. Some knowledge, he argued, is built into the structure of the mind itself — not as fully formed ideas, but as the organizing principles that make experience possible in the first place.

### The Categories: Mind's Built-In Software

Kant proposed that the mind comes equipped with innate **categories** — fundamental concepts like substance, causality, unity, plurality, necessity, and possibility. These categories are not learned from experience; they are the conditions that make experience intelligible. You cannot have an experience of the world without already organizing it in terms of substances (things that persist) and causes (events that produce other events).

Think of it this way. Imagine trying to watch a movie with no concept of time, no sense that one frame follows another, no expectation that characters persist from scene to scene. You would not see a movie. You would see a meaningless flicker of light. The categories are like the projector — they do not create the film, but without them, there is nothing to see.

Space and time, Kant argued, are even more fundamental. They are the innate forms of sensory experience itself. Every experience you have is spatial and temporal — not because the world forces this on you, but because your mind can only process experience in spatial and temporal terms. This is what Kant called **transcendental idealism**: transcendental because it describes the conditions that make experience possible, idealism because it acknowledges that the form of experience comes from the mind, not the world.

Kant called knowledge that is independent of experience **synthetic a priori** knowledge. "Synthetic" because it tells us something substantive about the world (not just definitional truths like "all bachelors are unmarried"). "A priori" because it is not derived from experience. For example, Kant argued that we can know, before any particular observation, that every event has a cause and that the total quantity of matter in nature remains constant. These are not empirical discoveries. They are the rules the mind imposes on experience.

### Schemas, Not Images

Kant also overturned a deeply held assumption shared by nearly every philosopher from Descartes to Hume: the idea that concepts are images. When you think of an apple, you might picture a red, round fruit. But Kant pointed out that your concept of "apple" is not any particular image. It is a **schema** — a cognitive rule that enables you to recognize any apple, regardless of its color, size, or variety. The schema for "apple" lets you identify a green Granny Smith, a bruised one on a market stall, and a perfect Fuji as all being apples. No single image could do that.

This distinction between concepts as schemas and concepts as images may seem abstract, but it has enormous consequences. It means that cognition is not a matter of matching pictures. It is a matter of applying rules. The mind does not passively mirror the world; it actively structures the world through the categories and schemas it brings to experience.

Kant captured this in one of the most famous sentences in the history of philosophy: "**Thoughts without content are empty; intuitions without concepts are blind.**" Pure thought, with no sensory input, tells you nothing about the world. Pure sensation, with no conceptual organization, is meaningless noise. You need both — the raw data of the senses *and* the organizing power of the mind — to know anything at all.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Try to think about something without using any concepts. You cannot do it, can you? Even thinking "I will think about nothing" uses the concepts of *will*, *thinking*, and *nothing*. Kant would say this proves his point: the mind cannot operate without its built-in conceptual framework. But does this mean the concepts are truly innate, or does it just mean they are so deeply learned that they feel innate?

### Did Kant Kill Psychology?

There is a famous story that Kant declared psychology impossible as a science. The truth is more nuanced. In his *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science* (1786), Kant argued that "inner sense" — introspection — cannot yield a genuine science because observing your own mental states changes them, and because mental life unfolds in only one dimension (time) while physics requires four (three of space plus time). A psychology based on introspecting the association of ideas would be restricted to describing correlations in a single temporal sequence, not explaining causal dynamics.

But Kant did not rule out psychology altogether. He acknowledged that the intensity of sensation can be measured quantitatively — an insight that later became the foundation of Fechner's psychophysics. And in his *Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View* (1797), he explored social and developmental psychology with remarkable insight, including a shrewd warning about how excessive introspection can lead to mental disturbance. Kant denied the possibility of one *kind* of psychology. He opened the door to several others.

Recent research has taken Kant's categories seriously as objects of scientific investigation. A 2020 article in *Frontiers in Psychology* explored how primary cognitive categories may be determined by their invariances — the features that remain constant across different contexts of perception — echoing Kant's argument that categories are not derived from experience but structure it. Developmental psychologist Elizabeth Spelke has documented "core knowledge systems" in infants — built-in capacities for understanding objects, agents, number, and space that appear before significant learning has occurred. These findings do not prove Kant right in every detail, but they suggest that his fundamental insight — the mind comes equipped with organizing structures — has remarkable empirical support.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Elizabeth Spelke has shown that infants as young as three months expect objects to continue existing when hidden, and that they are surprised when objects seem to violate physical laws. If these expectations are present before any learning could have occurred, what does that tell you about the relationship between experience and innate knowledge? Is this evidence for something like Kant's categories?

## The Architecture of Knowledge

Here is the shape of the revolution that Condillac, Reid, Leibniz, and Kant collectively wrought. The empiricists and sensationalists had argued that the mind is a blank slate, written on by experience. The Scottish realists said no — the mind comes equipped with faculties that directly apprehend the world. Leibniz said something stranger still — the mind is processing far more than it knows, and it actively organizes what it processes. Kant synthesized these threads into a new picture: the mind is neither a blank slate nor a passive mirror. It is an active architect, imposing structure on the raw materials of sensation through innate categories, schemas, and forms of intuition.

This was not just a philosophical argument. It was a blueprint for the future of psychology. The question of whether the mind comes equipped with built-in structures — and if so, what those structures are — would drive research programs for the next two centuries. From Gestalt psychology to cognitive science, from Chomsky's universal grammar to Spelke's core knowledge, the debate Condillac, Reid, Leibniz, and Kant started in the eighteenth century is still being fought today.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The mind is not a camera. It is not a blank page. It is not even a very good mirror. It is an active, structuring, organizing force that brings its own architecture to every experience it has. Every major advance in psychology since the eighteenth century has been, at bottom, an attempt to understand what that architecture looks like and how it works.

Kant's insight about schemas has been validated by modern cognitive science. In "Perceptual Symbol Systems" (1999), published in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, the psychologist Lawrence Barsalou argued that concepts are not abstract, amodal symbols (as classical empiricism assumed) but are grounded in perceptual simulations — reactivations of the same neural systems involved in perception. When you think about a cherry, you do not retrieve an abstract definition. You simulate seeing, touching, and tasting it. This is Kant's schema theory, updated with neuroscience: concepts are not pictures, but they are structured by the same systems that produce perception.

## Speaking of...

**You are tasting a new dish at a friend's house in Lagos.** The flavors are unfamiliar — bitter leaf, ogiri, a spice you cannot name. You try to describe it by comparing it to things you already know. "It tastes a bit like..." and then you trail off, because no comparison quite fits. This is Condillac's problem in reverse: you are trying to build a new sensation out of old ones, and it does not quite work. Your existing categories — sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umidi — are not enough. The dish exceeds your schema.

**A teacher in São Paulo is trying to teach fractions to eight-year-olds.** She uses physical objects — pizza slices, chocolate bars, measuring cups. The children grasp the idea when they can touch and manipulate things, but struggle when the fractions become abstract symbols on a page. This is Kant's point made concrete: raw sensory experience (the pizza) and abstract concepts (the fraction notation) are both necessary. Neither alone is sufficient. The teacher is building schemas, not just transmitting images.

**A neuroscientist in Seoul is studying patients with blindsight** — people who are cortically blind in part of their visual field but can nevertheless guess the location of objects they report not seeing. Their brains are processing visual information below the threshold of consciousness. This is Leibniz's petites perceptions, documented with fMRI scanners. The mind is registering more than it knows, and the boundary between conscious and unconscious processing is far blurrier than the empiricists ever imagined.

## The Road Not Yet Taken

The thinkers in this module — the sensationalists, the common sense philosophers, the German rationalists — all assumed, in different ways, that psychology should be a natural science, modeled on physics. They disagreed about method and content, but they shared the Newtonian faith that the mind, like the material world, operates according to universal laws.

But what if that assumption is wrong? What if human minds are fundamentally different from material objects — not because they are immaterial, but because they are *meaningful* in ways that rocks and rivers are not? What if understanding a person requires not explanation but interpretation? In the next module, we will encounter a thinker who asked exactly these questions. Giambattista Vico argued that we can truly know only what we ourselves have made — and that the human world of culture, language, and history requires a completely different kind of science from the one Newton gave us. The romantics would pick up this thread and pull it into a revolution that challenged everything the Enlightenment had built.
