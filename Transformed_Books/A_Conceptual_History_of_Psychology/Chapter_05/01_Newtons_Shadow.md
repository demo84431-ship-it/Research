# Newton's Shadow — How Physics Colonized Psychology

It is 1935, and Clark Hull's graduate students at Yale are filing into his office for their first advising session. The room smells of pipe tobacco and old wood. On the desk, next to a stack of data sheets from his rat-maze experiments, sits a massive leather-bound volume: Newton's *Principia Mathematica*, published in 1687 — nearly 250 years earlier. Hull tells each incoming student the same thing: read this before you read anything else. Not Skinner, not Watson, not even Thorndike. Newton. The students nod, confused but obedient. After all, Hull is building a "complete system of psychology" modeled on Newtonian mechanics — axioms, theorems, the whole architecture. He genuinely believes he can do for the mind what Newton did for falling apples and orbiting planets.

Here is the strange part. By 1935, physicists themselves had already moved past Newton. Einstein's relativity and quantum mechanics had dismantled the old clockwork universe. The deterministic certainty that Newton's equations promised — the idea that if you knew the position and velocity of every particle, you could predict the entire future — had collapsed under the weight of subatomic weirdness. Yet Hull kept the *Principia* on his desk anyway. He was not alone. Generations of psychologists had internalized Newton's framework so deeply that it persisted like a phantom limb — felt even after the original had been amputated.

Why did a 17th-century physicist's vision of the universe become the invisible blueprint for how psychologists studied the human mind? And what happens when you try to squeeze something as messy, meaningful, and historically particular as human experience into a mold designed for gravity and billiard balls?

## The Enlightenment's Grand Ambition

Sometime in the early 18th century, a new mood swept through the salons of Paris, the coffeehouses of Edinburgh, and the lecture halls of Göttingen. People began to believe — really believe — that human reason could remake the world. They called this mood the **Enlightenment**, a period in European thought during the 17th and 18th centuries when confidence in human reason and experience gradually replaced faith in religious tradition and royal authority as the arbiters of truth. It was not a single movement with a manifesto. It was more like a shared exhale — a collective sense that the old answers were no longer good enough.

The Enlightenment carried several commitments bundled together. First, a belief in **human progress**: that societies could improve, that ignorance could be cured, that poverty was not God's punishment but a problem to be solved. Second, an optimism about **science**: the methods that had unlocked the mechanics of the heavens could, in principle, unlock anything — including human nature itself. Third, a political vision of **liberal democracy**: Locke's ideas about consent of the governed fed directly into the American and French revolutions.

You can feel this confidence radiating from the period's architecture, its encyclopedias, its universal museums. The French *Encyclopédie* of Diderot and d'Alembert was not just a reference book — it was a statement that all human knowledge could be organized, classified, and made accessible. If you could catalog everything, you could understand everything. And if you could understand everything, you could fix everything.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** The Enlightenment happened in Europe, but its effects were global. In India, Enlightenment ideals influenced Rammohan Roy's push for education reform. In Brazil, they shaped the intellectual currents behind independence. Can you think of a moment in your own country's history when "reason over tradition" became a rallying cry?

The dark side of this optimism was a quiet arrogance. If science could explain everything, then the methods of one particularly successful science — physics — could be transplanted wholesale into every other domain. And one particular physicist's success story became the template for the entire enterprise.

## Newton Becomes the Gold Standard

Isaac Newton did not set out to colonize psychology. He was too busy with optics, calculus, and the motion of planets. But his *Principia Mathematica* (1687) achieved something so stunning that it redefined what "science" meant for the next three centuries. He unified Galileo's terrestrial laws of falling bodies with Kepler's celestial laws of planetary motion under a single theory of universal gravitation. One equation explained why apples fall and why the moon does not. The intellectual world gasped.

Newton's achievement was so dazzling that later generations began to treat specific features of his theory not as characteristics of *one particular science*, but as **requirements** for any science at all. Four features stood out.

### Universality: One Theory to Rule Them All

Newton's theory explained both terrestrial and celestial motion with the same gravitational law. This feature — **universality**, the idea that an adequate scientific explanation must cover all phenomena in a given domain — became an implicit criterion for scientific respectability. Newton himself was cautious about this, writing that "to the same natural effects we must, as far as possible, assign the same causes" (note his careful "as far as possible"). He was even willing to invoke God's intervention for certain celestial motions that gravity alone could not explain.

But his followers dropped the caution. Psychologists absorbed universality as a non-negotiable demand. If your theory of learning did not explain *all* learning — in rats, humans, children, adults, across cultures and centuries — it was not really scientific. This is why psychology has spent over a hundred years chasing what one researcher called its "Newton" — a single grand theory that unifies everything. In a 2025 article published in *ScienceDirect*, researchers traced how this quest for universal determinism shaped models across the social sciences, often at the expense of recognizing cultural and historical variation.

### Atomism: Breaking the World into Pieces

Newton speculated at the beginning of the *Principia* that all natural phenomena might eventually be explained by mathematical laws governing the tiny "corpuscles" — what we would now call atoms — that compose larger bodies. This was the **corpuscularian hypothesis**, championed by Newton's colleague Robert Boyle, which held that complex entities could be understood by breaking them into their smallest material parts. The principle of **atomism** — that scientific entities can be individuated and studied independently of other entities — became a template for psychological analysis.

Psychologists took this literally. If physics works by decomposing matter into atoms, then psychology should decompose the mind into mental elements. The associationists — Locke, Hume, Hartley, Mill — built entire theories around the idea that complex thoughts are just atoms of experience (sensations, images) combined by laws of association. You do not need to understand a whole person. You need to understand the pieces.

### Determinism: The Clockwork Universe

Newton's gravitational equations described a universe that ran like clockwork. But it was the French mathematician Pierre Simon de Laplace who pushed this to its logical conclusion. Laplace demonstrated that the solar system was mechanically stable within Newton's framework — it did not need God to keep it running. When Napoleon allegedly asked Laplace where God fit in his system, Laplace replied: "I had no need of that hypothesis."

Laplace imagined a superintelligence that knew the position and velocity of every particle in the universe at a given instant. Such an intelligence could calculate — and predict — every future event. This is **determinism**: the doctrine that every event has an antecedent set of conditions sufficient to produce it. Psychologists absorbed this as a foundational assumption. If you could know all the stimuli acting on a person and all their past conditioning, you could predict their behavior. It sounds reasonable until you remember that physicists themselves abandoned strict determinism in the early 20th century with the arrival of quantum mechanics and chaos theory. A *NIH PMC* article on chaos theory notes that "universal determinism" dominated science for centuries — until the scientists who built it walked away from it.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** If physicists gave up determinism nearly a hundred years ago, why do you think many psychologists still treat it as an unquestioned assumption? What would psychology look like if it genuinely embraced uncertainty?

### Explanatory Reduction: The Whole Is Just Its Parts

The principle of **explanatory reduction** holds that the best explanation of a complex entity is in terms of its material components. Newton's own words are revealing: he wished to "derive the rest of the phenomena of nature" from mathematical principles governing particles. Psychologists adopted this wholesale. The ultimate goal became explaining consciousness, emotion, and thought in terms of neurophysiology, and neurophysiology in terms of chemistry, and chemistry in terms of physics. Everything reduced downward. The mind was "nothing but" neurons; neurons were "nothing but" chemistry; chemistry was "nothing but" physics.

This chain of reductions felt like rigor. It felt like progress. It felt like what Newton would have wanted.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Consider a poem you love. Could you explain its meaning by analyzing the chemical composition of the ink on the page? If not, what does that tell you about the limits of explanatory reduction?

The legend of Newton's success also promoted a narrative about how science progresses: through continuous theoretical unification. Galileo's law of free fall and Kepler's laws of planetary motion were separate achievements, unified by Newton. Various gas laws — Boyle's, Charles's, Graham's — were later integrated into the kinetic theory of gases. Psychologists dreamed the same dream: unify classical conditioning and operant conditioning into a single theory of learning (Hull tried this explicitly in 1937). Develop a Grand Unified Theory of psychology. The dream persists. Psychology still awaits its Newton — and perhaps that wait tells us something important about whether the dream itself is the right one to have.

## Locke: The Man Who Cleared the Rubbish

In 1689, two men met in London and struck up a friendship that would shape the history of psychology. One was Isaac Newton, already famous. The other was John Locke, a physician, philosopher, and political exile who had just returned from hiding in Holland. Locke was deeply impressed by what he called the "incomparable Mr. Newton." They corresponded for the rest of their lives.

But Locke's path to that meeting was anything but straightforward. Born in 1632 in Somerset, England, he studied medicine at Oxford, where he crossed paths with Robert Boyle — the pioneering chemist and chief advocate of the **corpuscularian hypothesis**, the idea that all matter is composed of tiny particles. Locke served as Boyle's research assistant for years, absorbing the mechanical philosophy that would later infuse his psychological theory. Both men became members of the newly founded Royal Society.

Locke's life was shaped as much by politics as by science. As a teenager at Westminster School — which sits right next to the Palace of Whitehall — he may have watched the execution of King Charles I in 1649. It was common in those days for schoolchildren to be taken to public executions and then beaten afterward, to make sure the memory stuck. That memory certainly stuck with Locke. He became a fierce critic of the "divine right of kings" and argued that political power depends on the consent of the governed.

His political convictions nearly cost him his life. His patron, the Earl of Shaftesbury — whose life Locke had saved in 1668 by supervising a daring operation to remove a cyst from his liver (the silver tap left in Shaftesbury's abdomen inspired many jokes at court) — opposed the Catholic succession of James II. Shaftesbury was imprisoned in the Tower of London, escaped, and fled to Holland. Locke followed him into exile in 1683. They returned only after the Protestant William of Orange took the English throne in the "Glorious Revolution" of 1688.

Two years after returning, Locke published the work that would make him the father of British empiricism: *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690). He told Newton that his ambition was modest — to serve as an **under-labourer** for Newtonian science, "clearing ground a little, and removing some of the rubbish, that lies in the way to knowledge." It was a disarming metaphor. Locke was not claiming to be a genius. He was claiming to sweep the floor so that geniuses like Newton could work. But what he built on that swept floor turned out to be as revolutionary as anything Newton achieved.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Locke called himself an "under-labourer" — a humble helper clearing away obstacles. Many great thinkers have described their work in modest terms. Why do you think framing your contribution as "service" to a bigger project might actually be a powerful intellectual strategy?

## Every Idea Comes from Somewhere

Locke's central question in the *Essay* was deceptively simple: where do our ideas come from? Not "what is true?" or "what can we know?" — those were the old questions. Locke wanted to know about the *origin* of the raw materials of thought.

His answer became the foundation of **psychological empiricism**: the doctrine that all our ideas or concepts are derived from experience. Every thought you have, every concept you use, traces back to one of two sources. **Ideas of sensation** come from the outside world — the red of a sunset, the sound of a drum, the taste of salt. **Ideas of reflection** come from your inner experience — your awareness of your own thinking, doubting, willing. There is nothing in the mind that did not first pass through the senses or through introspection. No innate ideas. No knowledge stamped into the soul at birth. The mind starts as a blank page.

From these raw materials, the mind builds everything else. Locke distinguished between **simple ideas** — the elementary building blocks like "red," "sweet," "hard" — and **complex ideas**, which the mind assembles by combining simple ones. A "cherish" is a complex idea combining red, round, sweet, and so on. A "government" is a complex idea built from observations of people, rules, power, and territory. The mind is not passive. It actively selects, compares, and combines. But it can never create ideas from nothing. Experience is the only source.

Locke also advanced a radical theory of meaning. He argued that words do not have meaning in themselves — they derive their meaning from the ideas they stand for. This is **meaning empiricism**: the claim that meaningful words must ultimately be linked to ideas drawn from experience. If a word does not connect to any idea grounded in sensation or reflection, it is, for Locke, meaningless noise. This was a direct challenge to the scholastic philosophers who used elaborate Latin terms — "substantial forms," "real essences" — that, Locke argued, referred to nothing anyone had ever experienced.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Try to think of a concept you use regularly that seems hard to trace back to a specific experience. Does "justice" come from seeing a fair decision? Does "infinity" come from counting? What happens to meaning empiricism when you push it to its limits?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Newton did not colonize psychology on purpose. He was cautious, qualified, even willing to invoke God where his own equations fell short. It was the generations after Newton — the psychologists, the social scientists, the Enlightenment optimists — who turned his particular features into universal requirements. Locke, the self-described under-labourer, laid the philosophical groundwork for this colonization by arguing that all ideas come from experience and all meaningful words point back to experience. The result was a psychology modeled on physics: universal, atomistic, deterministic, reductive. The model was powerful. It was also, as we will see, profoundly limiting.

The depth of this colonization has been documented by historians of psychology. In *History, Man, and Reason: A Study in Nineteenth-Century Thought* (1971), the philosopher Maurice Mandelbaum traced how the mechanistic and deterministic assumptions of Newtonian physics became the invisible framework for 19th-century approaches to the human sciences. The framework was so pervasive that it shaped not just what psychologists studied, but what they considered worth studying — and what they dismissed as unscientific.

## Speaking of...

**The smartphone app developer.** A software engineer in Lagos builds a recommendation algorithm for a music streaming app. She designs it on universal principles — if a user likes Afrobeats and hip-hop, the algorithm predicts they will like any genre with similar tempo and rhythm patterns. It works brilliantly in Nigeria. Then the company expands to South Korea, and the algorithm fails spectacularly. Users there organize their listening around moods, social contexts, and seasonal occasions — none of which her universal model captures. She has built a Newtonian system: one law for all users, all cultures, all times. The users, it turns out, are not atoms.

**The medical student in São Paulo.** During a physiology lecture, the professor explains how the heart pumps blood by describing the mechanical contraction of cardiac muscle fibers — pressure, volume, flow rates, all perfectly Newtonian. A student raises her hand: "But what about the patient who recovers faster when his family visits? That is not in the equation." The professor pauses. The mechanical model explains the pump. It does not explain the patient.

**The cricket coach in Chennai.** He has reduced batting technique to its component parts: foot placement, bat angle, head position, follow-through. He drills each element separately, then reassembles them. His students can execute perfect textbook shots in practice. But in a match, against a bowler they have never seen, with the crowd roaring and the pitch crumbling, they freeze. The atomistic training worked on the pieces. It did not prepare them for the whole.

---

Locke built his empiricism on the idea that all knowledge comes from experience — but he also made a distinction that would haunt psychology for centuries. He argued that the qualities we experience — color, taste, warmth — exist partly in the objects themselves and partly in our perception of them. Some qualities are "really" in the object; others are merely in the mind. This distinction between primary and secondary qualities seemed straightforward. It was anything but. A young Irish bishop named George Berkeley would soon ask a devastating question: if secondary qualities like color exist only in the mind, how do you know that primary qualities like shape do not? What if *everything* you experience is a construction of the mind? And if that is the case, what happens to the material world that Newton's physics was supposed to explain?
