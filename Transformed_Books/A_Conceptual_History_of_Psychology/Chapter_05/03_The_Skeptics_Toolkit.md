In the winter of 1734, a young Scottish man barely into his twenties locked himself away in a small house in La Flèche, a quiet town in western France. He had failed at everything — a brief stint studying law that left him miserable, a brief venture into commerce that left him broke. Now he was writing. Not a novel, not a memoir, but something far more dangerous: a systematic dismantling of everything people thought they knew about the mind. His name was David Hume, and the book he was writing — *A Treatise of Human Nature* — would eventually be recognized as one of the most important works in the history of Western thought. But when it finally appeared in 1739, it "fell dead-born from the press," as he later recalled. Nobody noticed. Nobody cared.

What Hume was attempting was unprecedented. He wanted to do for the human mind what Newton had done for the physical world: reduce it to its simplest components and discover the laws governing their interaction. But Hume had a weapon that Newton never possessed — a ruthless skepticism that turned every claim about the mind back on itself. He had one devastating question, and he asked it of every concept he encountered: *From what impression is that idea derived?* If you couldn't point to a specific sensory experience or feeling that gave rise to your concept, then your concept was meaningless. You were just moving your lips.

The results of this interrogation were shocking. Hume discovered that we have no direct impression of a "self." He found that our idea of beauty isn't a property of beautiful objects at all — it's a feeling we project outward. And most consequentially for the future of psychology, he argued that causation — the very foundation of scientific reasoning — isn't something we observe in the world. It's something our minds manufacture. We see one billiard ball strike another, and the second one moves. We see it happen again and again. Eventually, we *feel* that the first ball *causes* the second to move. But that feeling of causation, Hume insisted, comes from inside us, not from the world.

If Hume was right about all of this — if causation is just a habit of the mind, if science can only describe correlations rather than uncover hidden powers — then what was left for psychology? Could you build a science of the mind on foundations that were, at bottom, just patterns of expectation? And if you couldn't observe causal powers, what was the point of searching for the mechanisms behind human behavior? These were the questions that would haunt psychologists for the next two centuries.

## The Mind's Razor: Impressions, Ideas, and the Test of Meaning

### What Hits You First

You're walking through a night market in Bangkok. The smell of sizzling garlic hits you. Neon signs blur in your peripheral vision. A vendor shouts a price. Your stomach growls. All of this — the smells, the lights, the sounds, the hunger pangs — arrives in your mind with force and immediacy. You don't choose to experience any of it. It simply *happens to you*.

Hume called these raw experiences **impressions**. They include every sensation that strikes your mind with "force and violence" — the red of a sunset, the sting of a burn, the ache of grief, the rush of joy. Impressions are your mind's first contact with reality. They are vivid, vivid, and undeniable.

Now close your eyes and think about that night market. You can recall the garlic, the neon, the vendor's voice. But the memory is weaker, hazier, more like a faded photograph than the real thing. Hume called these mental echoes **ideas** — faint images of impressions that arise when you think, reason, or remember. Ideas are impressions turned pale by memory and imagination.

Hume was a **psychological atomist**, which meant he believed every complex thought you have — your idea of a city, a friend, a philosophical argument — is built from smaller, indivisible pieces of experience. Bite into an apple: the redness, the sweetness, the crunch. Each of those qualities is a separate simple impression. Your complex idea of "apple" is simply those impressions fused together. Take the apple apart in your mind, and you find discrete sensory building blocks.

### The Test That Changes Everything

Here is where Hume becomes dangerous. Following Berkeley, Hume embraced **meaning empiricism** — the principle that a word is meaningful only if it can be traced back to some impression from experience. If you use a word and cannot point to the specific sensory experience or feeling that gave it meaning, then you are, quite literally, talking nonsense.

Hume put this directly: "When we entertain, therefore, any suspicion that a philosophical term is employed without any meaning or idea, we need to enquire *from what impression is that supposed idea derived?*" If you cannot answer that question, you may be certain you are mistaken when you imagine you have any such idea.

This wasn't academic gamesmanship. Hume used this test like a surgeon's scalpel, cutting into concepts that philosophers had treated as self-evident for centuries. Take the idea of a "self." You probably feel certain that there's a continuous "you" reading these words — a stable inner entity that persists through time. Hume tried to find it. He turned his attention inward, searching introspectively for this supposed self, and reported that he could never catch it:

> "For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble upon some particular perception or another, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pleasure or pain. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe anything but the perception."

There was no impression of a self — just a stream of perceptions. The "self," Hume concluded, was a fiction the mind creates to bundle those perceptions together.

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Try Hume's test right now. Take the concept of "justice." Can you point to a specific sight, sound, taste, touch, or feeling that corresponds to it? Or is "justice" a word built from other, more basic experiences — the feeling of indignation when someone is cheated, the satisfaction when wrongs are righted? What does your answer reveal about where moral concepts come from?

### Beauty Lives in You, Not in the Object

Hume extended his impression test to aesthetics with a result that still unsettles people. Consider the Venus de Milo, the famous Greek sculpture. You look at it and feel something — admiration, wonder, a sense of harmony. Naturally, you assume the sculpture *has* beauty, the way it has arms (well, one arm). But Hume asked: What impression corresponds to this "beauty" you perceive? You see marble. You see proportions. You see the play of light on surfaces. But none of those impressions is beauty itself.

The beauty, Hume argued, is not a property of the sculpture. It is "the effect which that figure produces upon the mind, whose peculiar fabric of structure renders it susceptible of such sentiments." Your feeling of beauty arises inside you, triggered by the object but not located in it. Yet you cannot help but project that feeling outward, as if the marble itself radiated beauty.

This was Hume's pattern. He would identify a concept we think describes the world, then show it actually describes something happening inside us. And nowhere would this move have more explosive consequences than when he turned his scalpel on the concept of causation.

## Hume's Fork: The Two Kinds of Truth

### The Divider

Imagine you're a librarian in 18th-century Edinburgh, and someone hands you a new book. How do you decide whether it contains anything worth reading? Hume gave you a sorting tool so sharp it cuts through everything ever written. He called it, informally, a fork — a division of all meaningful statements into exactly two types.

The first type: **relations of ideas**. These are statements whose truth or falsity depends entirely on the logical relationships between the concepts involved. "A triangle has three sides." "All bachelors are unmarried." "2 + 2 = 4." You don't need to go out into the world and check. You can determine their truth by pure reasoning, because denying them produces a contradiction.

The second type: **matters of fact**. These are statements whose truth or falsity depends on what actually exists in the world. "Water boils at 100°C." "All acids are corrosive." "Elation is regularly followed by disappointment." You cannot determine their truth by reasoning alone. You have to go look. You have to observe, experiment, experience.

Now here is the devastating move: Hume claimed these two types exhaust all meaningful propositions. If a statement doesn't fit one prong or the other, it isn't false — it's *meaningless*. It's noise dressed up as knowledge.

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Take any statement from a self-help book: "You must align your chakras to achieve inner peace." Does it contain abstract reasoning about quantity or number? No. Does it describe an observable matter of fact? Not really. What would Hume say to do with it? Are you comfortable with that conclusion? What might be lost if we actually followed his advice?

### The Bonfire of the Metaphysics

Hume understood the implications of his fork, and he stated them with chilling clarity. In the closing lines of his *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, he imagined running through a library and applying his test to every volume:

> "When we run over libraries, persuaded of these principles, what havoc must we make? If we take in our hand any volume; of divinity or school metaphysics, for instance; let us ask, Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."

That passage has been called the most devastating paragraph in the history of philosophy. In a few sentences, Hume threatened to incinerate centuries of metaphysical speculation, theological argument, and philosophical reasoning about the nature of reality. If your claims can't be checked against logic or experience, they're fuel for the fire.

This wasn't mere iconoclasm. Hume was drawing a line in the sand for any future science of the mind. If psychology was going to be a genuine science, it had to deal in matters of fact — in observable, testable claims about human experience and behavior. Everything else was smoke.

## The Ghost in the Machine: Hume on Causation

### The Billiard Ball Problem

Here is the experiment that undid centuries of philosophy. Place a white billiard ball on a table. Roll it toward a stationary red ball. The white ball strikes the red ball. The red ball moves. Simple enough. Now Hume asks you a question that sounds almost childish: *What did you actually see?*

You saw the white ball approach the red ball. You saw them touch — **contiguity** in space and time. You saw the white ball move first — temporal **priority**. And you've seen this kind of thing happen many times before — the two events are **constantly conjoined** in your experience. But Hume insists: did you see the *causation*? Did you observe some invisible force or power pass from the white ball to the red ball, compelling it to move?

You didn't. You saw one event follow another. You saw a pattern repeat. What you did *not* see — what no one has ever seen — is the "necessary connection" between cause and effect. As Hume put it: "We never have any impression, that contains any power or efficacy. We never therefore have any idea of power."

This is the **constant conjunction** theory of causation. All we ever observe is that one type of event reliably follows another. The ball moves, the other ball moves. The flame touches your skin, you feel pain. The lightning flashes, the thunder follows. We see sequence. We see regularity. We do not see — we *cannot* see — the mechanism that links them.

### The Feeling We Mistake for a Fact

But wait. You *do* feel something more than just sequence, don't you? When you see the white ball rolling toward the red one, you don't merely observe — you *expect*. You anticipate the red ball's movement before it happens. You feel, in your bones, that the collision *must* produce motion. That feeling of inevitability — that sense of **necessary connection** — seems as real as the balls themselves.

Hume agreed the feeling was real. He simply denied that it came from the world. It came from *you*. After observing enough repetitions of one event following another, your mind develops a habit — an internal expectation. That habit, that expectation, *is* your idea of necessary connection. You experience it as a property of the objects, but it's actually a property of your own psychology.

Here is how Hume put it: "After we have observed the resemblance in a sufficient number of instances, we immediately feel a determination of the mind to pass from one object to its usual attendant... Necessity, then, is the effect of this observation, and is nothing but an internal impression of the mind."

You project this feeling outward, onto the world, the same way you project beauty onto a sculpture. Causation, like beauty, lives in you — not in the objects.

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Think of a superstition common in your culture — a black cat crossing your path, spilling salt, breaking a mirror. Millions of people feel a "necessary connection" between these events and bad luck. Is this feeling psychologically different from the feeling you get when you believe the sun will rise tomorrow? If so, what makes one reliable and the other not? If not, what does that tell you about the source of causal belief?

### Two Definitions, One Revolution

Hume produced two definitions of causality that together reshaped how psychologists would think about the mind for centuries.

His first definition was "philosophical" — objective, outward-looking: "We may define a CAUSE to be an object precedent and contiguous to another, and where all the objects resembling the former are placed in like relations of precedency and contiguity to those objects, that resemble the latter." In plain language: to call something a cause is just to say it regularly precedes and touches something else. That's the legitimate, observable content of any causal claim.

His second definition was "natural" — psychological, inward-looking: "A CAUSE is an object precedent and contiguous to another, and so united with it, that the idea of the one determines the mind to form the idea of the other." This definition describes how your mind *works* when it makes causal judgments — one perception triggers the expectation of another through habit.

These two definitions split causation in half. The first tells you what science can legitimately observe: correlations. The second tells you why you feel causation is more than correlation: because your mind adds something to the raw data. This split — between the world as it is and the world as your mind constructs it — would echo through psychology for the next 300 years.

## Molière's Laugh: Science and the "Dormative Virtue"

### Why Opium Makes You Sleep

In Molière's comedy *Le Malade Imaginaire* (*The Hypochondriac*), a pompous medical student is asked why opium induces sleep. His answer: because of its "soporific power" — its "dormative virtue." The audience laughs, and rightly so. The "explanation" is circular. Opium makes you sleep because opium makes you sleep. Nothing has been explained.

Hume and the empiricists who followed him seized on this kind of circular reasoning to argue for a radically deflationary view of science. If every causal explanation ultimately reduces to "this happens because it always happens," then references to hidden forces, powers, and mechanisms are scientifically empty. The business of science, on this view, is simply to describe the **constant conjunctions** — the reliable correlations between observable events — that we call natural laws. Saying that gravity is a "force of attraction" between bodies is just saying that bodies move toward each other. The word "force" adds nothing.

This is the **empiricist conception of causal explanation**: science doesn't explain *why* things happen. It describes *what* happens, in what order, with what regularity. Explanation is redescription. Prediction is the goal. Mechanism is a mirage.

### The Useful Critique — and Its Limits

Hume's critique was genuinely useful in one respect. It killed the old Aristotelian idea that causal explanations are necessary truths — that you could figure out what heated water *must* do by pure reason, the way you figure out that a triangle *must* have three sides. Hume established once and for all that causal knowledge is empirical. You have to go look. Nobody can discover what bodies, acids, or human beings can or cannot do except through observation and experiment.

But the empiricist conception of science went too far, and practicing scientists have always known it. A chemist who explains the behavior of sodium in terms of its atomic structure isn't merely redescribing what sodium does. She's describing an underlying mechanism — the arrangement of electrons, the reactivity of the outer shell — that *explains* why sodium behaves as it does. The explanation isn't circular, even though the mechanism isn't directly visible.

Most working scientists remain what philosophers call **causal realists**: they believe that causation is grounded in real mechanisms, not just in patterns of observation. Causation supports qualified conditionals — "if you heat this water, it will boil, *unless* you increase the pressure" — not just flat conjunctions. And the frequency with which a cause produces its effect depends on whether anything interferes. A tubercle bacillus has the power to cause tuberculosis, but vaccination can block it. Plutonium has the power to kill, but lead shielding prevents it. The cause is real even when its effect doesn't appear.

This disagreement isn't just philosophical. It matters enormously for an applied science like psychology. If you want to *prevent* a harmful behavior — aggression, addiction, self-harm — you need more than correlations. You need to understand the underlying mechanisms well enough to intervene. You need to know not just that X is followed by Y, but *how* X produces Y, so you can block the process. Molière's doctor couldn't help anyone sleep better. A pharmacologist who understands receptor binding can.

## The Brain Vibrates: Hartley's Blueprint for Behaviorism

### From Ether to Neurons

David Hartley was a quiet English physician who did something Hume never attempted: he tried to locate the association of ideas inside the brain itself. Where Hume worked purely at the level of mental experience, Hartley wanted a physical theory — vibrations in neural tissue that could explain how sensations become ideas, and how ideas become actions.

Hartley drew his inspiration from Newton. Newton had postulated an invisible "ether" to explain how gravity could act across empty space — how the sun could pull the earth without touching it. Hartley took this model and applied it to the brain. External objects strike your senses and generate **vibrations** — waves of activity in the "white medullary substance" of the brain. These vibrations are the physical basis of impressions. When the vibrations are strong and immediate, you experience a sensation. When they're faint and lingering, you experience an idea.

Hartley gave the faint echoes a name: **vibratiuncles** — miniature vibrations, the neural traces left behind by repeated sensations. Every time you see a mango, the visual vibration leaves a vibratiuncle. Every time you taste a mango, the gustatory vibration leaves its own trace. The more often these experiences occur together, the more strongly the vibratiuncles link.

### How Memories Fuse

Hartley's account of how neural connections form was remarkably modern. He proposed that when two vibrations occur at the same time or in rapid succession, they literally fuse — they "run into one vibration." After enough repetitions, this fused vibration leaves a permanent trace, a **compound miniature**. Later, when just one part of the original experience recurs (say, the sight of a mango), it reactivates the whole compound trace — and you taste the sweetness even before you bite.

This is **synchronous association** (when impressions arrive simultaneously) and **successive association** (when they arrive in rapid sequence). Hartley claimed these two types cover all learning. Every complex idea you have is a network of fused vibratiuncles, linked by the timing of your past experiences.

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Think about a song that instantly transports you to a specific moment in your life — a wedding, a funeral, a first date. The music and the emotions fused together through repetition or intensity. Is this what Hartley meant by vibratiuncles merging? What does it tell you that a single sensory cue can unpack an entire complex memory?

### The Child, the Toy, and the Birth of Conditioning

Hartley didn't stop at ideas. He extended his theory to behavior — and in doing so, sketched out the basic logic of conditioning 150 years before Pavlov and Skinner.

He observed that infants automatically grasp objects placed in their palms. This is a **reflex** — an involuntary, wired-in motor response. But through repeated experience, the child's grasping response becomes associated with the mere *sight* of a favorite toy. The sensory vibration (seeing the toy) fuses with the motor vibration (the grasping movement). Eventually, the sight alone triggers the grasping — even before the toy touches the hand.

This is an early description of what would later be called **classical conditioning**: a reflexive response becomes triggered by a new stimulus through repeated pairing. Pavlov's dogs salivated at the sound of a bell; Hartley's children reached at the sight of a toy. The principle is identical. Only the terminology changed.

Hartley also described the reverse process. Originally reflexive behaviors — swallowing, breathing, even controlling the bladder — can come under voluntary control through repeated association with conscious ideas. You learn to control your breathing during a panic attack because the idea of "calm down" has become linked to the motor pattern of slowing your breath. This anticipates what would later be called **instrumental conditioning** — the learning of voluntary behavior through its consequences.

### Voluntary, Automatic, and Habitual

Hartley drew a crucial distinction that still organizes how psychologists think about behavior:

| Behavior Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reflexive (original automatic)** | Wired-in responses, mediated by spinal cord | A baby grasping a placed object |
| **Voluntary** | Brought under conscious control through ideomotor association | Deliberately reaching for a glass of water |
| **Secondary automatic (habitual)** | Originally voluntary, now automatic through practice | Serving a tennis ball after years of training |

*Table: Hartley's three types of behavior and their mechanisms.*

The key insight is the middle column's arrow of learning: reflexive → voluntary → habitual. A tennis serve starts as a conscious, effortful sequence. Through thousands of repetitions, the motor vibrations fuse, the vibratiuncles link, and the serve becomes "secondary automatic" — habitual, effortless, no longer requiring conscious thought. Hartley was describing what every athlete, musician, and driver knows from experience: practice makes the impossible feel inevitable.

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Think about learning to drive a car. At first, every action requires conscious attention — checking mirrors, pressing the clutch, turning the wheel. After a year, you arrive at your destination with no memory of the journey. Is this "secondary automatic" behavior? What happened in your brain during those thousands of hours of practice? Was Hartley's model of vibrational fusion a reasonable description, even if his neuroscience was wrong?

### The Blueprint

Hartley's contribution was enormous, even if his name is less famous than those who followed him. He accomplished three things that shaped the future of psychology:

First, he grounded association in the brain. Where Locke and Hume treated association as a purely mental phenomenon — a property of ideas — Hartley placed it in neural tissue. Ideas weren't just faint copies of impressions; they were faint vibrations in the physical brain. This was the first serious attempt at a neurological psychology.

Second, he extended association from ideas to behavior. Hume had explained how ideas associate with each other. Hartley explained how ideas associate with *actions* — how a thought can trigger a muscle contraction through the linking of ideal and motory vibratiuncles. This opened the door to explaining all behavior, not just thought, through associative principles.

Third, he anticipated the core logic of conditioning theory. Classical conditioning — a neutral stimulus becomes associated with a reflexive response through repeated pairing. Instrumental conditioning — voluntary behavior is shaped by its consequences. Hartley described both mechanisms using the language of vibrations and vibratiuncles. When Pavlov published his work on conditioned reflexes in 1903, and when Skinner developed his theory of operant conditioning in the 1930s, they were formalizing principles that Hartley had sketched out in 1749.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Hume and Hartley together represent the peak — and the tension — of the Newtonian program in psychology. Hume showed that our deepest convictions about causation, self, and reality are psychological constructions, not direct perceptions of the world. Hartley tried to explain *how* those constructions happen by grounding them in the brain. But their shared legacy cut both ways. If causation is just constant conjunction, and if all mental life is just associative habit, then psychology can describe and predict — but can it explain? Can it intervene? Can it help? The empiricist toolkit was powerful, but it left psychology with a paradox: the science of the mind seemed to deny that minds could truly understand anything, including themselves.

Hume's challenge to causation has never been fully resolved. In contemporary philosophy, the debate continues. In *Freedom and Belief* (2008), the philosopher Galen Strawson argued that our experience of causation is not merely a habit of expectation but a direct perception of causal power — that Hume was wrong to reduce it to constant conjunction. Meanwhile, developmental psychologists have shown that infants as young as six months expect one object to cause another to move when they collide — suggesting that some causal expectations may be innate, not learned. Hume's razor remains sharp, but the mind keeps growing back what it cuts.

## Speaking of...

**The superstition you can't shake.** You know, rationally, that stepping on a crack won't break your mother's back. But something in you flinches anyway. That's Hume's mechanism at work — repeated conjunction (stepping on cracks, nothing happening, or occasionally something bad happening afterward) creates an internal feeling of necessary connection. Your brain doesn't care about logic. It cares about patterns. And once a pattern is established, the feeling of causation is automatic. Every culture has these — from avoiding the number four in Chinese restaurants to not opening umbrellas indoors in Lagos. The feeling is universal. The content is local. The mechanism is Humean.

**The driver who can't explain how they drive.** Ask a veteran taxi driver in São Paulo to explain, step by step, how they navigate chaotic traffic while holding a conversation. They can't. The steering, braking, lane changes, and horn patterns are all "secondary automatic" — Hartley's term for habitual behavior that was once conscious but has been absorbed into motor memory through thousands of hours of practice. The vibratiuncles have fused. The serve has been served. And the driver arrives at the destination with no more awareness of their actions than a pianist has of individual finger movements during a sonata.

**The doctor who says "we don't know why it works."** A psychiatrist in Seoul prescribes an SSRI for depression. The patient improves. But when asked *why* the drug works, the psychiatrist admits the mechanism is only partially understood. We know the correlation — serotonin reuptake inhibition is regularly followed by mood improvement — but the causal story remains incomplete. Hume would smile: all you have is constant conjunction. The practicing physician shrugs: constant conjunction is enough to help the patient. But the researcher pushes deeper, because without understanding the mechanism, you can't predict who will respond, who won't, and who will experience side effects. Correlation gets you started. Mechanism gets you further.

---

Hume had stripped causation down to correlation, the self down to a bundle of perceptions, and science down to the description of observable patterns. He had done this so rigorously, so convincingly, that the foundations of psychology seemed to be nothing but habits of expectation. But not everyone was willing to accept this. A new generation of thinkers was already preparing a counterattack — arguing that Hume had gone too far, that the mind has innate structures that aren't derived from impressions, and that causation is more than a feeling. Thomas Reid in Scotland, Gottfried Leibniz in Germany, and Immanuel Kant in Königsberg were about to fight back. They would argue that Hume's razor, for all its precision, had cut away something essential — something the mind needs in order to function at all.
