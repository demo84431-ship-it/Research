# The Roman Age
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

You are a wealthy young Roman in the second century CE. Your father, a senator, has sent you to Alexandria to study at the Museum — the greatest center of learning in the known world. You walk through halls lined with papyrus scrolls, past rooms where physicians dissect human cadavers (a practice forbidden almost everywhere else in the empire), and into a lecture hall where a scholar is explaining that the nerves are hollow tubes through which tiny particles — "animal spirits" — travel from the brain to the muscles. You are impressed. This is real science: hands-on, empirical, detailed.

Then you return to Rome. And nobody cares.

Your fellow aristocrats are interested in aqueducts, military strategy, and law. They admire Greek learning the way a modern executive might admire a classical education — as a mark of culture, not a tool for discovery. The Romans built roads that still stand two thousand years later. They engineered sewage systems, heated baths, and siege machines that terrified their enemies. But they produced almost no original science. Why? How can a civilization be so brilliant at *doing* and so indifferent to *knowing*?

This paradox sits at the heart of the Roman contribution to psychology. The Romans did not advance Greek theory. But the thinkers who flourished under Roman rule — physicians, philosophers, mystics — did something equally important. They *translated* Greek ideas into systems that would dominate Western thought for over a thousand years. And some of those translations were spectacularly productive. Others were spectacularly wrong. Telling the difference would take centuries.

## The Philosopher's Life: When Theory Lost Its Appeal

### The Hellenistic Turn

After the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BCE, the Greek city-states collapsed into political chaos. The confident theoretical speculation of the classical period — Thales asking what the world is made of, Plato building entire metaphysical systems — gave way to something more desperate. People did not want to know the *nature* of reality. They wanted to know how to *survive* it.

Four schools of thought emerged, each offering a different answer to the same question: How should I live?

The **Skeptics**, led by **Pyrrho** (c. 365–c. 275 BCE), declared that knowledge is impossible. You cannot trust your senses, you cannot trust your reasoning, so you should suspend judgment on everything. Follow local customs. Do not rock the boat. The good life is the undisturbed life.

The **Cynics**, led by **Diogenes** (c. 412–323 BCE), took a more aggressive approach. They rejected civilization entirely. Diogenes lived in a ceramic jar, used the public square as his toilet, and masturbated in public to demonstrate his contempt for convention. The word "cynic" comes from the Greek for "dog-like." He was, in every sense, deliberately outrageous. His point was that social norms are arbitrary, and that natural simplicity is superior to artificial sophistication.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #1:** Diogenes chose poverty, public humiliation, and total social rejection as his path to wisdom. Modern psychology describes the "need to belong" as a fundamental human motivation (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Was Diogenes genuinely free from this need, or was his rebellion itself a form of social performance — defining himself *against* society rather than beyond it?

**Epicurus** (c. 341–270 BCE) offered a third path. He built on Democritean atomism to construct a philosophy of **moderate happiness**. Epicureanism was not the wine-and-revelry caricature that the word "epicurean" now suggests. Epicurus himself warned that rich food and excessive sex would make you miserable. The highest pleasure, he taught, was friendship. The greatest enemy was fear — especially fear of death and the gods. Since atoms disperse at death, there is no afterlife to dread. Since the gods do not intervene in human affairs, there is no divine wrath to appease. Freedom from fear, not indulgence of appetite, is the goal.

**Zeno of Citium** (c. 333–264 BCE) founded **Stoicism**, which became the most influential philosophy in the Roman world. Zeno taught that everything happens according to a divine plan. Your role is assigned; your destiny is fixed. **Virtue** consists in accepting your fate with indifference — whether it brings wealth or poverty, health or disease, life or death. Emperors like Marcus Aurelius, statesmen like Seneca, and slaves like Epictetus all embraced Stoicism. Its appeal crossed every social boundary because its promise was universal: you cannot control what happens to you, but you can control how you respond.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #2:** Stoicism has made a remarkable comeback in the 21st century — cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) draws directly on Stoic principles, and "stoic" self-help books fill airport shelves. Is this a genuine philosophical revival, or are we just repackaging ancient wisdom in modern branding? Does the source of an idea affect its validity?

### The Alexandrian Laboratories

While philosophers in Greece and Rome debated how to live, scientists in Alexandria were doing something unprecedented: cutting open dead bodies to see how they worked.

**Herophilus** of Chalcedon (335–280 BCE) founded the anatomical school at the Museum of Alexandria, where he performed systematic dissections of human cadavers — a practice permitted by the Ptolemaic rulers but banned almost everywhere else. He distinguished between **sensory nerves** and **motor nerves**, demonstrating that different types of nerves carry different types of signals. His colleague **Erasistratus** (c. 304–c. 250 BCE) explored the nervous system further, concluding that nerves transmit impulses — "motions" — from the senses to the brain and from the brain to the muscles. They speculated that these impulses were carried by **animal spirits**: tiny material particles flowing through hollow nerves.

This was not just anatomical cataloguing. It was the first attempt to explain *how* the body produces perception, movement, and thought in terms of physical mechanisms. The idea that the brain communicates with the body through channels carrying material signals would persist for two thousand years, reappearing in the mechanistic physiology of Descartes and Hobbes.

**Euclid** (c. 325–c. 265 BCE), curator of the Great Library, organized Egyptian, Babylonian, and Pythagorean mathematical knowledge into a deductive system — axioms from which theorems are logically derived. His *Elements* became the model for how to build a science: start with self-evident truths, derive consequences. Newton would use this format for his *Principia*. So would the behaviorist Clark Hull in the 20th century.

**Archimedes** (c. 287–c. 212 BCE) founded hydrostatics and organized the principles of mechanical equilibrium into a deductive system. Legend has it he discovered the principle of displacement while bathing and ran naked through the streets shouting "Eureka!" He put his theory to practical use designing catapults to defend Syracuse against the Roman siege. According to tradition, a Roman soldier killed him while he contemplated a geometric problem. The story may be apocryphal, but it captures something real: the Romans respected Greek learning but did not share the impulse to produce it.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #3:** Herophilus and Erasistratus discovered that nerves connect the brain to the rest of the body — a foundational insight for neuroscience. Yet their work was banned after Christian authorities objected to dissection. How many other scientific discoveries have been delayed or lost because of moral or religious objections to the *methods* required? Is there a pattern, or is each case unique?

## The Body's Balance: Galen and the Four Humors

**Galen** (c. 130–c. 200 CE) is one of the most important figures in the history of medicine — and one of the most consequential for psychology. Trained in Alexandria, he became personal physician to the Roman Emperor Marcus Aurelius. Like Aristotle, Galen believed that every organ and structure in the body has a purpose. His treatise *On the Usefulness of the Parts of the Body* systematically documented the function of every system he could observe.

Galen's most influential contribution to psychology was his development of Hippocrates' theory of bodily fluids into a theory of **personality types**. He proposed that four substances — **blood**, **yellow bile**, **black bile**, and **phlegm** — correspond to four temperaments:

| Humor | Element | Quality | Personality Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blood | Air | Hot & Moist | **Sanguine** — cheerful, optimistic |
| Yellow Bile | Fire | Hot & Dry | **Choleric** — irritable, emotional |
| Black Bile | Earth | Cold & Dry | **Melancholic** — sad, reflective |
| Phlegm | Water | Cold & Moist | **Phlegmatic** — calm, slothful |
*Table: Galen's four humors and their associated personality types — a system that dominated medicine for 1,500 years.*

Galen also advanced a detailed theory of **animal spirits** — refined vital particles produced in the brain that are responsible for perception and cognition. According to Galen, the heart distills **vital spirits** from the air we breathe. When these reach the brain's **rete mirabile** (the "marvelous net" of blood vessels at the base of the brain), they are further refined into animal spirits, which fill the ventricles (cavities) of the brain and the nerves. These spirits issue from the eyes in vision, travel through the nerves to the muscles in movement, and carry sensory information back to the brain.

This theory was wrong in almost every detail. The rete mirabile exists in goats and sheep — the animals Galen dissected — but not in humans. The nerves are not hollow tubes. Animal spirits are not material particles. Yet Galen's system dominated medical science for over a millennium and persisted into the 17th century, where it shaped the theories of Hobbes and Descartes.

> [!NOTE]
> **Key Terms:**
> - **Humors** (χυμοί): Bodily fluids whose balance was believed to determine health and temperament.
> - **Rete mirabile**: A network of blood vessels at the base of the brain, present in some animals but not humans — Galen's error had enormous consequences.
> - **Animal spirits**: Hypothetical fine particles in the brain and nerves, believed to carry sensory and motor signals.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #4:** Galen's theory of personality types has never been empirically validated, yet its categories (sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic) survive in everyday language. You probably know someone you would call "phlegmatic." Why do some discredited theories persist culturally while others disappear entirely? What makes a classification system *useful* even when it is *wrong*?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #5:** The Romans built infrastructure that lasted millennia but produced no original science. The Greeks produced foundational science but built almost nothing that survived. Is there an inherent tension between *doing* and *knowing* — between practical engineering and theoretical inquiry? Can a civilization excel at both, or does one always come at the expense of the other?

## Speaking of Empires and Ideas...

- **When someone says "I'm just not a math person,"** they are echoing the Roman attitude toward Greek theoretical learning. The Romans had access to Euclid, Archimedes, and every mathematical text in the Great Library. They used mathematics for engineering but never developed it further. Today, millions of people have access to every mathematical concept ever discovered, yet declare themselves "not math people." The Roman pattern — using knowledge practically while refusing to engage with it theoretically — is more common than you might think.

- **When your doctor prescribes treatment based on your "constitution"** — whether you run hot or cold, whether you are high-energy or sluggish — they are unconsciously channeling Galen. Modern medicine has replaced humors with hormones, neurotransmitters, and metabolic rates. But the underlying logic — that your body has a *type*, and that health depends on maintaining the right *balance* for that type — is Galenic to its core.

- **When a therapist uses CBT to help you examine your automatic thoughts,** the roots reach back to Stoicism. Epictetus taught that "it is not things that disturb us, but our judgments about things." Aaron Beck, the founder of CBT, acknowledged this debt directly. The Stoic insight — that you cannot control events, but you can control your interpretation of events — is the operational principle of modern cognitive therapy. You are doing Roman philosophy on a therapist's couch.

---

Galen gave the medieval world a complete system of the body. Plotinus gave it a complete system of the soul. Together, they would form the twin pillars of psychological thought for the next thousand years — until a restless Arab physician in Persia asked a question that would shake the foundations of both.
