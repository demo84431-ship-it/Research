# Chapter 3 · Module 2
# Augustine and the Fall of Rome
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

Around the year 400 CE, a man sits in his study in Hippo, a small city on the North African coast. He is writing furiously, filling papyrus with the most intimate self-examination the ancient world has ever seen. He confesses his sins — not in generalities, but with devastating specificity. He describes the pleasure he stole as a boy, the pears he did not need but took anyway, just for the thrill of theft. He catalogs his sexual appetites, his vanity, his years of "boiling over in fornications." He writes: "Give me chastity and continency — only not yet."

This is **Augustine of Hippo** (354–430 CE), and he is inventing something that did not exist before: the autobiography as psychological document. His *Confessions* is not a list of achievements. It is a forensic examination of his own mind — his emotions, his memories, his experience of time, his inability to will himself into goodness. In doing this, Augustine created a new way of understanding the self: not through external observation, but through *introspection*.

Augustine would shape Western psychology more profoundly than any thinker between Aristotle and Descartes. He did this not by advancing science, but by *replacing* it — substituting the empirical tradition of the Greek naturalists with a framework in which knowledge comes not from the senses but from God. The consequences would last for a thousand years.

---

## The World Falls Apart

### When Empires Collapse, Ideas Survive — In Different Forms

The Roman Empire did not fall in a day. It eroded over centuries — through government corruption, economic decay, plague, and relentless barbarian invasions. In 410 CE, the Visigoths under Alaric sacked Rome itself, an event that shocked the Roman world. The Vandals, the Huns, and a dozen other tribes followed. By 476 CE, when the last Western emperor, Romulus Augustus, was deposed, the commercial empire of the Romans had dissolved into isolated feudal enclaves.

Rome's population crashed from 1.5 million in the first century to 300,000 by the fifth century. By the end of the sixth, only about 50,000 people lived among the ruins. The money economy reverted to barter. Roads crumbled. Harbors silted. Libraries burned.

In this landscape of collapse, people turned to "other-world" philosophies that promised salvation beyond the material world. Neoplatonism — already influential — merged with a rising movement that would reshape Western civilization: **Christianity**.

### The Rise of Christianity

Christianity began as a persecuted minority faith. The Emperor Constantine's Edict of Milan in 313 CE granted religious toleration. By 380 CE, the Emperor Theodosius declared Christianity the official state religion and banned all pagan worship. This was catastrophic for science. Pagan learning was condemned alongside pagan religion. The Archbishop Theophilus of Alexandria destroyed much of the Great Library in 391 CE. **Hypatia** (c. 370–415 CE), a distinguished mathematician and astronomer, was murdered by a Christian mob — dragged into a church and torn apart with roof tiles, most likely on the orders of Cyril, who was later canonized as a saint.

The Emperor Justinian closed Plato's Academy in Athens in 529 CE and forbade the study of all "heathen learning." Scholars fled — first to Constantinople, then to Persia, carrying the works of Plato, Aristotle, Euclid, and Archimedes with them. There, Islamic scholars would preserve them. But in Western Europe, the lights went out.

> [!NOTE]
> **Key Terms:**
> - **Substance dualism**: The theory that mind and body are fundamentally different kinds of substance — the mind is immaterial and can survive bodily death.
> - **Neoplatonism**: A philosophical movement emphasizing the mystical and spiritual dimensions of Plato's thought, especially the idea that the soul is imprisoned in the material body.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #1:** The destruction of the Great Library of Alexandria is often cited as one of history's greatest intellectual tragedies. But libraries are *selections* — someone chose what to preserve and what to discard. What does it mean that we know about the Library's destruction but not about the thousands of works that were simply *ignored* by the scholars who curated it? Is neglect a form of destruction?

## Augustine: The Man Who Replaced Observation with Introspection

### "If I Am Mistaken, I Exist"

Augustine was born in North Africa to a pagan father and a Christian mother. He spent his youth in Carthage, pursuing rhetoric and pleasure with equal vigor. After years of debauchery, he converted to Christianity at age 31 and devoted the rest of his life to monastic devotion and theological writing.

Augustine developed a form of **substance dualism** that would later be associated — almost certainly influenced by — René Descartes. He argued that the soul is a special, simple spiritual substance, distinct from the material body and capable of surviving death. His proof was startlingly modern in its structure:

> The certainty that I exist, that I know it, and that I am glad of it, is independent of any imaginary and deceptive fantasies. They say, "Suppose you are mistaken?" I reply, "If I am mistaken, I exist." A non-existent being cannot be mistaken; therefore I must exist, if I am mistaken.

This argument — *cogito*-like in its logic — appears in *The City of God*, written five hundred years before Descartes published his *Meditations*. Augustine used it not to ground empirical science (as Descartes would) but to ground *faith*. The certain knowledge of your own existence leads, Augustine argued, to knowledge of God.

### The Inner Senses and Self-Knowledge

Augustine championed the **inner senses** as a source of self-knowledge. He argued that we can turn our attention inward and examine the contents of our own experience, thought, and memory. His *Confessions* is filled with acute psychological observations: on the nature of emotion, on the experience of time (he noticed that we perceive the present as a kind of thickness, not a point), on memory (some memories involve images, others — like memories of emotions — do not), and on dreams.

But Augustine was deeply hostile to the idea that sensory experience or reason could lead to genuine knowledge. He argued that both are impediments to truth, which comes only through divine illumination:

> Whatever a man has learned elsewhere is censured there [Holy Scripture] if it is harmful; if it is useful, it is found there.

This was not anti-intellectualism for its own sake. Augustine lived through the collapse of a civilization. He watched Rome burn. He saw the institutions that had sustained Greek learning crumble. In this context, the appeal of an eternal, unchanging source of truth — accessible not through fragile books and corruptible institutions but through direct communion with God — is not hard to understand. The cost, however, was enormous: the empirical tradition of the Greek naturalists was abandoned for over a thousand years.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #2:** Augustine's *Confessions* is often called the first autobiography in Western literature. But it is really the first *psychological* autobiography — a systematic examination of inner experience. Modern therapy asks you to do something similar: examine your thoughts, feelings, and memories with honesty. Is Augustine's introspection *therapy*? What is the difference between confessing to God and confessing to a therapist?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #4:** Augustine argued that the soul is a "thinking substance" distinct from the body. Modern neuroscience argues that thought is a product of brain activity. Yet both Augustine and neuroscientists agree on one thing: you can be certain that you are thinking. Is this shared certainty enough to build a psychology on? Or does the *explanation* of thought (spiritual vs. neural) matter more than the *experience* of it?

## Islam Saves the Books

### From Alexandria to Baghdad

While Western Europe descended into feudal isolation, the Islamic Empire — which within a century of Muhammad's death in 632 CE extended from Spain to Persia — became the repository of classical learning.

The conquerors initially destroyed what they found. In 642, Caliph Omar ordered the remains of the Great Library at Alexandria burned, reportedly claiming: "If these writings of the Greeks agree with the book of God, they are useless; if they disagree, they are pernicious." This attitude — remarkably similar to Augustine's — did not last. Around 800, Caliph Haroun-al-Raschid commissioned Arabic translations of Aristotle, Hippocrates, and Galen. His successors sent missions to Constantinople and India to find more works to translate. When the Islamic Empire conquered Persia, it acquired the Greek texts that Alexandrian scholars had carried there centuries earlier.

The result was a golden age of Islamic scholarship. **Avicenna** (Ibn Sina, c. 980–1037) produced a massive commentary on Aristotle's *De Anima* and developed the most influential version of the medieval theory of the **inner senses** — the idea that cognitive functions are located in the ventricles of the brain. His *Canon of Medicine* became the standard medical textbook across Europe and Asia for centuries.

### The Flying Man

Avicenna's most famous contribution to psychology is a thought experiment now known as the **flying man** argument. He asked his readers to imagine a person created fully formed, suspended in empty space, with all senses blocked — no sight, no touch, no sound, no proprioception. Would this person be aware of their own existence?

Avicenna's answer was yes. The person would affirm that they exist, without affirming the existence of any limb, organ, or external object. This, Avicenna argued, proves that the self is distinct from the body — a purely immaterial substance. The argument anticipates Descartes' *cogito* by six centuries.

**Averroës** (Ibn Roshd, 1126–1198), a judge and physician from Cordoba, took a very different position. He produced meticulous commentaries on Aristotle — earning the title "The Commentator" — and argued against Avicenna's Platonic modifications. Averroës maintained that the **active intellect** is immaterial and immortal, but denied that it belongs to any individual person. Since an immaterial substance has no spatial location, he reasoned, there is no way to distinguish one person's active intellect from another's. The active intellect is *one and the same* in all human beings.

This claim — known in the Christian world as the **Averroës heresy** — was condemned by the Bishop of Paris in 1270. It was philosophically radical: it implied that what you think of as *your* mind is not really yours at all. It is a single, shared, impersonal capacity. The idea resurfaces in various forms in modern discussions of collective intelligence and shared cognitive systems.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #3:** Avicenna's flying man argument and Descartes' *cogito* reach similar conclusions — "I know I exist because I am aware" — but for different purposes. Avicenna wanted to prove the soul is immaterial (a theological goal). Descartes wanted to ground empirical science (a philosophical goal). Can the same argument serve both purposes? Does the *intention* behind an argument change its meaning?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #5:** Averroës claimed that all humans share a single, identical active intellect. This was condemned as heresy. But consider: when you solve a mathematical problem, are you using *your* mind, or a capacity that is the same in everyone — like the capacity for language? If the intellect is shared, what makes *you* individually *you*?

## Speaking of Preservation and Loss...

- **When a university library digitizes its collection,** it is doing what Caliph Haroun-al-Raschid did in 800 CE — deciding that knowledge is worth preserving even when it comes from a different culture or tradition. The Islamic scholars who translated Aristotle into Arabic did not agree with everything he wrote. They preserved his work because they understood that *useful ideas do not require cultural endorsement*. The next time someone argues that a book or theory should be dismissed because of its origin, remember: you owe the survival of Western philosophy to translators who refused that logic.

- **When you catch yourself saying "I just know this is right" without evidence,** you are channeling Augustine. His doctrine of divine illumination — that true knowledge comes from God, not from the senses — sounds distant and theological. But stripped of its religious framing, it is remarkably common. How many of your beliefs do you hold because they *feel* certain, not because you have tested them? Augustine's error was not in trusting intuition. It was in *replacing* evidence with intuition.

- **When someone argues that "we are all connected" or that consciousness is universal,** they are echoing Averroës — whether they know it or not. The idea that individual minds share a common underlying capacity is not just mystical speculation. It appears in discussions of collective intelligence, shared cultural knowledge, and even the architecture of large language models. Averroës was condemned for the idea in 1270. Today, it might get you a TED talk.

---

The Islamic scholars saved the Greek texts. But it was the medieval Christians who decided what to do with them — and what to do with the people who used them. The story of that decision involves a castrated philosopher, a "dumb ox" who became a saint, and a theory of the mind hidden in the fluid-filled cavities of the brain.
