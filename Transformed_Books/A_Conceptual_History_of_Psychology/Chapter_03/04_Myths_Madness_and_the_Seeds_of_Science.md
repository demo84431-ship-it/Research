# Chapter 3 · Module 4
# Myths, Madness, and the Seeds of Science
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

You have almost certainly heard the story. During the medieval period, the Catholic Church — through the Inquisition — burned hundreds of thousands of women as witches. Scientists were persecuted, dissection was banned, and Western Europe sank into a thousand years of superstition and ignorance. Then the Renaissance arrived, and humanity was saved.

Almost none of this is true.

The real story of the medieval relationship between Church and science is stranger, more complicated, and far more interesting than the myth. The Church did not systematically persecute scientists. Human dissection was not banned — in many universities, it was *required*. The "witch craze" was real, but it reached its peak not in the medieval period but during the scientific revolution of the 16th and 17th centuries. And the medievals had a surprisingly sophisticated understanding of psychological disorders — one that in some ways was more nuanced than the "scientific" approaches that replaced it.

Understanding what actually happened in the medieval period matters for psychology because it reveals something important: the biggest obstacle to scientific progress was not religious persecution. It was something quieter, more pervasive, and harder to fight — a general *lack of interest* in testing ideas against reality.

---

## The Myths About the Medieval Church

### Myth 1: The Church Burned Scientists

It is commonly supposed that the medieval Church used the Inquisition to stifle scientific inquiry. The reality is more prosaic. The Inquisition was established by Pope Gregory IX in 1233 to combat the **Catharian heresy** — a movement that criticized the Church's wealth and power. The Catharians were dangerous not because of their scientific views but because they challenged the institutional hierarchy of the Church itself.

Few scientists are recorded as having been burned at the stake. The Church's standard response to problematic intellectual positions was excommunication and the expurgation of printed works — not execution. The Church was actively hostile to astrology (which was practiced alongside astronomy), but only one astrologer — Cecco d'Ascoli in 1327 — is recorded as having been condemned to death.

### Myth 2: The Church Banned Dissection

The Council of Tours issued a prohibition against human dissection in 1163, but this was not aimed at medical science. It was introduced to stop the practice of dismembering Crusaders' bodies before shipping them home. Many medical schools simply ignored the prohibition. The Hippocraticum Medicorum Collegium at Salerno — the leading medical school of the 12th century — continued dissection throughout. At the University of Bologna, human dissection was *mandatory*. Students resisted it, not the Church. Guglielmo da Saliceto published records of his dissections in 1275, and Mondino de' Luzzi did the same in 1316 — both without ecclesiastical consequence.

### Myth 3: The Church Burned Hundreds of Thousands of Witches

This is perhaps the most persistent myth. It is commonly claimed that "literally hundreds of thousands of women and children were condemned as witches and burned at the stake" during the medieval period. The numbers are greatly exaggerated — possibly by a factor of 100 percent (Maher & Maher, 1985). The Grand Inquisitor **Bernard Gui** (c. 1307–1323), one of the most active inquisitors on record, dealt with 930 cases in his lifetime. Of these, 80 accused were already dead when brought to trial. Only 43 were condemned to the stake.

For most of the medieval period, the Church did not even recognize the existence of witches. The famous *Malleus Maleficarum* ("The Witches' Hammer") — often blamed for the witch craze — was published in 1487, *after* the fall of Constantinople and during the Renaissance, which conventionally marks the *end* of the medieval period. The European witch craze was real, but it peaked in the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries — during the scientific revolution, not before it. Some historians have argued that the craze was actually *fueled* by the new scientific mentality, which encouraged the search for empirical evidence of demon possession.

> [!NOTE]
> **Key Terms:**
> - **Inquisition**: Office created by Pope Gregory IX in 1233 to combat heresy; its officers were generally Franciscan friars.
> - **Accidie** (ἀκηδία): A form of depression recognized by medieval theologians — characterized as debilitating apathy or disgust with life, distinct from melancholy.
> - **Empirik**: A medieval term for a practitioner who relied on observation and practical skill rather than theoretical knowledge — the ancestor of the modern empirical scientist.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #1:** The myth of medieval scientific persecution persists because it serves a useful narrative: it positions modern science as the heroic liberator from religious tyranny. But if the myth is largely false, what does its persistence tell us about how we construct *origin stories* for our own disciplines? Does psychology need a "dark age" to make its emergence feel triumphant?

## Accidie: A Medieval Diagnosis That Disappeared

### The Noonday Sickness

In the fifth century, the Christian theologian **John Cassian** listed **accidie** as the eighth deadly sin. It was a form of depression that monks experienced around midday — a debilitating apathy, a disgust with life, a desperate desire to be *anywhere* else. It came to be called the "noonday sickness" because it pulled monks away from their midday prayers.

Accidie was distinguished from **melancholie** — sadness associated with personal loss or feelings of inadequacy. Aristotle and Saint Paul had recognized both forms of depression, but considered only accidie sinful. The distinction was important: melancholy was a natural response to loss; accidie was a spiritual failure, a collapse of purpose.

The distinction survived for over a millennium. As late as the 17th century, the English physician **Richard Napier** (1559–1634) distinguished cases of accidie from melancholy in his clinical practice. He noted that accidie was more common among the leisured upper classes than among the laboring poor — a finding that resonates with modern research on depression and social class.

Eventually, the diagnosis of accidie fell out of use. It was dropped from the list of deadly sins and does not appear in any edition of the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* (DSM). But the condition it described has not disappeared. The psychiatrist **Robert Findley-Jones** (1986) has suggested that accidie can be discriminated from regular depression using modern clinical instruments, and that it appears to be especially prevalent among housewives and the unemployed in contemporary Australia.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #2:** Accidie was recognized as a distinct form of depression for over a thousand years, then dropped from the diagnostic vocabulary. If a condition can be *recognized*, *named*, and *treated* in one era and *forgotten* in the next, what does that say about the relationship between a diagnosis and the condition it describes? Is accidie a *real* disorder that we have stopped recognizing, or a *cultural construct* that lost its context?

### More Than Demons

The medieval understanding of psychological disorders was broader and more sophisticated than the popular image suggests. Most disorders were attributed to constitutional or environmental brain damage — "natural fools" (people with congenital intellectual disabilities) and those whose disorders resulted from head injuries. Other causes included:

- **Humoral imbalance** brought about by substances like strong wine
- **Emotional stress** from overwork or excessive study
- **Psychological and social causes**: marriage problems, frustrated love, failed ambition, guilt, jealousy, fear, bereavement, economic hardship, discord between parents and children, social abuse

This was not a narrow or unsophisticated framework. It recognized that psychological disorders have multiple causes — biological, psychological, and social — a position that modern psychology has only recently rediscovered under the banner of the **biopsychosocial model**.

Medieval treatment was correspondingly eclectic: rest, controlled diet, music, medicines, bloodletting, purgatives, amulets, counseling, and prayer. Most of these were grounded in a holistic conception of health derived from Hippocrates. It was only in the 18th and 19th centuries that "scientific" treatments like spinning, water-dousing, and electrical stimulation became popular — along with dangerous experiments in psychopharmacology involving iron, arsenic, and strychnine.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #3:** The medievals treated psychological disorders with rest, diet, music, and counseling. The 18th and 19th centuries replaced these with spinning, water-dousing, electrical stimulation, and strychnine. Which approach was more humane? Does "scientific" always mean "better" when it comes to treatment?

## The Empiriks: When Theory Refused to Meet Reality

### Why Nobody Checked

The most important failure of medieval science was not persecution. It was *indifference*. Throughout the medieval period, opportunities for empirical testing were routinely ignored — not because of clerical interference, but because of a general lack of interest in testing theories against observations.

Consider **Galen's rete mirabile** — the "marvelous net" of blood vessels at the base of the brain that he described in detail. This structure exists in ungulates (sheep, goats) — the animals Galen dissected. It does not exist in humans. Any physician who dissected a human brain could have discovered this in minutes. Yet Galen's description was repeated uncritically in medieval medical texts for over a thousand years. Vesalius finally demonstrated the error in the 16th century.

Consider **Aristotle's theory of falling bodies** — that heavier bodies fall faster than lighter ones. This is intuitively plausible but trivially easy to test. Drop two objects of different weights. Observe. Yet for nearly two thousand years, virtually no one bothered. Galileo finally tested it (or so the legend goes) by dropping weights from the Tower of Pisa.

The medieval pattern was consistent: accept the authority of the text, do not challenge it with observation. Medical degrees at Oxford were awarded on the basis of three public lectures on the works of Galen — not on hours spent in anatomy labs. If you needed surgery during the medieval period, you did not consult a physician. You went to a butcher — someone skilled in the practical art of the knife. These practitioners were called **empiriks**, and they were originally dismissed as charlatans who ignored scientific theory and based their practice on observation alone.

Science as we know it emerged when theorists *also* became empiriks — when they began subjecting their own and their predecessors' theories to empirical tests. This is what distinguishes the pioneers of the scientific revolution: they did not just *theorize*. They *tested*.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #4:** The empiriks — the medieval butchers-turned-surgeons — were dismissed as unscientific because they relied on experience rather than theory. Today, we often dismiss "practitioners" (therapists, coaches, counselors) who rely on clinical experience rather than published research. Is the modern tension between "evidence-based practice" and "practice-based evidence" a replay of the medieval divide between theorists and empiriks? Who gets to define what counts as knowledge?

## Anticipations: The Thinkers Ahead of Their Time

### Seeds That Took Centuries to Sprout

Not all medieval scholars were content to repeat Aristotle. A handful developed methodological principles that were centuries ahead of their time — and centuries ahead of anyone willing to use them.

**Robert Grosseteste** (c. 1168–1253), chancellor of the University of Oxford, produced detailed analyses of Aristotle's logic and wrote extensively on the confirmation and falsification of scientific theories.

**Roger Bacon** (c. 1214–1292), a Franciscan friar who studied at Oxford and Paris, articulated what he called the "first and second prerogatives of experimental science." The first: a theory developed to fit existing observations should be further tested by deriving *novel predictions* from it. The second: **experimentation** — controlled intervention — can supplement and extend naturalistic observation. Bacon is often unfairly caricatured as a necromancer because of his interest in alchemy. But his core insight — that theory must be tested by prediction and experiment — is the foundation of modern scientific method.

**Duns Scotus** (c. 1265–1308) and **William of Occam** (c. 1280–1349), both Franciscans, described methods of comparative causal analysis that would later be known as "Mill's methods." Occam enunciated the principle of **theoretical economy**: "Entities are not to be multiplied beyond necessity." When two theories are empirically equivalent — they predict the same observations — choose the simpler one. This principle, now known as **Occam's razor**, remains a foundational tool in science.

Occam's razor found its psychological expression in **Morgan's canon** — the principle, formulated by the comparative psychologist C. Lloyd Morgan in 1894, that psychologists should not explain animal behavior by reference to complex cognitive states if it can be explained in simpler terms. The connection between a 14th-century friar and a 19th-century psychologist is direct: both were trying to prevent unnecessary theoretical complexity.

> [!NOTE]
> **Key Terms:**
> - **Occam's razor**: The principle that the simplest adequate explanation is to be preferred — named after William of Occam.
> - **Morgan's canon**: The principle that animal behavior should not be explained by complex mental states if simpler explanations suffice — the psychological application of Occam's razor.
> - **Prerogatives of experimental science**: Roger Bacon's principles — test theories with novel predictions and supplement observation with controlled experimentation.

These thinkers were ahead of their time, and their work had little immediate impact. Few rushed to implement their principles. Many failed to follow their own prescriptions, regressing to appeals to authority and tradition. Most continued to assimilate Aristotle to Christian theology, and their development of Aristotle's more empirical writings brought them into conflict with the Church. Bacon was confined for years. Occam fled to Bavaria when the Inquisition examined his writings.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think #5:** Roger Bacon articulated the core principles of experimental science in the 13th century — three hundred years before Galileo and Vesalius put them into practice. What conditions need to be in place for a good idea to actually *take hold*? Is having the right idea enough, or does the idea need the right *social, economic, and institutional context* to become productive?

## Speaking of Myths and Methods...

- **When someone says "the Middle Ages were the Dark Ages,"** you now know better. The medieval period produced universities, the preservation of Greek philosophy, the first detailed theories of cognitive neuroscience (the inner senses), and sophisticated understandings of psychological disorder. The "darkness" was not ignorance — it was the *absence of empirical testing*. Ideas were generated, debated, and refined. They just were not *checked*.

- **When a company insists on "data-driven decision making,"** they are rejecting the medieval model and embracing the empirik model. The medieval approach was authority-driven: the boss says it, so it is true. The empirik approach was observation-driven: the data says it, so it is true. Most organizations still operate on a hybrid — some data, a lot of authority — which makes them more medieval than they would like to admit.

- **When you hear someone invoke Occam's razor** — "the simplest explanation is probably correct" — remember that this principle was formulated by a man who fled the Inquisition. Occam was not making a philosophical point about elegance. He was making a *strategic* point about survival: do not postulate more than you need, because unnecessary complexity draws unnecessary attention. The razor is not just a tool for good science. It is a tool for staying alive in dangerous intellectual environments.

---

The medieval period ended not with a bang but with a printing press. Around 1453 — the year Constantinople fell — Johann Gutenberg published his first Bible. Within fifty years, eight million volumes were in circulation. Within a century, twenty million. The explosion of printed text would do what the medieval Church could not: it would make it impossible to control what people read, what they thought, and what they dared to test against reality. The scientific revolution was about to begin — and psychology, though it did not know it yet, was about to become a science.
