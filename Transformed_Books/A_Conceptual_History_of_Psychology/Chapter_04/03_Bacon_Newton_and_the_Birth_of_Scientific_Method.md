In 1621, a powerful English politician was publicly disgraced. Francis Bacon — Lord Chancellor of England, knight, attorney general — was convicted of accepting bribes during his tenure as the highest legal officer in the land. He was fined £40,000, imprisoned in the Tower of London, and banished from court. His political career was over.

But Bacon had a second career — one that would prove far more consequential. In the years before his fall, he had been quietly writing a book that would reshape how humanity thinks about knowledge itself. The book was called *Novum Organum* — "New Instrument" — and its target was nothing less than the entire way the Western world had thought about science for two thousand years. Aristotle's logical works, collectively known as the *Organon*, had been the foundation of scientific method since antiquity. Bacon's book was a declaration of war on Aristotle's method — and a blueprint for something better.

Bacon did not discover a new planet or a new law of motion. What he discovered was something more fundamental: a *way of thinking about thinking*. And his most famous insight — that the human mind is systematically biased in ways we cannot see — was rediscovered by psychology three centuries later.

---

## The Method Before the Method

Before Bacon, there was no agreed-upon method for doing science. The Aristotelian approach was **deductive**: start with self-evident principles, then reason downward to specific conclusions. If your principles were true — and Aristotle believed they could be grasped by rational intuition — then your conclusions were guaranteed to be true.

This sounds rigorous, but it had a fatal flaw: it depended entirely on the quality of the starting principles. If your "self-evident" principle was actually wrong — as Aristotle's principles about motion, gravity, and the heavens turned out to be — then every conclusion built on it was wrong too. The entire medieval scientific tradition was a magnificent logical structure built on rotten foundations.

Bacon saw this clearly. He argued that the veneration of ancient authorities — Aristotle, Galen, Ptolemy — was the single greatest obstacle to scientific progress. He wrote:

> *"We can look in vain for advancement in scientific knowledge from the superinducing and grafting of new things on old. A fresh start must be made, beginning from the very foundations."*
> — Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum* (1620/1954, p. 51)

His solution was not to abandon logic, but to turn it upside down. Instead of starting with general principles and reasoning downward, science should start with specific observations and reason *upward*. This was **induction** — but not the simple kind.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Think about how you form beliefs about the world. Do you start with a general rule and apply it to specific cases (deduction)? Or do you notice patterns in specific cases and generalize from them (induction)? Which approach feels more natural to you? Which is more likely to lead you astray?

## Eliminative Induction: Not Just Counting Swans

Bacon was not interested in what he called "childish" induction — simply observing a few instances and generalizing. Watching ten white swans and concluding "all swans are white" is exactly the kind of reasoning that leads to error. The eleventh swan might be black.

Instead, Bacon championed **eliminative induction** — a method in which you systematically *rule out* alternative explanations until only one remains. He wrote:

> *"Induction that will be of use for the discovery and demonstration of the arts and sciences must analyse Nature by proper rejections and exclusions, and then, after a number of negatives, come to a conclusion on the affirmative instances."*
> — Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum* (1620/1994, p. 111)

This was a radical idea. Instead of looking for evidence that *confirms* your theory, you should look for evidence that *disproves* the alternatives. If you want to know what causes a disease, do not just study the sick. Study the healthy too, and find out what the sick have that the healthy do not. This logic — later formalized by John Stuart Mill as the "method of difference" — is the backbone of modern experimental design.

Bacon also introduced the idea of the **Instance of the Fingerpost** — a crucial observation that can adjudicate between competing theories. In rural England, signposts at crossroads had wooden fingers pointing toward nearby villages. Bacon's "fingerpost" was an observation that points toward the correct theory. For example: does light speed up or slow down when it passes from air into water? The particle theory of light predicts one answer; the wave theory predicts the other. One experiment can point to the truth.

This is exactly the logic behind modern hypothesis testing. You do not prove a theory by finding confirming evidence. You test it by trying to *break* it — and if it survives, you have reason to trust it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Bacon's most enduring insight was not about induction. It was about *doubt* — specifically, doubt about your own mind. Before you can do good science, you must understand the ways your thinking goes wrong. And there are, he argued, four systematic ways the human mind deceives itself.

## The Four Idols: Bacon's Psychology of Error

Bacon was one of the first thinkers to study the *psychology* of scientific reasoning — the cognitive biases that distort human thinking. He identified four categories of systematic error, which he called **idols of the mind**:

**Idols of the Tribe** (*Idola Tribus*) are biases built into human nature itself. We are pattern-seeking creatures, and we see patterns even where none exist. We project regularity onto nature — we assume the universe is simpler and more orderly than it actually is. We cling to theories we like, even when the evidence contradicts them. We trust familiar analogies over unfamiliar truths. Bacon called the result "wishful science" — and the term remains apt.

**Idols of the Cave** (*Idola Specus*) are the biases that come from your individual history — your education, your personality, your peculiar interests. Some people are drawn to novelty; others cling to the familiar. Some see differences everywhere; others see only similarities. Your "cave" is the unique tunnel of experience that shapes how you see the world. Bacon warned scientists to be especially suspicious of any idea they felt personally enthusiastic about.

**Idols of the Marketplace** (*Idola Fori*) are biases embedded in language itself. Words can trick us into thinking we understand something when we do not. The word "gravity" sounds like it names a thing — a force, a substance, an entity. But it may just be a label for a mathematical relationship. Common language, Bacon argued, is a poor tool for describing nature, and scientists must develop precise technical vocabulary to avoid being misled by everyday words.

**Idols of the Theatre** (*Idola Theatri*) are biases created by established theoretical systems — the philosophical "plays" that are performed in the theatre of education. Aristotle's physics, Galen's medicine, Ptolemy's astronomy — these were not just theories. They were entire worldviews, taught as truth in every university, reinforced by the Church, and defended by generations of scholars. To question them was to question the institution itself.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Can you identify an "idol of the cave" in your own thinking — a bias that comes from your personal background or education? What about an "idol of the marketplace" — a word or concept that you use without really knowing what it means? How would you even notice these biases if they are, by definition, invisible to you?

## Science as a Social Enterprise

Bacon did not just imagine science as a method. He imagined it as a *community*. He recognized that individual scientists, no matter how brilliant, are limited by their own idols. The solution was collaboration — scientists working together, sharing results, checking each other's work.

In his utopian fiction *New Atlantis* (1627), Bacon described a society of scientists and technologists devoted to knowledge and discovery. He petitioned King James I to finance cooperative research projects. Although the king declined, Bacon's vision eventually became reality. The Royal Society of London was founded in 1662, followed by the Académie des Sciences in Paris in 1666, and similar institutions across Europe.

These societies did something revolutionary: they created the *experimental report*. When the Royal Society was small, members demonstrated their experiments in person. As membership grew, they developed a convention: members should write a "recipe" — a description of their methods detailed enough for anyone to reproduce the experiment. This was the birth of **experimental replication** — the idea that a scientific claim is only valid if someone else can get the same result by following the same procedure.

The *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, first published in 1665, became the model for all scientific journals that followed. The modern psychology paper — with its methods, results, and discussion sections — is a direct descendant of those 17th-century "recipes."

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Bacon's greatest legacy was not any particular scientific discovery. It was the idea that science is a *social* enterprise — a collective, self-correcting process in which individual biases are checked by communal scrutiny. This idea is the foundation of peer review, replication, and the entire institution of modern science.

## Newton: The Great Synthesis

Isaac Newton (1642–1727) was born in Lincolnshire, England, the same year Galileo died. He was educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he received his degree in 1665. When the plague forced the university to close, Newton retreated to his family farm and spent two years in what may be the most productive period of solitary thought in history. During this time, he developed the binomial theorem, invented calculus, built the first reflecting telescope, and began working on his theory of universal gravitation.

Newton's *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* (*Principia*), published in 1687, unified the work of Galileo and Kepler into a single, elegant system. Galileo had described how objects move on Earth. Kepler had described how planets move in the sky. Newton showed that both were governed by the same law: **universal gravitation**. Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force proportional to their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The same force that makes an apple fall makes the moon orbit the Earth.

This was the **Newtonian synthesis** — the unification of terrestrial and celestial mechanics under a single set of laws. It was the greatest intellectual achievement of the Scientific Revolution, and it established the paradigm of **mechanistic explanation**: the idea that the universe operates like a vast clockwork machine, governed by fixed, mathematical laws of cause and effect.

Newton also contributed to optics, demonstrating that white light is a mixture of colors, and developed a **corpuscularian theory of light** — treating light as a stream of tiny particles. He was appointed president of the Royal Society in 1703 and held the position until his death in 1727.

### The Method Debate: Did Newton Cheat?

Here is a puzzle that reveals something important about scientific method. Newton claimed he had followed Bacon's method of inductive ascent — carefully observing phenomena, then generalizing from them:

> *"Particular propositions are inferred from the phenomena, and afterwards rendered general by induction. Thus it was that the impenetrability, the mobility, and the impulsive force of bodies, and the laws of motion and gravitation, were discovered."*
> — Isaac Newton, *Principia* (1687/1969, p. 547)

But this was not actually what Newton did. His first law of motion — "every body continues in a state of rest, or of uniform motion in a right line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed upon it" — cannot be established by induction, because no body actually moves in a straight line. Every body is subject to external forces. Newton's law was not derived from observation. It was a *bold theoretical postulate* — a speculative leap that happened to be spectacularly successful.

This raises a question that still haunts the philosophy of science: is good science the product of careful, Baconian induction? Or does it require the kind of creative, speculative leap that Newton actually made? The answer, as it turns out, is: both. And the tension between them — between cautious observation and bold hypothesis — has never been fully resolved.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Think about a time you made a bold guess about something — a hypothesis about why a friend was upset, or what was causing a problem at work. Was your guess based on careful evidence? Or was it a creative leap? Did it turn out to be right? What does that tell you about how knowledge is actually created?

## Speaking of Methods and Mirrors...

- A clinical psychologist in Nairobi notices that her patients from rural areas respond differently to therapy than those from the city. She wonders whether her therapeutic framework — developed in a Western context — is an "idol of the theatre" that does not apply universally.

- A software engineer in Berlin builds an AI model that predicts customer behavior with 95% accuracy on test data. When deployed, it drops to 60%. The model was like Newton's first law: it looked elegant in theory, but the real world has forces the model did not account for.

- A teacher in Mexico City asks her students to design an experiment to test whether background music helps concentration. One student suggests testing only students who like music. Another suggests testing both music-lovers and non-listeners. The second student, without knowing it, has reinvented Bacon's eliminative induction.

Bacon gave science its method. Newton gave science its greatest achievement. But neither of them answered a question that had been lurking since Galileo split the world into primary and secondary qualities: if the physical world is just matter in motion — shapes, sizes, and movements — then where does the *mind* fit in? What is consciousness? How does the brain produce thoughts, feelings, and experiences? A French philosopher, working in a stove-heated room, was about to give an answer that would define psychology's central problem for the next four centuries.
