# Chapter 4 · Module 5
# Materialism, Empiricism, and the Reflex
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

A drunken soldier once cut the head off a turkey-cock with a sabre. The animal stayed upright. Then it walked. Then it ran. When it hit a wall, it turned around, beat its wings, and kept running. It finally fell down — not because it realized it was dead, but because the mechanical system that had been keeping it upright finally ran out of coordination.

This grotesque little scene, reported by La Mettrie in 1748, was more than a curiosity. It was evidence for a radical claim: that the body can operate without a mind. If a turkey can run without a head — without a brain, without consciousness, without anything that Descartes would have called a "soul" — then what, exactly, is the mind *for*? Is it necessary at all? Or is it just something that happens when the machinery gets complex enough?

By the mid-18th century, Descartes' elegant compromise — a mechanical body plus an immaterial soul — was under siege from all sides. A Dutch philosopher dissolved the mind-body distinction entirely. A French physician declared that humans are nothing but machines. An English political theorist argued that all knowledge comes from the senses. And a Scottish doctor discovered that a headless frog can still scratch an itch. Between them, they laid the foundations of materialism, empiricism, and the reflex — the three pillars on which modern psychology would eventually stand.

## Spinoza: One Substance, Two Faces

Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) was born in Amsterdam to a Portuguese Jewish family. He was excommunicated from the Jewish community at the age of 23 for his unorthodox views — views that were equally unwelcome in Christian circles. He spent the rest of his life grinding lenses and writing philosophy, living in deliberate simplicity.

Spinoza's solution to the mind-body problem was breathtakingly elegant: he denied that there *were* two substances. There is only *one* substance in the universe — which he called "God" or "Nature" — and mind and body are not two different things. They are two *attributes* of the same thing, two ways of understanding the same underlying reality. Think of it like a coin: the heads and tails are not two separate objects. They are two sides of one coin. You cannot have one without the other.

This position is called **monism** — the view that there is only one kind of substance. In Spinoza's version, mental events and physical events are perfectly correlated because they are the *same event* described in two different ways. When your brain fires neurons (a physical event), you feel an emotion (a mental event). These are not cause and effect. They are two descriptions of the same occurrence.

This dissolved the interaction problem entirely. Descartes had struggled to explain how an immaterial mind could move a material body. Spinoza's answer: it cannot, because there *is* no immaterial mind separate from the body. The mind just *is* the body, experienced from the inside.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Think about a moment of intense fear — your heart racing, your palms sweating, your muscles tensing. Was the fear a *mental* event that caused the *physical* symptoms? Or were the physical symptoms and the fear the *same thing*, described in two different ways? What difference does it make which interpretation you choose?

## The Mind-Body Solutions: Occasionalism, Parallelism

Not everyone followed Spinoza. The French philosopher Nicolas Malebranche (1638–1715) proposed a different solution: **occasionalism**. According to Malebranche, mind and body do not interact at all. They are completely separate substances that never touch. When you decide to raise your arm, your mind does not cause your arm to move. Instead, God directly causes your arm to move on the *occasion* of your decision. Every apparent mind-body interaction is actually a direct act of God. Your decision to raise your arm and the arm's rising are perfectly correlated because God synchronizes them — like two clocks that always show the same time because God keeps them in sync.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) proposed yet another solution: **pre-established harmony**. According to Leibniz, mind and body are like two clocks that were perfectly synchronized at the moment of creation and have been running in harmony ever since. Your mental life and your bodily life are perfectly correlated, not because they interact, but because God designed them from the beginning to run in parallel. Every mental event corresponds to a physical event, not because one causes the other, but because they were pre-programmed to coincide.

These solutions sound strange, but they were attempts to answer a genuine problem. If mind and body are truly different substances — as Descartes claimed — then how do they interact? Occasionalism and parallelism were honest attempts to deal with the puzzle. They were also, from a scientific standpoint, useless. A theory that attributes every mind-body correlation to God's direct intervention is not testable, not predictive, and not productive. It solves the philosophical problem by abandoning the scientific one.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The history of the mind-body problem is a history of elegant solutions that do not actually explain anything. Spinoza's monism, Malebranche's occasionalism, Leibniz's pre-established harmony — each is philosophically ingenious. None of them generates a single testable prediction. The lesson for psychology: a theory that cannot be wrong cannot be useful.

## La Mettrie: Man Is a Machine

Julien Offroy de La Mettrie (1709–1751) was a French military physician who had no patience for philosophical elegance. He wanted answers — and he was willing to follow the evidence wherever it led, even if it led to heresy.

During the siege of Freiburg in the Franco-Austrian war, La Mettrie contracted a fever. The fever distorted his thinking, altered his emotions, and disrupted his perception. This experience convinced him that the mind is not a separate substance at all. It is a product of the body — specifically, of the brain. If a fever could change how he thought and felt, then thought and feeling must be functions of the physical organism.

In 1748, he published *Man a Machine* (*L'Homme Machine*), in which he declared:

> *"I believe thought to be so little incompatible with organized matter, that it seems to be one of its properties, like electricity, motive power, impenetrability, extension, etc."*
> — La Mettrie, *Man a Machine* (1748/1996, p. 35)

This was the most radical claim yet. Descartes had said animals are machines but humans have souls. La Mettrie said *everyone* is a machine — humans included. The difference between humans and animals is not a difference in kind (soul vs. no soul). It is a difference in the *complexity of organization*. A human brain is to an ape's brain as a complex clock is to a simple one. Same stuff. Different arrangement.

La Mettrie argued that substances like opium, wine, and coffee can alter thought and emotion — evidence that mental states are physically caused. He pointed to comparative anatomy: the human brain is larger and more convoluted than any animal's, and this complexity explains the difference in mental capacity. He even predicted that apes could learn language if properly trained — an idea that would not be tested until the 20th century, when Allen and Beatrice Gardner taught sign language to the chimpanzee Washoe.

La Mettrie was also surprisingly optimistic about the moral implications of materialism. If humans are machines, he argued, they are no worse than animals — and animals, he noted, rarely engage in religious wars or systematic torture. Materialism does not lead to moral nihilism. It leads to humility.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** If you accepted that you are a machine — a very complex one, but a machine nonetheless — would it change how you treat other people? Would it change how you think about free will? About responsibility? About what makes life meaningful?

## Hobbes: All Knowledge Comes from the Senses

Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) shared La Mettrie's materialism but arrived at it from a different direction. Hobbes was an **empiricist** — he believed that all knowledge comes from sense experience. There are no innate ideas, no rational intuitions, no knowledge prior to experience. Everything you know, you learned through your senses.

> *"The original of them all is that which we call sense; for there is no conception in a man's mind which hath not at first, totally, or by parts, been begotten upon the organs of sense."*
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* (1651/1966, p. 1)

This was a direct challenge to Descartes, who had argued that some knowledge — ideas of God, axioms of geometry, the laws of logic — is innate, present in the mind before any experience. Hobbes denied this entirely. The mind at birth is a blank slate. Everything written on it was put there by the senses.

Hobbes also made a claim that would become central to empiricist psychology: thinking is just *faint sensing*. When you imagine a tree, you are not accessing some abstract mental category. You are having a weakened version of the sensory experience of seeing a tree. "Imagination," Hobbes wrote, "is nothing more than decaying sense." This idea — that cognition and sensation differ in degree, not in kind — became the foundation of British empiricism and, eventually, of associationist psychology.

Hobbes also proposed a mechanistic account of the **association of ideas**. Ideas that were experienced together in sense perception become linked in memory. The smell of a particular food and the pleasure of eating it become associated, so that the smell alone can later trigger the pleasure. This was the first clear articulation of what would later become the **law of contiguity** — one of the fundamental principles of learning theory.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Think about a smell that instantly transports you to a specific memory — maybe the scent of a particular food, or a perfume, or the air after rain. Is this an example of Hobbes's "decaying sense"? Is the memory just a faint replay of the original experience? Or is it something more?

## The Reflex Arc: Whytt's Headless Frog

The final piece of the puzzle came from an unlikely source: a Scottish doctor cutting the heads off frogs.

Robert Whytt (1714–1766) taught medicine at the University of Edinburgh. He was interested in a simple question: can a body respond to stimulation without a brain?

The answer, it turned out, was yes. Whytt demonstrated that decapitated frogs continue to respond to stimulation — as long as the spinal cord remains intact. A headless frog will use its legs to brush away an acid-soaked tissue applied to its skin, just as an intact animal uses its legs to rid itself of fleas. Decapitation, Whytt found, does not eliminate reflexive behavior. It can actually *enhance* it.

Whytt introduced the concept of the **stimulus** — defined as the application of any form of physical energy to a nerve — into the theoretical vocabulary of psychology. He identified a range of innate reflexive behaviors: digestion, coughing, sneezing, penile erection. And he made a discovery that anticipated Pavlov by more than a century: certain originally neutral stimuli can acquire the capacity to generate reflexes by *association* with their natural triggers:

> *"Thus the sight, or even the recalled idea of grateful food causes an uncommon flow of spittle into the mouth of a hungry person; and the seeing of a lemon cut produces the same effect in many people."*
> — Robert Whytt (cited in Boakes, 1984, p. 95)

This is **conditioned salivation** — the same phenomenon that Pavlov would later make the centerpiece of his experimental program. Whytt observed it in 1751. Pavlov published his first studies in 1903. The gap between observation and systematic investigation was over 150 years.

Whytt suggested that the spinal cord contains an unconscious "sensitive soul" capable of making adaptive responses to stimulation. This idea — that the nervous system can process information and generate intelligent behavior without consciousness — was controversial, but it was correct. Modern neuroscience confirms that the spinal cord contains complex circuits capable of generating coordinated motor responses without input from the brain.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The discovery of the reflex arc — stimulus → spinal cord → response — was the foundation of behaviorism. When Watson argued that all behavior can be explained as chains of stimulus-response connections, he was building on a principle that Whytt had demonstrated with headless frogs. The reflex arc is the simplest unit of behavior. Everything else is built on top of it.

## Speaking of Machines and Empiricists...

- A neurosurgeon in Mumbai stimulates a patient's brain during surgery. The patient's arm moves — but the patient did not *decide* to move it. The surgeon has just demonstrated, in a modern operating room, what Descartes described in 1649: the body can move without the mind's consent.

- A child in Lagos learns to associate the sound of a bell with mealtime. After a few weeks, the child salivates at the sound of the bell alone. This is Whytt's conditioned salivation, Hobbes's association of ideas, and Pavlov's classical conditioning — all in one everyday example.

- A philosopher in Berlin argues that consciousness is an "illusion" created by brain processes. A philosopher in São Paulo argues that consciousness is a fundamental feature of reality. They are replaying, without knowing it, the debate between La Mettrie and Spinoza — materialism vs. monism, brain-as-producer vs. mind-as-attribute.

The Scientific Revolution had dismantled the medieval worldview and replaced it with a new one: the universe as a machine, governed by mathematical laws, accessible through observation and experiment. The body was a machine. The mind was... something else. Or maybe not. The debate between dualists, materialists, and monists had created a landscape of possibilities, but no consensus. What was needed now was a different kind of progress — not more philosophy, but more *data*. In the next century, a new generation of scientists would stop arguing about what the mind *is* and start measuring what it *does*. The age of experimentation was about to begin.
