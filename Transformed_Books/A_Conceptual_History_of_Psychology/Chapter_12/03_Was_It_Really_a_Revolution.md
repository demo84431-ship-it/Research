# Chapter 12 · Module 3
# Was It Really a Revolution? Paradigm Shifts and Theoretical Meaning
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In 1962, a philosopher of science named Thomas Kuhn published a book called *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*. It changed how scientists thought about their own work. Kuhn argued that science does not progress by the steady accumulation of facts. Instead, it proceeds through **paradigm shifts** — moments when one theoretical framework is overthrown and replaced by a fundamentally different one. The Copernican revolution replaced the Ptolemaic model of the solar system. Einstein's relativity replaced Newtonian mechanics. In each case, the old paradigm did not just get updated. It got abandoned.

The pioneers of the cognitive revolution loved Kuhn's book. As James Jenkins later recalled, "everyone toted around their little copy of *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*." Jerome Bruner, George Miller, Ulric Neisser, Allen Newell, and Herbert Simon saw themselves as revolutionaries — overthrowing the behaviorist paradigm and replacing it with a cognitive one. The narrative was satisfying: behaviorism was the old order, cognitive psychology was the new, and the anomalies that behaviorism could not explain — language, attention, problem-solving — were the cracks that brought the old paradigm down.

But was it really a revolution in the Kuhnian sense? Did behaviorism actually die? And if it did not, what exactly changed?

## The Paradigm That Would Not Die

Here is a fact that complicates the revolution narrative: behaviorism did not die. In the 1950s and 1960s, while cognitive psychology was being developed, Skinner's radical behaviorism was *expanding* institutionally. The *Journal for the Experimental Analysis of Behavior* was founded in 1958. The *Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis* followed in 1968. Division 25 of the APA was designated as the Division of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior. Behavior modification programs were being implemented in schools, hospitals, and industry. Skinner himself was becoming one of the most famous psychologists in the world.

This is not what a Kuhnian paradigm shift looks like. When Einstein's relativity replaced Newtonian mechanics, Newtonian physics did not continue to expand. It was absorbed as a special case. But behaviorism was not absorbed into cognitive psychology. It continued alongside it — with its own journals, its own professional organizations, its own research programs, and its own institutional support.

Moreover, the two paradigms did not even share a common set of core theoretical commitments that could be directly compared. Watson, Tolman, Hull, and Skinner disagreed about almost everything — the role of cognition, the nature of learning, the proper scientific method. There was no single "behaviorist paradigm" to overthrow. Similarly, Chomsky, Broadbent, Bruner, Miller, Newell, Simon, and Neisser disagreed about many fundamental issues. There was no single "cognitive paradigm" to install.

What happened was not a paradigm shift in the strict Kuhnian sense. It was something more subtle and, in some ways, more interesting.

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Skinner's radical behaviorism expanded institutionally during the same period that cognitive psychology was being developed. Does a scientific field have to die for a revolution to have occurred? Or can two incompatible frameworks coexist?

## What Actually Changed: From Intervening Variables to Hypothetical Constructs

The real change was not in what psychologists studied — it was in how they conceived of their theoretical claims about the mind.

Neobehaviorists like Hull and Tolman had introduced theoretical constructs — drive, habit strength, cognitive maps — to explain behavior. But they insisted that these constructs must be **operationally defined** in terms of observable stimuli and responses. "Habit strength" was defined as the number of reinforced trials. "Drive" was defined as hours of deprivation. The theoretical construct was not allowed to have any meaning beyond its operational definition.

This created a problem. If "habit strength" just means "number of reinforced trials," then saying "the rat pressed the lever because of its habit strength" is just saying "the rat pressed the lever because it had been reinforced many times." The theory adds nothing. It is, as Skinner pointed out, circular.

Cognitive psychologists broke free from this circularity by treating their theoretical constructs as **hypothetical constructs** with **independently meaningful content** — content that is not reducible to operational definitions. When a cognitive psychologist talks about "working memory," they are not just describing a pattern of observable behavior. They are referring to a real cognitive system with specific properties — limited capacity, rapid decay, phonological encoding — that can be studied through multiple methods (behavioral experiments, brain imaging, computational modeling). The construct has "surplus meaning" beyond any single operational measure.

The distinction comes from MacCorquodale and Meehl's classic 1948 paper. They distinguished between **intervening variables** (which are just shorthand for patterns in observable data) and **hypothetical constructs** (which refer to real internal states with meaning beyond the data). Neobehaviorists treated their theoretical terms as intervening variables. Cognitive psychologists treated theirs as hypothetical constructs.

This was not just a philosophical technicality. It was a fundamental change in what it meant to do psychological science. It meant that psychologists could postulate internal mental states — representations, rules, schemas, memory systems — and study them as real entities, not just as convenient descriptions of behavior.

| Feature | Neobehaviorist Theories | Cognitive Theories |
|---|---|---|
| **Theoretical terms** | Operationally defined | Independently meaningful |
| **Example** | "Habit strength" = reinforced trials | "Working memory" = limited-capacity system |
| **Status of internal states** | Intervening variables (logical devices) | Hypothetical constructs (real entities) |
| **Meaning** | Reducible to observables | "Surplus meaning" beyond observables |
| **Explanation** | Circular (re-describes data) | Substantive (postulates mechanisms) |

*Table: The theoretical shift from neobehaviorism to cognitive psychology.*

> Hull defined "concept formation" as the ability to discriminate stimuli and associate verbal labels with them. But a parrot can do that without understanding concepts. What is the difference between labeling and understanding? Is it possible to define understanding without circularity?

## The Missing Cognitive Content

The difference between neobehaviorist and cognitive theories was not just about operational definition. It was about content. Neobehaviorist theories, even the most "liberalized" ones, lacked genuinely cognitive content.

Consider Hull's account of concept formation. Hull defined a concept in terms of stimulus discrimination and verbal association. A person "grasps" the concept of "dog" when they can discriminate dogs from non-dogs and associate the verbal label "dog" with them. But as critics pointed out, a parrot can achieve this form of discriminative learning without any mastery of concepts or linguistic meaning. Hull's "concept" was not really a concept at all. It was a conditioned verbal response.

Or consider Osgood's "representational mediation process." Osgood defined a representation as an internal stimulus that elicits a behavior normally elicited by an external stimulus, in the absence of that stimulus. So the word "hammer" represents hammers because it elicits the same response that hammers do. But this is not what representation means. The word "hammer" represents hammers because it *refers to* them, *means* them — not because it causes the same response. A word can represent something without causing any response at all. You can think about hammers without reaching for one.

Cognitive psychologists defined their constructs differently. When Chomsky talked about "grammar," he meant a system of rules that generates sentences. When Bruner talked about "strategies," he meant flexible, goal-directed plans for processing information. When Miller talked about "chunks," he meant organized units of meaning. These constructs had genuine cognitive content — they described what the mind *does*, not just what the body responds to.

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Osgood said the word "hammer" represents hammers because it elicits the same response. Chomsky said language is governed by rules, not responses. Who is right? Can you represent something without responding to it?

## The Strong Continuity Debate

There is one more piece to this puzzle. Behaviorists maintained what Greenwood calls **strong continuity** between cognitive processes and reflexive associative processes. In other words, they believed that human cognition is just a more complex version of the same basic processes — conditioning, association, reinforcement — that govern animal behavior. This is why they could claim that studying rats pressing levers tells you something important about human language and thought.

Cognitive psychologists denied this. Chomsky argued that language is qualitatively different from conditioned behavior — it is governed by rules, not associations, and it cannot be learned through reinforcement. Lashley argued that complex serial behavior requires central cognitive control structures that are fundamentally different from stimulus-response chains. Bruner argued that concept formation involves active hypothesis testing, not passive association.

But — and this is important — denying strong continuity between cognition and conditioning did not require denying strong continuity between *human and animal psychology*. Cognitive psychologists could (and did) argue that animals also have cognitive capacities — that rats build cognitive maps, that primates use tools and solve problems, that birds form representations. They denied that all cognition can be reduced to conditioning, but they affirmed that cognition exists across species. This was a more nuanced position than either the behaviorists' (all cognition is conditioning) or the earlier structuralists' (only humans have real cognition).

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Behaviorists said human cognition is just complex conditioning — the same process that operates in rats. Cognitive psychologists said cognition is qualitatively different from conditioning. What does the evidence from animal cognition (tool use in primates, cognitive maps in rats, problem-solving in birds) tell you about this debate?

## Speaking of Paradigms...

The question "was it really a revolution?" matters because it tells us something about how science actually works. The clean Kuhnian narrative — old paradigm dies, new paradigm is born — is appealing but often misleading. In reality, scientific change is messier. Old ideas do not die cleanly; they persist, transform, and sometimes return in new forms. Behaviorism did not die. It was supplemented. The cognitive revolution did not overthrow behaviorism; it added something that was missing.

In economics, the "Keynesian revolution" of the 1930s did not eliminate classical economics. Both frameworks coexist, each illuminating different aspects of economic behavior. In biology, the "Darwinian revolution" did not eliminate all teleological thinking — evolutionary biology still speaks of "function" and "purpose." In medicine, evidence-based practice did not eliminate clinical judgment — it provided a framework for integrating it with data.

The cognitive revolution was a revolution in the same sense: not a clean overthrow, but a fundamental expansion of what psychology could study and how it could study it. Before the revolution, psychology was limited to observable behavior. After it, psychology could study the mind.

> [!TIP] **Stop and Think**
> Kuhn argued that scientific revolutions involve the replacement of one paradigm by another. The history of psychology suggests something different: paradigms coexist, each useful for different purposes. Is this a weakness of psychology as a science, or a strength?

---

The cognitive revolution was not a Kuhnian paradigm shift. Behaviorism survived. But something real did change: psychologists began to treat cognitive states and processes as legitimate objects of scientific study, defined by independently meaningful theoretical constructs rather than circular operational definitions. This was not a revolution in the sense of one theory replacing another. It was a revolution in the sense of a new kind of science becoming possible.
