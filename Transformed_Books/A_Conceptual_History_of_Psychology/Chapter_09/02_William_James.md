# Chapter 09 · Module 2
# William James — The Stream of Consciousness
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In 1867, a 25-year-old American traveled to Germany, hoping that mineral baths might relieve his chronic back pain. His name was William James, and he was a mess. He had tried art, chemistry, physiology, and medicine. He had abandoned each one. He had gone on a zoological expedition to the Amazon and contracted smallpox. His health was terrible. His eyesight was failing. He could not sleep. And he was depressed — so depressed that he had begun to think about suicide.

In Germany, James visited the laboratories of Fechner, Helmholtz, and Wundt. He came away with a new conviction: "perhaps the time has come for psychology to begin to be a science." Returning to America, he completed his medical degree in 1869 and immediately resolved never to practice medicine. His depression deepened. He became suicidal.

Then something extraordinary happened. James cured himself — not with medicine, not with therapy, but with an act of will. He read the French philosopher Charles Renouvier, who argued that free will is a legitimate concept. James decided to believe in free will. His first act of free will, he later wrote, was "to believe in free will." The depression lifted. He never fully recovered his health, but he recovered his purpose.

William James would go on to write the most influential psychology textbook of the 19th century, found the philosophical movement of pragmatism, and shape the development of American psychology more than any other single figure. He did this despite hating laboratory work, despising most of his German colleagues' experiments, and calling psychology a "nasty little subject" in his later years.

## The Principles of Psychology

James was offered a position as instructor in physiology at Harvard Medical School in 1872. In 1875, he persuaded President Charles Eliot to let him introduce a course on "The Relations Between Physiology and Psychology." That same year, he got money from Harvard to set up a laboratory for experimental demonstrations — though as G. Stanley Hall later pointed out, it included no more than "a metronome, a device for whirling a frog, a horopter chart, and one or two bits of apparatus" stored in a stairwell closet.

In 1878, James contracted with Henry Holt to write a book on psychology. It took him twelve years. The *Principles of Psychology* was published in two volumes in 1890 — nearly 1,400 pages of brilliant, sprawling, digressive prose. It was widely read by psychologists and the educated public alike. For many years, it was the standard psychology textbook in most American and many European universities.

Not everyone approved. Wundt dismissed it as "literature . . . not psychology." Titchener condemned it. Later functional and behaviorist psychologists were less than enthusiastic about James's commitment to introspection, free will, and spiritualism. But the book's influence was enormous. It anticipated many of the later developments of scientific psychology, from functionalism to behaviorism to cognitive psychology.

James himself was ambivalent about psychology. He hated laboratory work and was not impressed by the work of German pioneers. He described Fechner's psychophysics as destined to amount to "nothing" and Müller's experimental work as "brutal." He became disillusioned with what he called the "nasty little subject" but worked conscientiously throughout his life to promote its development.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** James took twelve years to write the *Principles of Psychology*. He described the process as agonizing. But the book became the most influential psychology text of its era. Does great work require suffering? Or is that just a romantic myth? Think about your own most important work — was it produced through struggle or through flow?

## The Stream of Consciousness

James was highly critical of Wundt's experimental analysis of the elemental contents of consciousness. Where Wundt saw discrete elements — sensations, feelings, images — James saw a continuous flow. He famously described consciousness not as a "chain" or "train" of discrete states but as a **stream of consciousness** — a continuous, ever-changing flow of experience.

> "Consciousness, then, does not appear to itself as chopped up in bits. Such words as 'chain' or 'train' do not describe it fitly. It is nothing jointed; it flows. A 'river' or a 'stream' are the metaphors by which it is most naturally described."
> — William James, *The Principles of Psychology* (1890, 1, p. 239)

James stressed the selectivity and functionality of consciousness. We are not aware of everything. We attend to what matters — what is relevant, interesting, useful. Consciousness is not a passive mirror reflecting the world. It is an active, selective, purposeful process.

James also followed Darwin in maintaining that much of human and animal behavior is governed by instinct. He developed an account of emotion that came to be known as the **James-Lange theory of emotion**, published by James in 1884 and independently by the Danish physiologist Carl Georg Lange in 1885.

According to this theory, emotion is not the cause of physiological arousal and behavior, as commonly supposed. It is our *experience* of physiological arousal and behavior:

> "We feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike or tremble because we are sorry, angry or fearful."
> — William James (1884, p. 190)

Our fear of a bear does not cause us to run away. It is simply our experience of the physiological and behavioral responses to our perception of the bear. The body responds first. The emotion follows.

This was a radical claim — and it was controversial. Critics asked: can you really have the physiological response without the emotion? If you are running from a bear but not feeling afraid, is that fear? James's answer was nuanced, but the theory provoked a debate that continues to this day.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** The James-Lange theory says you feel afraid because you tremble — not that you tremble because you feel afraid. Think about a moment of sudden fear — a car swerving toward you, a loud noise in the dark. Did the fear come before the physical response, or after? Can you separate the two?

## Pragmatism: Truth Is What Works

James was the most successful promoter of **pragmatism** — the philosophical view that the adequacy of any theoretical system should be judged by its practical utility. The name derives from Kant's characterization of uncertain beliefs with practical value as "pragmatic beliefs."

James developed a pragmatist theory of truth: a belief or proposition is true if it "works satisfactorily in the widest sense of the word." He really did mean the widest sense — a belief should be accepted as true if it satisfies our feelings or produces a beneficial effect, such as a religious belief or a belief that mineral baths relieve backache.

Most American psychologists embraced pragmatism in the broad sense that theories should be judged by their practical utility. Few were committed to the specifics of James's theory of truth. Most rejected his idea that the truth of a theory could be warranted by the satisfaction derived from believing it.

But the broader pragmatist commitment — that psychology should be useful, that theories should be evaluated by their practical consequences — became the defining feature of American psychology. It was the philosophical foundation of functionalism, applied psychology, and eventually behaviorism.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** James argued that a belief is true if it works. A therapist in Mumbai believes that a particular form of meditation reduces anxiety. She has no controlled studies to support this belief, but her patients report improvement. Is the belief true? By James's standard, yes. By modern scientific standards, not yet. Which standard is more useful?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** James cured his depression by deciding to believe in free will. This sounds circular — using free will to believe in free will. But it raises a profound question: can the act of believing something change your mental state? Modern research on placebo effects, self-efficacy, and growth mindsets suggests that it can. Was James's self-cure a genuine case of free will, or was it a placebo effect? Does the distinction matter if it worked?

## Münsterberg and the Birth of Applied Psychology

James arranged for Hugo Münsterberg (1863–1916), a former student of Wundt's, to take over his psychology courses and the laboratory at Harvard in 1892. James raised over $4,000 to purchase laboratory equipment, because, as he put it: "We are the best university in America, and we must lead in psychology."

Münsterberg disagreed with Wundt on a number of theoretical and methodological issues. He was an epiphenomenalist who maintained that consciousness is a by-product of neural processes and plays no causal role in behavior. He published his theory of the will as *Voluntary Action* in 1888 — a position that matched James's own epiphenomenalist treatment of emotion.

Despite the fact that he had to learn English from scratch, Münsterberg quickly mastered the language and developed a quintessentially American orientation to psychology. He became a greater promoter of popular and applied psychology than James ever was. He developed applied fields of psychology including psychotherapy, forensic psychology, and industrial psychology.

Münsterberg was one of the early pioneers of psychotherapy. His book *Psychotherapy* (1909) defined the new field for some years, until his work was eclipsed by Lightner Witmer. He was also a pioneer in forensic psychology, studying the reliability of eyewitness testimony and the psychology of false confessions. And he applied psychology to industry, studying worker efficiency and vocational guidance.

Mary Calkins (1863–1930), the first female president of the APA, was also a product of the Harvard program. She worked with James, Josiah Royce, and Münsterberg on associative memory and defended her dissertation before Münsterberg, James, and Royce — who unanimously recommended that she be awarded a PhD. Harvard refused because she was a woman. She was offered a PhD from Radcliffe (the women's college affiliated with Harvard) instead. She refused it on principle.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Mary Calkins was unanimously recommended for a PhD by James, Royce, and Münsterberg — three of the most prominent psychologists and philosophers of the era. Harvard refused because she was a woman. She went on to become the first female president of the APA and developed the paired-associate method of studying memory, which is still used today. How many other women in the history of science have been denied recognition for reasons that had nothing to do with their abilities?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> William James did not found a school of psychology. He had few theoretical disciples. But his influence on American psychology was greater than any single experimental program. He introduced the stream of consciousness, the James-Lange theory of emotion, and pragmatism. He wrote the textbook that trained a generation. He championed the idea that psychology should be useful, not just accurate. And he hired Münsterberg, who pioneered applied psychology. James's legacy is not a theory. It is an attitude: that psychology should engage with the world, not just the laboratory.

## Speaking of Streams, Emotions, and Pragmatism...

- A musician in Seoul describes her creative process: "I don't plan each note. The music flows through me. I follow where it leads." This is James's stream of consciousness, experienced from the inside. The musician does not experience discrete elements of thought. She experiences a continuous flow.

- A doctor in Lagos tells a patient: "Try smiling, even if you don't feel happy. The physical act of smiling can change how you feel." This is the James-Lange theory applied as therapy — the idea that changing the body can change the emotion. Modern research on facial feedback has largely supported this insight.

- A startup founder in São Paulo pitches his product by saying: "It works." He does not explain the theory behind it. He does not cite research. He shows results. This is pragmatism in its purest form — truth as practical utility. James would have approved. A scientist might want more.

---

James had introduced psychology to America. But the Americans who studied with Wundt — and who came after James — would shape the discipline in ways that neither James nor Wundt anticipated. Some would build laboratories. Some would create tests. Some would open clinics. And one would create a system of psychology so rigorous, so austere, so devoted to the analysis of consciousness that it would collapse under its own weight. His name was Edward Bradford Titchener.
