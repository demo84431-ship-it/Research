# Chapter 06 · Module 4
# The Nervous System as Machine — Müller, Helmholtz, Sechenov, and Fechner
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In 1845, four young men took a solemn oath. They were students of Johannes Müller at the University of Berlin — brilliant, ambitious, and impatient with their teacher's philosophy. Ernst Brücke, Emil du Bois-Reymond, Carl Ludwig, and Hermann von Helmholtz sealed their oath in blood: "no other forces than the common physical-chemical ones are active in the organism." They would devote their careers to proving it.

Their teacher, Müller, was one of the greatest physiologists of the century — and a committed vitalist. He believed that living organisms are governed by a vital force distinct from the physical and chemical forces that govern inanimate matter. His students thought this was nonsense. They founded the Berlin Physical Society in 1845, dedicated to the reductive explanation of all physiological processes in terms of known physical-chemical laws.

Between them, these four men — and their Russian colleague Ivan Sechenov — would transform the understanding of the nervous system. They would measure the speed of neural conduction, prove that nerves conduct electricity, discover that the brain can inhibit as well as stimulate behavior, and create the first bridge between physiology and experimental psychology. They would also raise a question that has never been fully answered: if the nervous system is a machine, what role does consciousness play?

---

## The Sensory-Motor Theory

The localization debate had established that specific brain regions control specific functions. But a more radical claim was gaining ground: that the *entire* nervous system — from the spinal cord to the cerebral cortex — is a reflexive sensory-motor system. Every component can be characterized as having a sensory or motor function. There is no separate "cognitive" system. There are only more and less complex reflexes.

Thomas Laycock (1812–1876), professor of medicine at the University of Edinburgh, was one of the first to articulate this **sensory-motor theory** of the nervous system:

> "The brain, although the organ of consciousness, is subject to the laws of reflex action, and . . . in this respect, it does not differ from the other ganglia of the nervous system. . . . The ganglia within the cranium, being a continuation of the spinal cord, must necessarily be regulated as to their reaction on external agencies by laws identical to those governing the functions of the spinal ganglia."
> — Thomas Laycock (1845, p. 298)

John Hughlings Jackson, a former student of Laycock, extended this idea. He treated sensation and movement as the basic elements of human psychology and claimed that they are instantiated in the cerebral cortex as "nervous arrangements representing impressions and movements." All mental disorders caused by damage to the cortex — aphasia, epilepsy, delirium — are due to "lack, or to disorderly development, of sensori-motor processes."

This was a radical claim. It meant that the cortex is not a separate "thinking" organ that presides over the reflexes of the lower nervous system (as Flourens had argued). It is part of the same reflexive system — just more complex. The higher cognitive functions are strongly continuous with the lower sensory-motor functions. They are merely more elaborate reflexes.

Not everyone agreed. Fritsch and Hitzig claimed that the anterior frontal regions of the cortex are responsible for abstract thought and play a minimal role in sensory-motor function. Ferrier maintained that they are responsible for focused attention. The cortex, these critics argued, is not just a more complex reflex machine. It has genuinely cognitive functions that cannot be reduced to sensation and movement.

The debate was not resolved in the 19th century. It is not resolved today.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Consider the act of reading this sentence. You are processing visual information (sensation), interpreting meaning (cognition), and — if you are thinking about what you read — engaging in something that feels like pure thought. Is this "pure thought" just a very complex sensory-motor process? Or is it something qualitatively different? How would you design an experiment to find out?

## Müller and the Berlin Physical Society

Johannes Müller (1801–1858) was the most important figure in 19th-century German physiology. He received his doctorate from the University of Bonn in 1822 and was appointed chair in physiology at the University of Berlin in 1833. His two-volume *Handbook of Human Physiology* (1833–1840) became the internationally recognized sourcebook of contemporary research in physiology and neuroanatomy.

Müller made many contributions. He replicated Hall's studies of the reflex arc and Magendie's discrimination of sensory and motor nerves. He developed an integrated hierarchical theory of nervous system functions and an early account of "trial-and-error" learning based upon spontaneous nervous activity. He demonstrated that there are five types of sensory nerves, each with its own **specific energy** — each giving rise to distinctive sensations of color, smell, taste, sound, and touch. The properties of our sensations, Müller argued, are determined by our nervous system, not by the external world.

But Müller was also a vitalist. He believed that physiological processes are the product of an emergent vital force distinct from physical and chemical forces. His students — the four blood-oath takers — rejected this entirely. They founded the Berlin Physical Society and dedicated themselves to proving that all physiological processes can be explained by known physical-chemical laws.

The daunting complexity of most physiological processes made this goal difficult to achieve in the 19th century. But the commitment to reductive explanation inspired the students to make extraordinary contributions. Ludwig explained the formation of urine in purely physical-chemical terms — the first detailed reductive explanation of a physiological process. Du Bois-Reymond demonstrated that the nervous system conducts rather than generates electricity. Helmholtz measured the speed of neural conduction. Schwann developed the cell theory.

Müller's commitment to vitalism did not prevent him from doing excellent experimental work. And the students' rejection of vitalism did not prevent them from acknowledging the limits of their reductive program. The history of science is full of productive disagreements.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Müller believed in vitalism — the idea that living organisms are governed by a force distinct from physics and chemistry. His students rejected this and swore to explain everything in physical-chemical terms. Was Müller wrong? Consider: can every aspect of life — growth, healing, consciousness — be fully explained by physics and chemistry? Or is there something about living systems that requires a different kind of explanation?

## Helmholtz: The Speed of Thought

Hermann von Helmholtz (1821–1894) was one of the most versatile scientists of the 19th century. An army surgeon who was honorably relieved of his duties so he could devote himself to research, Helmholtz made major contributions to physics, physiology, and physiological psychology. In 1847, he advanced the principle of the **conservation of energy** — the total quantity of energy remains constant throughout any qualitative change. This principle applied to physical, chemical, physiological, and — by implication — psychological systems.

The conservation of energy had a profound implication for psychology. If the physical world constitutes a closed system, then there is no room for a psychic or vital force that is categorically distinct from physical energy. Psychic force, if it exists, must be a special form of physical or electrical force. The nervous system came to be represented as a conductor of electrical energy, received via sensory stimulation and discharged through motor behavior — analogous to the recently developed telegraph.

Helmholtz was the first to measure the speed of neural conduction. He estimated it at around 25–45 meters per second in frogs and around 30–35 meters per second in humans. This was astonishingly fast — but it was too slow to be purely electrical. (Electrical current in an insulated conductor travels close to the speed of light.) The nervous system, Helmholtz demonstrated, is not a simple electrical wire. It is something more complex — what we now know to be an electrochemical system.

Helmholtz also developed a theory of perception that anticipated late-20th-century cognitive psychology. He argued that our perception of distance, shape, size, causality, and motion is based upon **unconscious inference** — a cognitive judgment that goes beyond the information provided by sensation:

> "We never perceive the objects of the external world directly, on the contrary, we only perceive the effects of these objects on our nervous apparatuses. . . . Now, in which way have we passed over for the first time from the world of sensations of our nerves to the world of reality? Obviously only through an inference."
> — Hermann von Helmholtz (1855, p. 40, trans. Pastore, 1974)

Although our perception of distance and shape is really a cognitive judgment based on inference, it appears as direct perception because it is unconscious and instantaneous. This theory — perception as unconscious inference — would become one of the most influential ideas in the history of psychology.

Helmholtz also developed a trichromatic theory of color vision (independently of Thomas Young) and made important contributions to acoustics. His *Treatise on Physiological Optics* (1856–1866) and *On the Sensation of Tone* (1862) remain landmarks in the science of perception.

## Sechenov: Thought Is Two-Thirds of a Reflex

Ivan Mikhailovich Sechenov (1829–1905) was a Russian student who arrived at the University of Berlin and studied with Müller, du Bois-Reymond, Ludwig, and Helmholtz. He returned to Russia and became professor of physiology at the Military-Medical Academy in St. Petersburg. In 1863, he published *Reflexes of the Brain* — a book that would make him the founder of Russian reflexology and a precursor of behaviorism.

Sechenov's key discovery was **inhibition** — the ability of the nervous system to suppress as well as stimulate behavior. Building on earlier work by Edouard Weber (who demonstrated that stimulation of the vagus nerve reduces heart rate), Sechenov showed that stimulation of certain regions of the brain depresses normal reflex activity. A frog's automatic withdrawal of its leg from acid can be suppressed by stimulating the thalamus.

From this finding, Sechenov drew a sweeping conclusion: all behavior is reflexive, and voluntary behavior is simply reflexive behavior that has come under the control of inhibitory stimuli. What we call a "strong will" is simply the product of successfully learned inhibition — the ability to refrain from alcohol, to resist temptation, to control impulses.

Sechenov went further. He claimed that thought itself is a form of reflex — specifically, the first two-thirds of one:

> "Thought is generally believed to be the cause of behavior . . . but this is the greatest of falsehoods; the initial cause of all behavior always lies, not in thought, but in external sensory stimulation, without which no thought is possible."
> — Ivan Sechenov, *Reflexes of the Brain* (1863/1965, p. 322)

> "A thought is the first two-thirds of a psychical reflex."
> — Sechenov (1863/1965, pp. 320–321)

Sechenov was not an epiphenomenalist. He did not deny that thought causes behavior. He argued that thought is the proximate cause, but that thought itself is fully determined by external sensory stimulation. There is no spontaneous generation of thought. Every thought has a sensory cause.

Sechenov's extreme position may have been influenced by his political radicalism. He was a political progressive who hoped that psychology would enable humans to realize their true potential and surmount the repressive constraints of traditional societies. He was not driven to this position by his rejection of vitalism — Bain and Skinner would later develop non-reflexive accounts of learned behavior without any commitment to vitalism.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Sechenov claimed that "a thought is the first two-thirds of a psychical reflex" — that thinking is just a reflex that has been interrupted before it reaches the behavioral stage. Think about a problem you solved recently. Was the solution a reflex? Did it come from external stimulation? Or did it emerge from something internal — a flash of insight, a creative connection, a moment of understanding?

## Fechner: Measuring the Mind

Gustav Theodor Fechner (1801–1887) was a physicist who accidentally created a new science. He earned his medical degree at the University of Leipzig in 1822, but his interests lay in physics and mathematics. He became professor of physics and did significant research on the measurement of electric current. Then he injured his eyes studying visual after-images and was forced to resign his position.

During his recovery, Fechner had a revelation. He believed that mental and physical states are qualitatively different but quantitatively identical — they are ultimately one and the same, viewed from different perspectives. He realized he could test this belief by establishing the mathematical relationship between the physical intensity of stimuli and the perceived intensity of sensations.

In a series of experiments, Fechner systematically varied the intensity of auditory, visual, tactual, and thermal stimuli and measured the intensity of sensational responses by means of just noticeable differences. He concluded that the perceived intensity of a sensation is a logarithmic function of the physical intensity of a stimulus. This relationship is expressed in **Fechner's law**: S = k log R, where R represents the physical intensity of a stimulus, S the perceived intensity of a sensation, and k is a constant.

Fechner's studies were published in *Elements of Psychophysics* (1860). William James dismissed the work as "dreadful" and amounting to "nothing." But many were convinced that Fechner had refuted Kant's claim that psychology could never attain the status of a genuine science. Fechner had established quantified psychophysical laws based on experiments in which manipulated differences in the physical intensity of stimuli were correlated with subjects' introspective reports of sensational differences. Psychology had its first mathematical law.

There is a delicious irony in Fechner's legacy. He is often represented as having established the physical basis of mentality — demonstrating that mental states depend on physical stimuli. But Fechner himself believed the opposite. He believed he had demonstrated the mentality of the physical — the existence of a "world-soul." Under the pen name Dr. Mises, he railed against the materialism of his age. The founder of psychophysics was a panpsychist.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Fechner's law says that the perceived intensity of a sensation increases logarithmically — doubling the physical intensity of a stimulus does not double the perceived intensity. Think about the difference between a whisper and a normal voice, and between a normal voice and a shout. Do the two differences feel the same? Or does the jump from whisper to normal feel bigger than the jump from normal to shout?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The German physiologists of the 19th century did not set out to create a science of psychology. They were studying nerves, muscles, reflexes, and sensation. But in the process, they built the tools, methods, and theoretical frameworks that made experimental psychology possible. Müller's specific energies, Helmholtz's unconscious inference, Sechenov's inhibition, Fechner's psychophysics — each of these contributions was a piece of the puzzle. The question was: who would assemble them into a coherent discipline?

In *A History of Experimental Psychology* (1929, 2nd ed. 1950), Edwin G. Boring — the influential historian of psychology at Harvard — told the story of how these pieces came together. Boring's account, which shaped how generations of psychologists understood their discipline's origins, placed Fechner at the center of the narrative. Boring called Fechner "the founder of scientific psychology" — a claim that has been debated ever since, but that captures something real: Fechner's psychophysics was the first systematic attempt to measure the relationship between mind and body using experimental methods.

## Speaking of Machines, Inhibition, and Psychophysics...

- A software engineer in Tokyo designs an AI system that processes visual information in layers — simple features first (edges, colors), complex features later (objects, faces). This layered processing mirrors the sensory-motor theory: simple reflexes at the bottom, complex cognition at the top. The question is whether the system "understands" what it sees or merely processes it.

- A parent in Nairobi tells their child: "Don't touch the hot stove." The child reaches for it anyway. The parent says "No!" more firmly. The child stops. This is Sechenov's inhibition in everyday life — learned suppression of a reflexive response through social training. What the parent calls "discipline," Sechenov called "cerebral reflex inhibition."

- A music producer in London adjusts the volume of a track. She knows that doubling the physical intensity of the sound does not double the perceived loudness — she has to increase it much more than double to make it sound twice as loud. She is, without knowing it, navigating Fechner's law. The logarithmic relationship between physical stimulus and perceived intensity is built into every mixing console in the world.

---

The physiology was in place. The methods were developed. The tools were built. What was missing was an institutional home — a laboratory, a journal, a curriculum, a community of researchers who called themselves psychologists. In the next module, we will see how two very different thinkers — a German professor and a Russian radical — proposed two very different visions of what psychology should become. One of them won. The other was forgotten. But the arguments of the forgotten one would echo through the 20th century in ways that nobody expected.
