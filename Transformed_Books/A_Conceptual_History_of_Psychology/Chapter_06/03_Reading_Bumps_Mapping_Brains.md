# Chapter 06 · Module 3
# Reading Bumps, Mapping Brains — Phrenology and the Fight Over Localization
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In the early 1800s, a Viennese physician named Franz Joseph Gall noticed something peculiar about his schoolmates. The boys who excelled at rote memory — who could recite long passages of Latin without effort — tended to have large, prominent eyes. Gall was a serious anatomist, and he did not think this was a coincidence. He began collecting observations, visiting prisons and asylums, studying the skulls of criminals and geniuses, and comparing the heads of different animal species. Over decades, he developed a theory that would make him famous, infamous, and eventually dismissed as a crank.

Gall's theory was phrenology: the doctrine that the degree of development of psychological faculties is reflected in the contours of the skull. A protrusion above the left ear indicates a developed faculty of acquisitiveness — a tendency to collect and hoard. An indentation in the same spot indicates the opposite. By reading the bumps on a person's head, Gall claimed, you could diagnose their character, predict their behavior, and advise them on their career.

The scientific establishment was initially respectful. Gall was a skilled neuroanatomist whose dissection techniques were admired. His claim that psychological functions are localized in specific brain regions was ahead of its time. But his specific methods — measuring skulls and correlating bumps with behavior — were eventually discredited. When Descartes' skull was found to lack the expected protrusion for rationality, Gall's followers concluded that Descartes had not been as great a thinker as previously supposed. This was not good science.

Yet the question Gall raised — are psychological functions localized in specific brain regions? — was one of the most important questions in the history of psychology. The debate it sparked would consume the best minds in European physiology for half a century.

## The Reflex Arc: Hall's Discovery

Before we can understand the localization debate, we need to understand a more basic discovery: the **reflex arc**.

In the early 19th century, the English physiologist Marshall Hall (1790–1857) established that there are numerous connections between sensory and motor nerves in the spinal cord. He introduced the notion of a reflex arc — a system comprising a sensory nerve, interconnecting nervous tissue in the spinal cord, and a motor nerve. When you touch a hot stove, the sensory nerve sends a signal to the spinal cord, which immediately sends a signal to the motor nerve in your arm, and you pull your hand back. The brain does not need to be involved. The reflex arc handles it.

Hall distinguished between two systems. The **excitory-motor system**, located in the lower brain and spinal cord, accounts for automatic, instinctual, and emotional behavior. The **sensory-volitional system**, located in the cerebral cortex, accounts for rational, learned, and purposive behavior. This was a form of **neurophysiological dualism** — the view that the cognitive functions of the cerebral cortex are categorically distinct from the sensory-motor functions of the lower brain and spinal cord.

This distinction would become central to the localization debate. If the cortex is the seat of reason and the spinal cord is the seat of reflex, then where exactly in the cortex do the higher functions reside? Are they spread evenly across the whole cortex, or are they concentrated in specific regions?

Gall had an answer: they are localized. His critics had a different answer: they are not. The argument would rage for fifty years.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** When you pull your hand away from a hot stove, you do not decide to do it — it happens automatically. But when you decide to reach for a cup of coffee, that decision seems to come from somewhere else — from your conscious mind, from your will. Hall's distinction between the excitory-motor system and the sensory-volitional system captures this difference. Is it a real distinction, or is it an illusion? Are "voluntary" actions just more complex reflexes?

## Gall's Phrenology: The First Empirical Physiological Psychology

Franz Joseph Gall (1758–1828) was born in Baden, Germany, and trained as a physician in Vienna. He was a serious scientist — a skilled neuroanatomist who developed dissection techniques that were admired throughout Europe. His fundamental claim was bold: the brain is the organ of the mind, and different psychological faculties are localized in different brain regions.

Gall identified 27 fundamental faculties, which he conceived as distinct and independent. These included traditional cognitive faculties like perception, memory, and language, but also adaptive and socially oriented faculties like the "carnivorous instinct," the "maternal instinct," and the "disposition to murder." He claimed that animals share 15 of these faculties with humans, but that humans possess 12 additional faculties that animals lack.

Gall's method was comparative. He studied the skulls of normal and abnormal adults, children, and the elderly, and he analyzed the psychological faculties of different animal species. He insisted that psychological functions should be established empirically — through the comparative study of behavioral repertoires — before being correlated with brain regions. This was, in principle, a sound scientific approach.

The problem was the execution. Gall claimed that the degree of development of a faculty is reflected in the size of the brain area in which it is localized, which can be determined by measuring the contours of the skull — a method he called **cranioscopy**. If the area above your left ear protrudes, you have a developed faculty of acquisitiveness. If it is indented, you do not.

Gall's specific claims were undermined by later research. The skull does not reflect the contours of the brain. And his followers were overly enthusiastic in their appeal to positive instances and uncritically dismissive of negative ones. When Descartes' skull lacked the expected protrusion for rationality, they concluded that Descartes had not been as great a thinker as supposed. This was not science. It was rationalization.

Yet Gall's broader project — the empirical study of psychological functions and their neural localization — was ahead of its time. He was arguably the first empirical physiological psychologist. He insisted that psychological functions should be studied through behavioral observation, not introspection. He focused on individual differences, comparative psychology, and the adaptive functions of behavior. He anticipated many of the concerns of later functional and behaviorist psychologists.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Gall's phrenology is now considered a pseudoscience. But his basic claim — that psychological functions are localized in specific brain regions — turned out to be correct. What is the difference between a pseudoscience and a science that is wrong? Is phrenology more like astrology (no redeeming features) or more like early atomic theory (wrong in detail but right in principle)?

## Flourens: The Experimental Counterattack

Pierre Flourens (1794–1867) was the man who dismantled phrenology. A French surgeon and member of the French scientific establishment, Flourens undertook the first systematic critique of Gall's theory in *An Examination of Phrenology* (1843). He demonstrated that many of Gall's localizations were inaccurate and, more fundamentally, that the skull does not reflect the contours of the brain.

But Flourens did more than criticize. He pioneered the experimental method of **ablation** — the systematic removal of neural tissue from live animals and careful observation of their consequent behavior. This was a major methodological advance. Instead of correlating skull bumps with behavior (as Gall had done), Flourens directly manipulated the brain and observed the results.

Flourens's enduring achievements were the localization of specific functions in specific brain structures. He established that the cerebellum is the center for the control of motor behavior and the medulla oblongata is the center for the control of vital functions such as respiration and heartbeat. These findings have stood the test of time.

But Flourens also made a claim that would prove controversial: that the cognitive functions of the cerebral cortex are unitary. He argued that perception, will, and intelligence are not localized in specific regions of the cortex but are subserved by the "whole mass" of the cerebral hemispheres. This was his fundamental objection to Gall: Gall had denied the unity of the immaterial soul that Descartes had affirmed.

Flourens's primary ground for this claim was his commitment to substance dualism. He believed that the unified cognitive functions of the cerebral cortex are the expression of the unified powers of the immaterial soul. The soul, being indivisible, could not be localized to discrete brain regions. To claim otherwise was to open the door to materialism, atheism, and fatalism.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Flourens argued that the cortex functions as a whole because the soul is indivisible. This is a philosophical argument, not a scientific one. Can you think of a modern example where a philosophical commitment has influenced a scientist's interpretation of evidence? Does philosophy help or hinder scientific progress?

## The Bell-Magendie Law: Separating Sensory from Motor

While Flourens was fighting Gall in France, another French physiologist was making a discovery that would transform the understanding of the nervous system.

François Magendie (1783–1855) was a physician and experimental physiologist who sought to establish physiology as a natural science in the same way that Galileo and Newton had established physics. He founded the *Journal of Experimental Physiology* in 1821 and championed experimentation as the basis of physiological and medical knowledge.

Magendie's major contribution was his demonstration of the separate locations of sensory and motor nerves in the spinal cord. He exposed the spinal cord of a six-week-old puppy and found that severing the anterior roots eliminated motor movements but left sensitivity intact, while severing the posterior roots eliminated sensitivity but left motor movements intact:

> "The anterior and posterior roots of the nerves which arise from the spinal cord have different functions, that the posterior roots seem to be particularly destined for sensibility, while the anterior roots seem to be especially connected with movement."
> — François Magendie (1822/1944, pp. 101–102)

This was not entirely original. The British physician Charles Bell had reported a similar result in 1811, but had circulated his report only privately. This led to a priority dispute — the **Bell-Magendie law** — that has never been fully resolved. Bell was first to attribute sensory and motor functions to the posterior and anterior spinal roots, but Magendie was first to provide complete experimental confirmation.

Magendie, like Flourens, maintained the traditional distinction between the higher cognitive functions of the cerebral cortex and the sensory-motor functions of the lower brain and spinal cord. He avowed that cognitive functions could be studied as "the result of the action of the brain," but also held that they may be "dependent upon the soul" (1838/1843, p. 146). He maintained a neutral parallelism, claiming that science can only describe the correlation between mental and physiological states without causally explaining the relation between them.

## Broca's Area: The Case of "Tan"

In 1861, a French physician named Pierre-Paul Broca (1824–1880) made a discovery that would tip the balance of the localization debate. A patient known as "Tan" (whose real name was Leborgne) was admitted to Broca's surgery. Tan had lost his speech 21 years earlier — his only utterance was the syllable "tan" — and had been a patient at La Bicêtre Hospital in Paris. He died within a week of admission.

Broca performed an autopsy and discovered damage to the superior region of the left frontal lobe. He localized the "faculty of articulate language" to this area — now known as **Broca's area**. On the basis of this case and other autopsies, Broca claimed that he had isolated an autonomous faculty of language, which could be eliminated without damage to other intellectual faculties such as memory and intelligence.

This was a direct challenge to Flourens's claim that the cortex functions as a whole. If language could be destroyed while leaving other cognitive functions intact, then at least one cognitive function was localized. The cortex did not act "as a whole."

Broca presented his results at the Société d'Anthropologie, where the debate about cortical unity had been raging. His evidence was not without problems — some of his patients suffered from extensive brain damage, and the relationship between brain damage and behavioral deficits is not always straightforward. But the finding was compelling, and it shifted the balance of the debate decisively toward localization.

## Fritsch and Hitzig: The Cortex Fires Back

The final blow to Flourens's doctrine came from Germany. In 1870, Gustav Fritsch (1839–1927) and Eduard Hitzig (1838–1907) conducted a series of experiments on dogs and rabbits — on a dressing table in a small Berlin house — that demonstrated that the cerebral cortex responds to electrical stimulation and that one region of the cortex is responsible for muscular contractions.

This was revolutionary. The cortex had long been considered inexcitable — Flourens and Magendie had both claimed that it does not respond to stimulation. Fritsch and Hitzig proved otherwise. They showed that electrical stimulation of a specific region of the cortex produces specific muscular movements in specific parts of the body. The motor cortex had been discovered.

Their experiments were quickly replicated. David Ferrier (1843–1928) in Britain mapped the motor cortex in detail through experiments on dogs, cats, rabbits, and guinea pigs. His 1876 book *The Functions of the Brain* represented the triumph of neural localization and initiated a program of research directed to the detailed mapping of discrete functional centers of the cerebral cortex.

The discovery of the motor cortex had an immediate practical benefit: it greatly advanced the prospects of effective neural surgery by establishing targeted sites for the removal of suspected tumors or swelling. It also had a darker chapter. In 1874, Robert Bartholow of the Medical College of Ohio replicated Fritsch and Hitzig's experiments on Mary Rafferty, an Irish domestic servant with a cancerous ulcer that exposed her brain through a hole in her skull. He passed electric current through needles inserted into her brain, producing convulsive movements. Mary died a few days later. The American Medical Association condemned Bartholow, but he went on to have a distinguished career.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Bartholow's experiment on Mary Rafferty is one of the earliest documented cases of unethical human experimentation. He claimed to have her consent, and he maintained that the experiment did not cause her death. But the AMA condemned him anyway. Where is the line between scientific discovery and the exploitation of vulnerable people? Does the value of the knowledge justify the means of obtaining it?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The localization debate was not just a scientific argument. It was a philosophical and theological one. Gall's phrenology threatened the concept of the indivisible soul. Flourens defended the soul by defending cortical unity. Broca and Fritsch and Hitzig demonstrated localization — and, in doing so, implicitly supported the materialist view that the mind is a product of the brain. The scientific facts did not settle the philosophical questions. They made them more urgent.

In *Mind, Brain, and Adaptation in the Nineteenth Century* (1970), the historian Robert M. Young provided the definitive account of the localization debate from Gall to Ferrier. Young showed how the debate was shaped not only by experimental evidence but also by the philosophical and theological commitments of the researchers involved. The scientists were not neutral observers. They were participants in a cultural argument about the nature of the human soul, and their experimental conclusions were often influenced by their prior convictions.

## Speaking of Localization and Its Limits...

- A neurosurgeon in London uses fMRI to map a patient's brain before removing a tumor. She needs to know which regions control language, movement, and vision so she can avoid damaging them. This is Broca's legacy — the direct descendant of 19th-century localization research.

- A child in Lagos suffers a head injury in a car accident and loses the ability to speak but can still understand language and communicate through writing. This is a modern version of Broca's aphasia — a specific language deficit caused by damage to a specific brain region.

- A psychologist in São Paulo studies patients with diffuse brain damage (from Alzheimer's disease, for example) and finds that cognitive decline is not localized — memory, language, attention, and executive function all deteriorate together. This is closer to Flourens's model of cortical unity than to Gall's model of discrete localization. The truth, as usual, is more complicated than either side believed.

---

The localization debate had established that specific brain regions control specific functions. But this raised a more fundamental question: what is the relationship between the brain and the mind? Is the whole nervous system a reflexive sensory-motor machine, as some physiologists argued? Is consciousness merely a byproduct of neural activity, as Huxley claimed? And if so, does the will have any power at all? These were the questions that consumed the next generation of researchers — a generation that included one of the most remarkable scientists of the 19th century, who measured the speed of thought itself.
