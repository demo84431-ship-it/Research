# Chapter 06 · Module 1
# The Positivist Promise — Comte, Mill, and the Dream of a Social Science
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In 1820, a young Frenchman named Auguste Comte was arrested for challenging a police officer to a duel. He spent several days in jail. This was not unusual for Comte — he spent much of his life in conflict with authority. What was unusual was what he did after his release. He sat down and began writing a six-volume work that would attempt to classify all human knowledge, rank every science from physics to sociology, and propose a new religion based entirely on scientific principles.

Comte was not modest. He had been secretary to the social theorist Henri de Saint-Simon, and he had absorbed his mentor's conviction that society could be reorganized on scientific principles. The French Revolution had shattered the old order, but nothing coherent had replaced it. Comte believed he knew why: society lacked a science of itself. Physics had Newton. Biology had its laws. But society had nothing — no systematic knowledge, no predictive framework, no foundation for rational governance. Comte intended to fix this.

He published the first volume of his *Course of Positive Philosophy* in 1830. Over the next twelve years, he produced five more. The work was sprawling, often contradictory, and occasionally brilliant. It contained an idea that would reshape the relationship between psychology and the natural sciences: the claim that the highest form of human knowledge is not causal explanation but the description of observable correlations. This idea — **positivism** — would become the dominant philosophy of science for much of the 19th century, and its influence on psychology was enormous.

But Comte's positivism also contained a strange omission. He left no room for psychology as an independent science. How could a philosophy of science erase an entire discipline? And what happened when psychologists refused to be erased?

---

## Comte's Three Stages

Comte's most famous idea was his **law of three stages**. He claimed that every society — and every branch of knowledge within a society — passes through three stages of cognitive development.

In the **theological stage**, people explain natural events in terms of anthropomorphized forces. Lightning storms happen because the gods are angry. Crops fail because a deity is displeased. The world is populated by invisible agents with human-like intentions. This is not stupidity, Comte insisted. It is a necessary first stage of human understanding. When you know almost nothing about how the world works, attributing events to the intentions of powerful beings is a reasonable strategy.

In the **metaphysical stage**, these personalized forces are replaced by depersonalized ones. Planetary motions are explained by gravitational forces. Disease is explained by imbalances in bodily humors or abstract principles of nature. The agents disappear, but the explanations are still speculative — they posit hidden essences and ultimate causes that cannot be directly observed.

In the **positive stage**, the highest form of knowledge, people stop asking "why" in the ultimate sense and start asking "how" in the empirical sense. Natural events are explained in terms of the description of observable correlations, which can be employed to predict the course of nature. You do not need to know what gravity *is*. You need to know that objects fall at 9.8 m/s² and that this regularity allows you to predict and control.

Comte believed that the natural sciences — physics, chemistry, biology — had already reached the positive stage. Sociology (a term he coined) would complete the ascent. A science of society, grounded in observable correlations, would eventually displace traditional religion and politics as the foundation of social order.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Think about how people in your community explain illness. Do they invoke germs and viruses (positive stage)? Imbalances or energies (metaphysical stage)? Curses or divine punishment (theological stage)? Most societies operate with a mixture of all three. Does Comte's hierarchy still make sense if the stages coexist rather than replace each other?

## Dogmatic Empiricism: Science as Correlation

The form of positivism that most 19th-century scientists embraced was not Comte's elaborate system but something simpler: **dogmatic empiricism**. This was the view that scientific theories and causal explanations should be restricted to the description of the correlation of observables. Science tells us *what* happens, not *why* it happens. It enables us to predict and control, but it does not reveal the inner nature of things.

John Stuart Mill characterized Comte's positive philosophy in these terms:

> "The constant resemblances which link phaenomena together, and the constant sequences which unite them as antecedent and consequent, are termed their laws. The laws of phaenomena are all we know respecting them. Their essential nature, and their ultimate causes, either efficient or final, are unknown and inscrutable to us."
> — John Stuart Mill, *Auguste Comte and Positivism* (1866/1961, p. 6)

This position had a powerful advantage for 19th-century scientists working in a religiously conservative environment. If science only describes correlations and makes no claims about ultimate causes, then it cannot conflict with theology. God, the soul, the purpose of creation — these are questions about ultimate causes, and science has nothing to say about them. This principle of **methodological empiricism** — the view that scientific theories must be based upon observation — provided a convenient truce between science and religion.

Many 19th-century physiologists and psychologists adopted this stance explicitly. John Hughlings Jackson, a British neurophysiologist, claimed:

> "We cannot understand how any conceivable arrangement of any sort of matter can give us mental states of any kind. . . . I do not concern myself with mental states at all, except indirectly in seeking their anatomical substrata. I do not trouble myself about the mode of connection between mind and matter. It is enough to assume a parallelism."
> — John Hughlings Jackson (1931, 1, p. 52)

This was **parallelism** — the view that mental and physical states run in parallel without one causing the other. It was the perfect positivist position: study the correlations between brain states and mental states, but do not speculate about how or why they are connected. It was safe, productive, and philosophically modest.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Imagine you are a 19th-century physiologist who has just discovered a correlation between a brain region and a psychological function. Your university is funded by a conservative government, and your bishop is watching. Would you claim that the brain *produces* the mental function? Or would you say you are merely studying correlations? What is the difference — in practice?

## Mill's Methods and the Science of Character

Comte had left no room for psychology as an independent science. He believed that everything worth knowing about human behavior was covered by sociology (the study of behavior in its social context) and phrenology (the correlation of brain regions with psychological faculties). John Stuart Mill disagreed.

In *A System of Logic* (1843), Mill described the methods of comparative causal analysis known as **Mill's methods** — the methods of agreement, difference, and concomitant variation. These methods, Mill argued, could be applied to any science, including psychology. If you can identify the conditions under which a psychological phenomenon occurs (agreement), the conditions under which it does not occur (difference), and the way it varies with other factors (concomitant variation), you have a science.

But Mill was realistic about the limits. He called the moral sciences (including psychology) "a blot on the face of science" and argued that their backwardness could be remedied only by applying the methods of physical science. Yet he also maintained that psychology is bound to remain an **inexact science**, at least outside of controlled experimental situations. The problem is not that human behavior is lawless. The problem is that too many factors are involved, and you cannot anticipate them all.

Mill also championed the scientific study of character, which he called **ethology**. He conceived of character as a set of social capacities and propensities that could be derived from the fundamental laws of association. Ethology would be the bridge between the abstract laws of psychology and the concrete reality of individual human beings. Unfortunately, Mill never developed this science himself. He deferred the task to his friend Alexander Bain, whose attempt — *On the Study of Character* (1861) — was, by most accounts, not his best work.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Mill argued that psychology is an "inexact science" because human behavior is too complex for precise prediction. Is this still true today? Can you think of areas of psychology where prediction is precise (like psychophysics) and areas where it remains rough (like predicting individual behavior)? What makes the difference?

## Mental Chemistry: Ideas That Are More Than the Sum of Their Parts

Mill made one more contribution to associationist psychology that would prove surprisingly important. He questioned the standard empiricist assumption that complex ideas are simply the sum of their parts — that your idea of an orange is just the mechanical combination of its color, shape, taste, and smell.

Mill argued that some associative processes are more like chemical bonding than mechanical combination. When hydrogen and oxygen combine to form water, the resulting substance has properties (like being wet) that neither hydrogen nor oxygen possesses alone. Similarly, Mill claimed, some complex ideas have properties that cannot be found in their component sensations:

> "Our idea of an orange really consists of the simple ideas of a certain color, a certain form, a certain taste and smell, &c., because we can by interrogating our consciousness, perceive all these elements in the idea. But we cannot perceive, in so apparently simple a feeling as our perception of the shape of an object by the eye, all that multitude of ideas derived from other senses. . . . These therefore are cases of mental chemistry: in which it is proper to say that the simple ideas generate, rather than that they compose, the complex ones."
> — John Stuart Mill, *A System of Logic* (1843/1973–1974, pp. 853–854)

This was a significant departure from the atomistic tradition of Hume and Hartley. If ideas can generate rather than merely compose, then the mind is not just a passive receiver of sensations that sticks them together like building blocks. It is an active system that can produce genuinely new properties from old ingredients.

The concept of **mental chemistry** would later influence Gestalt psychologists, who argued that perception involves the emergence of organized wholes (*Gestalten*) that are not reducible to their parts. Mill, writing in 1843, had already glimpsed this possibility.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Consider the taste of a complex dish — a Thai curry, for example. Can you separately perceive the coconut milk, the chili, the lemongrass, the fish sauce? Or is the taste of the curry a single, unified experience that is more than the sum of its ingredients? Is this an example of mental chemistry?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The 19th century promised psychology a place at the table of the sciences — but only if it agreed to play by the rules. Comte's positivism said: describe correlations, do not speculate about causes. Mill's methods said: apply the tools of physics, but accept that psychology will remain inexact. Mental chemistry said: ideas are not just assemblies of parts — the mind generates something new. These were not just philosophical positions. They were political negotiations — attempts to make psychology scientifically respectable without provoking the religious establishment or overstepping what the evidence could support. The terms of that negotiation would shape psychology for the next two centuries.

In *Constructing the Subject: Historical Origins of Psychological Research* (1990), the historian Kurt Danziger traced how these political negotiations shaped the very methods of psychological research. Danziger showed that the experimental methods psychologists adopted in the late 19th century were not neutral tools — they embodied assumptions about what counts as knowledge, who counts as a subject, and what counts as data. The positivist commitment to the correlation of observables did not just guide theory. It shaped the practice of experimentation itself.

## Speaking of Correlations and Causes...

- A data scientist in São Paulo builds a model that predicts student dropout rates with 85% accuracy using demographic and behavioral data. A school administrator asks: "But *why* are they dropping out?" The model cannot say. It describes correlations, not causes. This is Comte's positivism in action — and its limitation.

- A doctor in Mumbai prescribes a medication based on clinical trials showing it reduces symptoms. The trials show correlation (drug → improvement), not the full causal mechanism. The doctor is practicing dogmatic empiricism — and saving lives by doing so.

- A parent in Lagos notices that their child's mood improves after outdoor play. They do not know the neurochemistry of serotonin and sunlight. They observe a correlation and act on it. This is Mill's method of agreement in everyday life: whenever the child plays outside, the mood improves.

---

The associationists had built a psychology of ideas, sensations, and associations. The positivists had given psychology a philosophy of science. But neither had answered the most pressing question: where in the brain do these processes happen?
