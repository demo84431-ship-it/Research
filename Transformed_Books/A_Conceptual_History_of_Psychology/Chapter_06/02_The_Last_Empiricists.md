# Chapter 06 · Module 2
# The Last Empiricists — James Mill, J.S. Mill, and Alexander Bain
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In 1819, a Scottish philosopher and economist sat down at his desk in the East India Company's headquarters in London and began writing a comprehensive account of the human mind. James Mill was not a psychologist by training. He was a political radical, a utilitarian, and a close friend of Jeremy Bentham. His main interest was reforming society — improving education, overhauling government, alleviating poverty. But he believed that before you could fix society, you had to understand the mind that composed it.

Mill spent seven years on his *Analysis of the Phenomena of the Human Mind* (1829). He was not modest about its ambitions. He intended to reduce all mental life to a single principle: the association of ideas. Every complex thought, every emotion, every moral judgment — all of them, Mill argued, were built from atomistic sensory elements glued together by the laws of association. There was nothing in the mind that was not first in the senses, and nothing in the senses that was not first combined by association.

His son, John Stuart Mill, would later edit a new edition of the *Analysis* — and, in the process, quietly dismantle some of its most fundamental assumptions. The younger Mill discovered that ideas could be emergent, that perception involved unconscious inference, and that the mind was far more active than his father had imagined. Between them, the two Mills and their protégé Alexander Bain would produce the last great flowering of British empiricist psychology — and, in doing so, reveal its limits.

---

## James Mill: Points of Consciousness

James Mill (1773–1836) was a man of fierce convictions and austere habits. He rose from poverty in Scotland to become one of the most influential thinkers in London, largely through his friendship with Bentham and his career at the East India Company. His *History of British India* (1818) was so scathing in its criticism of Indian civilization that it reportedly contributed to the reform of colonial policy — though his views on Indian culture were themselves deeply prejudiced.

Mill's contribution to psychology was his *Analysis of the Phenomena of the Human Mind*, in which he characterized the sensory elements out of which ideas and associations are formed as atomistic **points of consciousness**. These were the building blocks of the mind — simple sensations that combine through association to form complex ideas.

Mill added little that was genuinely new to the principles of association developed by Hume and Hartley. He claimed that the main purpose of his work was to document further evidence for the principles of association. He did make one notable claim: that the principle of similarity is not a fundamental law of association, since it can be derived from the principle of contiguity. If two ideas are similar, Mill argued, it is because they share elements that were previously experienced together.

Mill's interest in psychology was secondary to his political projects. He was a utilitarian who believed that moral, social, and political questions should be determined by the principle of utility — the right action is the one that maximizes happiness and minimizes misery. His main concern was to adapt associationist psychology to utilitarian goals. A major task of education, he maintained, is to facilitate the association of individual happiness with benevolent social behavior.

This was psychology in the service of social engineering. If you could control the associations, you could shape the character. If you could shape the character, you could build a better society.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** James Mill believed that all complex ideas are built from simple sensory elements combined by association. Think about your concept of "justice." Can you trace it back to specific sensory experiences? Or is it the kind of concept that seems to require something more than sensation and association?

## J.S. Mill: Mental Chemistry and the Rational Unconscious

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) was lucky to survive his father's education. The young Mill was introduced to Greek at age 3, Latin and mathematics at age 6, philosophy at age 8, and logic at age 12. By his teenage years, he was studying economics and politics. The pressure was extraordinary. At age 20, Mill suffered a nervous breakdown — a crisis that forced him to reevaluate his personal and political orientation.

Mill recovered, in part, through his association with Harriet Taylor, who became his intellectual partner and, after the death of her first husband, his wife. Taylor's influence softened Mill's utilitarianism and inspired his feminist tract *The Subjection of Women* (1869). She also encouraged his willingness to challenge his father's intellectual legacy.

In *A System of Logic* (1843), Mill described his famous methods of comparative causal analysis — the methods of agreement, difference, and concomitant variation. But his most important contribution to psychology was his notion of **mental chemistry**: the idea that complex ideas can have properties that are not found in their component sensations, just as water has properties (like being wet) that neither hydrogen nor oxygen possesses alone.

Mill also made a discovery that would prove even more consequential. In defending Berkeley's theory of distance perception against the critique of Samuel Bailey, Mill was forced to postulate something radical: **unconscious inference**.

Bailey had argued that if neither visual nor tactile sensations alone can convey information about distance, then no mere association of such sensations can convey it either. Bailey, following Thomas Reid, claimed that we directly perceive distance visually. Mill defended Berkeley, but in doing so, he abandoned two fundamental principles of British empiricism.

First, Mill abandoned the notion that scientific theories should be restricted to objects of conscious experience. He postulated that distance perception involves an unconscious cognitive inference — a **rational unconscious** governed by norms of rationality and logical inference. This was the first explicit postulation of a rational unconscious in the history of psychology.

Second, Mill abandoned the notion that we have direct introspective access to all our mental states. He acknowledged that the original sensations upon which our perceptual inferences are based have become so integrated within perceptual judgment that they are "dim, confused, and difficult to be recalled."

Bailey protested that he had no introspective awareness of any such inferential process:

> "I cannot recognize in my experience such a process as the sensation of color suggesting an external thing. I directly and immediately see the colored external object."
> — Samuel Bailey (1855–1863, 2, p. 35)

Mill granted the point — and insisted that the inference was unconscious anyway. This was a stunning departure. The entire empiricist tradition, from Locke to Hume, had assumed that the mind is transparent to itself — that you can introspect on any mental state. Mill said no. Some of the most important mental processes are invisible to consciousness.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Try to introspect on how you perceive the distance of an object across the room. Can you catch yourself making an inference? Or does the distance just seem to be "there," directly perceived? If you cannot introspect on the inferential process, does that mean it is not happening?

## Bain: The First Modern Textbook

Alexander Bain (1818–1903) was the last great British empiricist and associationist psychologist. He was also, in many ways, the first modern psychologist — the first to produce a comprehensive textbook that integrated associationist psychology with contemporary physiology.

Bain was the self-educated son of a poor weaver. He attended Marischal College in Aberdeen, where he excelled academically, and later traveled to London, where he befriended John Stuart Mill. The two met regularly to discuss philosophy and psychology. Mill was so impressed that he asked Bain to read the proofs of *A System of Logic* and persuaded his own publisher to produce Bain's major work.

That work was *The Senses and the Intellect* (1855), followed by *The Emotions and the Will* (1859). Together, these two volumes constituted the first modern textbook of psychology. They set the standard for later psychology texts, whose authors felt obliged to include some account of the structure and function of the nervous system.

Bain maintained the traditional empiricist commitment to introspection, but he acknowledged that "consciousness is not indispensable to the operations of intellect" (1855, p. 316). He also recognized the innate basis of many features of human and animal psychology, such as emotions and instincts. In many respects, his texts were transitional — looking backward to traditional associationism and forward to evolutionary theory and late-19th-century neurophysiology.

In 1876, Bain founded the journal *Mind: A Quarterly Review of Psychology and Philosophy* with George Croom Robertson. The first issues were devoted to the question of whether psychology could be a genuine science. The founding editorial hoped that the journal would enable its readers "to procure a decision on . . . the scientific standing of psychology" (Robertson, 1876, p. 3). Over time, the focus shifted to purely philosophical questions, and *Mind* eventually became the premier British journal in philosophy — though "Psychology" was not dropped from the subtitle until 1974.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Bain founded a journal to determine whether psychology could be a science. That question is still debated today. What would you need to see to be convinced that psychology *is* a science? What would convince you that it is *not*?

## Voluntary Behavior and the Spencer-Bain Principle

Bain's most fertile contribution to psychology was his account of voluntary behavior. Traditional reflex theory held that behavior is always a response to external stimulation. But anyone can observe that organisms often behave spontaneously — a newborn lamb kicks and wriggles before it has learned to walk, a child waves its arms before it has any purpose in doing so.

Bain argued that the nervous system contains a store of energy that can be discharged spontaneously, without external stimulation:

> "There is in the constitution a store of nervous energy, accumulated during the nutrition and repose of the system, and proceeding into action with, or without, the application of outward stimulants."
> — Alexander Bain, *The Emotions and the Will* (1859, p. 328)

This spontaneously generated behavior becomes directed or purposive when it is associated with the experience of pleasure and pain. A newborn lamb initially generates random movements. Some of these movements bring it into contact with its mother's teat, which produces warmth and nourishment — pleasure. Over time, the movements that produce pleasure are repeated, and the lamb learns to walk toward its mother. This account of behavioral learning — in which behaviors followed by success, satisfaction, or pleasure tend to be repeated — later became known as the **Spencer-Bain principle** (named after Bain and Herbert Spencer, who independently developed similar ideas).

This was a remarkably modern insight. It anticipated the distinction between two forms of learning that would become central to 20th-century psychology: stimulus-determined (classical) conditioning and consequence-determined (operant) conditioning. Bain recognized instances of both.

He cited a number of stimulus-determined associative reflexes that Pavlov later characterized as conditioned reflexes:

> "The mere idea of a nauseous taste can excite the reality even to the point of vomiting. The sight of a person about to pass a sharp instrument over glass excites the well-known sensation in the teeth. The sight of food makes the saliva begin to flow."
> — Alexander Bain (1868, p. 90)

That last example — the sight of food making saliva flow — is exactly the phenomenon Pavlov would make the centerpiece of his experimental program forty years later. Bain observed it, noted it, and moved on. He did not see it as the foundation of a new science. Sometimes the most important observations are the ones that are made too early to be appreciated.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Think about a habit you have developed — reaching for your phone when you are bored, feeling hungry when you smell coffee, tensing up when you hear a particular sound. Is this habit stimulus-determined (triggered by a cue) or consequence-determined (shaped by what follows)? Or both?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The British associationists — James Mill, John Stuart Mill, and Alexander Bain — built the most sophisticated psychological theory of the 19th century. They reduced the mind to ideas and associations, developed methods for studying it scientifically, discovered the rational unconscious, and anticipated classical and operant conditioning. But they did not build a laboratory. They did not measure reaction times or map sensory thresholds. They did not create an institutional home for psychology as an experimental science. That work would be done in Germany — by physiologists who cared less about ideas and more about nerves.

In *From Soul to Mind: The Emergence of Psychology from Erasmus Darwin to William James* (1997), the historian Edward S. Reed traced how psychology gradually shifted from a "science of the soul" to a "science of the mind" over the course of the 19th century. Reed argued that the British associationists — particularly J.S. Mill and Bain — played a crucial role in this transition by developing the concept of the unconscious, which allowed psychologists to study mental processes without committing to a soul or to the transparency of consciousness.

## Speaking of Associations and Unconscious Inference...

- A music teacher in Seoul plays a chord on the piano and asks her student: "What do you feel?" The student says: "Sadness." The teacher asks: "Why?" The student cannot say. The association between the minor chord and the feeling of sadness is unconscious — built through years of cultural exposure. The student perceives the sadness directly, without inferring it. This is Mill's unconscious inference in action.

- A chef in São Paulo creates a new dish by combining ingredients that have never been combined before — fermented black beans with passion fruit and smoked fish. The result has a flavor that none of the ingredients possess alone. This is mental chemistry in the kitchen: the whole is genuinely different from the sum of its parts.

- A cricketer in Chennai watches the bowler's arm and begins to move before the ball is released. He does not consciously analyze the bowler's wrist position, the angle of release, or the likely trajectory. His body responds to cues he cannot articulate. This is Bain's conditioned reflex — and it is also, in a sense, the Spencer-Bain principle: the movements that have led to successful hits in the past are the ones that are automatically triggered now.

---

The associationists had mapped the mind from the inside — through introspection, logic, and philosophical argument. But a very different kind of mapping was underway in the hospitals and laboratories of Europe. Doctors were cutting open skulls, stimulating brains with electricity, and discovering that specific regions of the cortex control specific functions. The mind, it turned out, had a geography. And the first person to claim he could read it from the outside was a Viennese physician who measured bumps.
