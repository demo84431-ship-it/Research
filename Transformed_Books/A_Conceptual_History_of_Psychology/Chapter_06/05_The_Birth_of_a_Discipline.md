# Chapter 06 · Module 5
# The Birth of a Discipline — Physiological Psychology vs. Objective Psychology
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In 1873, Wilhelm Wundt sat down to write a book that would change the course of psychology. He was a German physiologist who had studied with Helmholtz, and he was deeply influenced by the experimental tradition we have been tracing through this chapter. He had the methods. He had the tools. He had the theoretical framework. What he did not have was a name for what he was building.

Wundt called his book *Principles of Physiological Psychology*. The title was carefully chosen. The word "physiological" indicated that his psychology was based on the experimental methods of physiology — measurement, controlled stimulation, systematic observation. But the word "psychology" indicated something equally important: that the explanatory concepts of his science would be psychological, not physiological. He was borrowing physiology's methods, not its explanations.

This was a crucial distinction. And it put Wundt on one side of a divide that would define psychology for the next century. On the other side stood Ivan Sechenov, who argued that psychology should borrow both physiology's methods *and* its explanatory concepts. For Sechenov, the goal was not a psychology that uses physiological tools. It was a physiology of behavior.

Between them, Wundt and Sechenov proposed two very different futures for psychology. One future involved introspection, consciousness, and the study of mental processes. The other involved reflexes, stimulation, and the study of behavior. Both futures would be realized. But they would not coexist peacefully.

---

## Wundt's Physiological Psychology

Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920) is often called the founder of experimental psychology, and the title is not entirely undeserved. In 1879, he established the first formal laboratory for experimental psychology at the University of Leipzig. He founded the journal *Philosophical Studies* in 1881. He trained a generation of students who went on to establish psychology departments across Europe and America.

But Wundt's psychology was not what many people think. He is often credited with founding the psychology of consciousness — the introspective study of mental states. This is partly true. Wundt did believe that the proper subject matter of psychology is conscious experience. But his approach was far more sophisticated than simple introspection.

Wundt insisted on the autonomy of psychological explanation with respect to physiological explanation. He called his form of experimental psychology **physiological psychology** because it was based on the experimental methods of physiology, not because it was based on the explanatory concepts of physiology. The distinction matters. When Helmholtz measured the speed of neural conduction, he was doing physiology. When Wundt measured the time it takes to respond to a stimulus, he was doing psychology — using physiological methods, but asking psychological questions.

Wundt also recognized that experimental methods alone cannot capture the full range of psychological phenomena. He devoted the second half of his career to *Völkerpsychologie* — the psychology of peoples — which he treated as a necessary complement to laboratory science. Language, myth, custom, art — these are products of culture and history, and they cannot be studied in a laboratory. They require the interpretive methods of the human sciences.

This aspect of Wundt's work is often overlooked. He is remembered as the founder of the experimental laboratory, not as the advocate of cultural psychology. But Wundt himself saw both as essential parts of a complete psychology.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Wundt distinguished between experimental psychology (the study of conscious experience in the laboratory) and Völkerpsychologie (the study of cultural products like language and custom). Are these the same discipline? Could a single science of psychology encompass both? Or are they fundamentally different enterprises that share a name?

## Sechenov's Objective Psychology

On the other side of the divide stood Ivan Sechenov. In his 1871 article "Who Must Investigate the Problems of Psychology and How," Sechenov argued that progress in psychology could be achieved only by developing reflexive physiological theories of human and animal behavior. His approach was **objective psychology** — a psychology based not only on the experimental methods of physiology but also on its explanatory concepts.

Sechenov was committed to the strong continuity of human and animal psychology. He maintained that the study of human psychology is best approached through the study of animal psychology, in which the basic reflexive components of human psychology are revealed in their elemental form:

> "It is clear then that the psychical phenomena of animals, and not those of man, should be used as the primary material for studying psychical phenomena."
> — Ivan Sechenov (1871/1973, p. 339)

This principle — that animal psychology should be the foundation of human psychology — became the foundation of 20th-century behaviorist psychology. Sechenov was not a behaviorist; he did not deny the existence of consciousness. But he believed that consciousness is best understood through the study of reflexive behavior, not through introspection.

Sechenov's approach was the mirror image of Wundt's. Where Wundt said "use physiology's methods but keep psychology's explanations," Sechenov said "use both." Where Wundt studied conscious experience, Sechenov studied reflexive behavior. Where Wundt worked with human subjects in a laboratory, Sechenov worked with frogs on a table.

Both approaches were scientifically legitimate. Both produced valuable knowledge. But they were pulling psychology in opposite directions — toward the study of consciousness and toward the study of behavior. The tension between them would define the discipline for the next century.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Sechenov argued that animal psychology should be the foundation for understanding human psychology. Is this reasonable? What can a frog tell you about human consciousness? What can it tell you about human behavior? Is the gap between animal and human psychology a difference of degree or a difference of kind?

## Epiphenomenalism: The Steam Whistle

The sensory-motor theory of the nervous system raised a question that had been lurking since Descartes: if the nervous system is a machine, what role does consciousness play?

Thomas Huxley (1825–1895), the biologist and champion of Darwin's theory of evolution, offered a provocative answer at the 1874 meeting of the British Association in Belfast. In his address "On the Hypotheses That Animals Are Automata, and Its History," Huxley argued that consciousness is an epiphenomenal by-product of the nervous system — a side effect that plays no causal role in behavior.

Huxley's analogy was memorable:

> "The consciousness of brutes would appear to be related to the mechanism of their body simply as a collateral product of its working, and to be as completely without any power of modifying that working as the steam-whistle which accompanies the work of a locomotive engine is without influence on its machinery."
> — Thomas Huxley (1874, p. 575)

The steam whistle accompanies the engine's work but does not drive it. Similarly, consciousness accompanies neural activity but does not cause behavior. Volition — the feeling of willing an action — is not the cause of the action. It is merely "the symbol of that state of the brain which is the immediate cause of the act."

Huxley cited cases of animals and humans engaging in coordinated, purposive behavior despite decortication or temporary loss of consciousness. Frogs with their cortex removed will still try to remove chemical irritants applied to their bodies. A French army sergeant with a head wound would eat, drink, smoke, and walk during periods of unconsciousness, while seemingly insensitive to pain.

Yet at most, these cases demonstrated that purposive behavior is possible *without* consciousness — not that consciousness is impotent *when it is present*. As Huxley himself noted, a frog with a cortex responds to sights and sounds that the decorticated frog ignores. And the sergeant was normally truthful and honest, but lied and cheated during his unconscious lapses.

**Epiphenomenalism** was not mandated by either materialism or the sensory-motor theory. Neither La Mettrie nor Carpenter embraced the doctrine. Many psychologists continued to insist on the causal role of consciousness in behavior. But epiphenomenalism found its most forceful expression in early American behaviorist psychology — and the debate it sparked has never been resolved.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Huxley compared consciousness to a steam whistle — present but causally impotent. Think about a decision you made today — choosing what to eat, what to say, how to spend your time. Was the *feeling* of deciding actually the cause of your action? Or was the feeling just a report — a conscious afterthought generated by neural processes that had already determined the action?

## Ideomotor Behavior and Unconscious Cerebration

William Carpenter (1813–1885), professor of physiology at University College London, proposed a more nuanced view. He argued that ideas associated with behavior can come to generate behavior automatically — what he called **ideomotor behavior**. When you imagine yourself reaching for a cup, the idea of reaching can trigger the actual movement without your conscious will.

Carpenter used this concept to explain the efficacy of hypnosis and suggestion. In hypnosis, ideas produce behavior without the agent willing the behavior or being conscious of the connection between the idea and the behavior. The idea itself, once implanted, generates the action.

Carpenter also introduced the notion of **unconscious cerebration** — unconscious thought processes that operate beneath the threshold of consciousness. He employed this concept to explain involuntary attention, unconscious problem solving, and dreams. Mill borrowed the term for his own account of unconscious inference in perception.

But Carpenter did not abandon the will entirely. He argued that the will never initiates behavior directly but can act only by "direction of the attention" — focusing on ideas that automatically generate behavior. Attention strengthens certain ideas at the expense of others and enables individuals to determine which automatic behavior comes into play. Inattention or misdirected attention can result in bad habits and dissolution.

In his medical practice, Carpenter recommended a peculiar mix of moral exhortation and rote learning characteristic of Victorian morality. He urged patients to focus their attention on pledges to avoid strong drink, drugs, and prostitution and to ingrain good conduct through repetition until it became habitual. This was, in effect, a therapeutic application of ideomotor theory: change the ideas, change the behavior.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Carpenter claimed that ideas can generate behavior automatically — that imagining an action can trigger the action itself. Try this: vividly imagine biting into a lemon. Can you feel your mouth pucker? Is this an example of ideomotor behavior? If ideas can trigger actions without conscious will, what does that mean for the concept of free will?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> By the end of the 19th century, the stage was set for psychology's institutional birth. The physiology was in place — the reflex arc, the sensory-motor theory, the localization of function, the measurement of neural conduction. The methods were developed — experimental ablation, electrical stimulation, psychophysics. The theoretical frameworks were articulated — Wundt's physiological psychology, Sechenov's objective psychology, Huxley's epiphenomenalism, Carpenter's ideomotor theory. What remained was the question that had haunted psychology since Descartes: is the mind a machine, a ghost, or something in between? The 20th century would not answer this question. It would, instead, build two psychologies — one that studied consciousness and one that studied behavior — and spend a century arguing about which one was real.

In *Pioneers of Psychology* (1996), the historian Raymond E. Fancher traced how the institutional and intellectual foundations laid by 19th-century physiologists shaped the emergence of psychology as a professional discipline. Fancher showed that the path from physiology to psychology was not a straight line but a tangled web of competing theories, personal rivalries, and institutional pressures. Meanwhile, in "Practical Phrenology as Psychological Counseling in the 19th-Century United States" (2001), Michael M. Sokal documented how the popular reception of phrenology — despite its scientific discrediting — nourished public expectations for a scientifically useful form of psychology, laying the groundwork for the applied psychology that would flourish in 20th-century America.

## Speaking of Two Psychologies...

- A psychologist in Berlin designs an experiment in which participants press a button as quickly as possible after seeing a light. She measures reaction time — the interval between stimulus and response. She is doing Wundt's physiological psychology: using experimental methods to study a psychological process. She does not claim to be explaining the brain. She claims to be measuring the mind.

- A behavioral researcher in Moscow trains a rat to press a lever for food. He records the rate of lever-pressing as a function of the schedule of reinforcement. He is doing Sechenov's objective psychology: studying behavior through its environmental determinants. He does not claim to be studying the rat's consciousness. He claims to be studying the rat's behavior.

- A philosopher in São Paulo asks: "Is the mind a computer?" Some say yes — the mind processes information, stores memories, and generates outputs, just like a machine. Others say no — the mind experiences, feels, and understands, and no machine can do that. They are replaying, without knowing it, the debate between Huxley (consciousness is a steam whistle) and his critics (consciousness is the engine). The question has not changed. The answers have not converged.

---

The 19th century had built the tools, methods, and theoretical foundations for a science of psychology. But it had not yet built the institution. That would happen in the 1880s, when Wundt opened his laboratory in Leipzig and psychology became, for the first time, an autonomous academic discipline. But the story of psychology's institutional birth is also the story of its fragmentation — the beginning of a century-long argument about what psychology is, what it studies, and whether it can ever be unified. That argument begins in the next chapter.
