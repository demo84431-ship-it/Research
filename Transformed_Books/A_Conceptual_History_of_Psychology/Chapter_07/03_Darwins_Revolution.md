# Darwin's Revolution — Natural Selection and Its Enemies
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

On December 27, 1831, a 23-year-old Englishman stood on the deck of HMS Beagle as it sailed from Plymouth Harbor. His name was Charles Darwin, and he was not the ship's naturalist — that role belonged to the surgeon Robert McKormick. Darwin was an unpaid gentleman companion, hired to share the captain's dinner table and conversation. The previous captain had shot himself after three years at sea, and the current captain, Robert Fitzroy, was worried about his own mental stability. He needed someone to talk to.

Darwin had failed at everything. He had started studying medicine at Edinburgh but could not stomach watching operations. He had transferred to Cambridge to study theology, on his father's advice, but the prospect of a career as a country clergyman proved no more inspiring. He graduated with a poor third-class degree — about the equivalent of a 2.0 GPA. His father despaired that he would ever amount to anything.

But Darwin was a born naturalist. From childhood, he was an avid collector of rocks, shells, and plants. At Cambridge, he befriended the botanist John Stevens Henslow, who recommended him for the Beagle position. Darwin's uncle paid £2,000 for the privilege. And so, with no particular qualifications and no particular plan, Darwin set sail on a voyage that would change the history of science.

He did not know it yet. Neither did anyone else.

---

## The Voyage of the Beagle

The Beagle's mission was to survey the coastlines of New Zealand, Australia, and South America. Darwin was able to study and collect biological specimens at many of the landfalls, including the Galápagos Islands — a volcanic archipelago about 600 miles off the coast of South America. He became increasingly intrigued by the multitude of species he encountered and their differences in different environments, such as the varieties of tortoises and finches he discovered on the islands.

Throughout the voyage, Darwin collected a mass of biogeographical evidence for species change — evidence that species in different environments have different characteristics, even when they appear to be related. But he had no mechanism to explain it. He had read Lamarck and rejected the inheritance of acquired characteristics as insufficient. He had read his grandfather Erasmus Darwin and found the theory vague. He needed something more.

One of the books Darwin took with him on the Beagle was Lyell's *Principles of Geology*, which undermined biblical estimates of the age of the Earth at around 3,000 years. Lyell's geological estimate — about 100 million years — gave Darwin the deep time his theory would require. But the mechanism was still missing.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Darwin spent five years on the Beagle collecting evidence, but he did not develop his theory until two years after returning to England. The evidence alone was not enough. He needed an idea — a way of connecting the evidence into a coherent explanation. Where do scientific ideas come from? Is discovery a logical process, or is it something more like a creative leap?

## The Theory of Natural Selection

Darwin returned to England in 1836 and began working on a theory to account for the "descent with modification" of species. He later identified two sources of his theory.

The first was the established practice of artificial selection by agricultural breeders. Darwin observed how breeders develop desirable characteristics in their animals — high quality of fleece, large body mass — through selective breeding. Occasionally, a harsh winter or drought would produce the same outcome naturally, by weeding out the weaker stock.

The second was Malthus's *Essay on the Principle Population*. In October 1838, Darwin read Malthus "for amusement" and realized that the competition for limited food resources would naturally ensure that variations in inheritable characteristics conducive to survival would tend to be passed on to future generations, whereas those that were not would not:

> "It at once struck me that under these circumstances favorable variations would tend to be preserved and unfavorable ones tend to be destroyed. The result of this would be the formation of new species."
> — Charles Darwin, *Autobiography* (1892/1958, pp. 42–43)

This was the insight that became the theory of **natural selection**: organisms exhibit chance variations in their characteristics. They produce more offspring than can survive, creating a struggle for existence. Those chance variations conducive to survival are naturally selected, since organisms possessing them tend to survive and reproduce, while those lacking them tend to die off.

The key innovation was the word "chance." In Darwin's theory, variations are not directed toward any goal. They are random. Natural selection does not drive organisms toward perfection. It simply eliminates those that happen to be poorly adapted to their current environment. There is no purpose, no direction, no progress. There is only variation, selection, and time.

Darwin represented evolution as an "irregularly branching tree" — not a ladder. He cautioned against the use of terms like "higher" and "lower" in comparing different species. This was a radical departure from every evolutionary theory that had come before.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Darwin's theory eliminated purpose from evolution. There is no goal, no direction, no inherent progress. Species do not evolve "toward" anything. They simply change in response to environmental pressures. Does this bother you? Does it feel wrong to describe human existence as the product of a purposeless process? Or does the absence of purpose make evolution more elegant, not less?

## Darwin's Delay: Twenty Years of Hesitation

Darwin developed his theory around 1840. He sketched versions of it in 1842 and 1844. But he did not publish it until 1859 — almost twenty years after his return to England.

His delay had several causes. He spent years organizing and cataloguing his specimen collection. He wrote a book on barnacles. He suffered from chronic illness that restricted him to only a few hours of work each day. But a major reason may have been his genuine reluctance to publish, given his justified fears of the reaction he anticipated.

Darwin was not wrong to be afraid. He was proposing a rigorously materialistic and mechanistic theory that was bound to provoke a hostile reaction among theologians, the general public, and many naturalists. He had established his scientific reputation and was elected a fellow of the Royal Society in 1839. He was in no rush to destroy it.

Darwin might never have published in his own lifetime had he not received a paper from Alfred Russel Wallace in 1858. Wallace, a fellow English naturalist working in the Malay Archipelago, had independently developed a theory almost identical to Darwin's. During a bout of tropical fever, Wallace suddenly realized that natural selection could serve as the mechanism for evolutionary change and wrote his paper within a few days.

Darwin's first inclination was to cede priority to Wallace. But his friends persuaded him to have Wallace's paper and a hastily prepared statement of his own theory read at the July 1858 meeting of the Linnean Society. Neither Darwin nor Wallace was present. The papers were read into the minutes. Neither attracted particular attention.

The following year, Darwin published *On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection*. The first print run of 1,250 copies sold on the first day.

## The Explosion: Huxley, Wilberforce, and the Oxford Debate

The publication of *The Origin of Species* immediately became the object of religious and scientific controversy. Wallace graciously ceded priority to Darwin and characterized the theory as "Darwinism."

The most famous instance of the controversy was the Oxford meeting of the British Association in 1860, at which Thomas Huxley — who became known as "Darwin's bulldog" — championed Darwin's theory against Bishop Samuel Wilberforce. In the course of his critique, Wilberforce insultingly inquired of Huxley whether he was descended from an ape on his grandmother's or grandfather's side. Huxley responded that he would "rather have a miserable ape for a grandfather" than a man capable of abusing his intellect and resorting to ridicule in serious scientific debate.

Darwin wrote to Huxley in congratulation:

> "How durst you attack a live bishop in that fashion? I am quite ashamed of you! Have you no respect for fine lawn sleeves? By jove, you seem to have done it well."
> — Charles Darwin (cited in Bibby, 1959, p. 70)

The Oxford meeting was not as dramatic as later portrayed. Contrary to legend, Admiral Fitzroy did not wander around holding a Bible above his head. But for Fitzroy, a staunch believer in the literal biblical account of creation, Darwin turned out to have been an unfortunate choice of companion. Fitzroy blamed himself for indirectly contributing to Darwin's blasphemous theory. Five years later, in 1865, he cut his own throat.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** The Oxford debate between Huxley and Wilberforce is one of the most famous episodes in the history of science. But was it really a debate between science and religion? Huxley later said the real issue was not whether evolution is true, but whether scientists have the right to pursue truth without theological interference. Is this distinction still relevant today?

## The Problems: Time, Heredity, and the Eclipse of Darwinism

Darwin's theory was almost immediately beset by internal problems. The effects of natural selection operating on minor variations required vast amounts of time — on the order of 100 million years. But calculations by the physicist Lord Kelvin, based on thermodynamics, put the age of the Earth at only about 30 million years, later revised downward to 20 million. This was far too short for Darwinian evolution.

Fleeming Jenkin, a Scottish engineer, argued that favorable variations would be eliminated through dilution within a few generations, given Darwin's "blending" theory of reproduction. If a tall organism mates with a short one, the offspring are medium height. The favorable variation disappears. Jenkin's objection was not fatal — Wallace pointed out that Darwin could postulate a greater range of variation — but it was damaging.

Most significantly, Weismann's theory of the germ-plasm undermined the still-favored conception of evolution as a progressive developmental process based on the inheritance of acquired characteristics. If heredity is "hard" — if the mechanism of inheritance is independent of an organism's life experience — then Lamarckian evolution is impossible. Natural selection becomes the only viable mechanism.

But most biologists did not want to accept this. They continued to endorse progressive developmental theories. Ernst Haeckel, the foremost German advocate of Darwinian theory, treated the individual development of the embryo as the model for the progressive development of species. His **recapitulation theory** — that ontogeny (the growth of individual organisms) recapitulates phylogeny (the evolutionary history of a species) — represented humankind, or at least white European races, as the pinnacle of evolutionary progress.

Darwin resisted such a conception, given his vision of evolution as an irregularly branching tree. But he failed to convince most of his fellow scientists. The late 19th century saw the **eclipse of Darwinism** — a period when Darwin's theory was dismissed as a historical curiosity. Most theorists continued to hold progressive developmental theories. It was only when experimental biologists turned their attention to the mechanisms for the inheritance of acquired characteristics at the turn of the century and found them wanting that the climate began to shift back in favor of natural selection.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Darwin's theory was rejected by most scientists for decades after 1859 — not because of religious opposition, but because of scientific objections (time, heredity, the lack of a mechanism for variation). The popular narrative says Darwin won immediately. He did not. What does this tell you about how science actually works? Is the truth always recognized quickly?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Darwin carefully avoided discussing human evolution in *The Origin of Species*. He waited twelve years before publishing *The Descent of Man*. Why the hesitation? Was it scientific caution, personal fear, or strategic calculation? When should a scientist publish a controversial finding — immediately, or only when the evidence is overwhelming?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Darwin's theory of natural selection was one of the most significant intellectual accomplishments of the 19th century. It eliminated purpose, direction, and progress from the biological world. It replaced the ladder of nature with a branching tree. It replaced divine design with blind variation and selective retention. And yet — as Darwin himself recognized — it raised as many questions as it answered. How do variations arise? How is heredity transmitted? Can natural selection account for the vast differences between humans and even the highest primates? These questions would occupy biology and psychology for the next century. Some of them have still not been fully answered.

## Speaking of Delays, Debates, and Rejections...

- A researcher in São Paulo spends ten years developing a theory of urban migration before publishing it. Her colleagues ask why she waited so long. She says: "I was afraid of being wrong." Darwin waited twenty. The fear of being wrong — and of the consequences of being right — is not unique to the 19th century.

- A university student in Lagos reads about the Scopes trial of 1925 and is shocked that a teacher was prosecuted for teaching evolution. Then she learns that in some parts of her own country, evolution is still not taught in schools. The controversy Darwin started in 1859 is not over.

- A physicist in Seoul explains to a friend why scientific theories are often rejected at first: "New ideas threaten existing power structures. If evolution is true, then the Church's authority is undermined. If climate change is real, then the fossil fuel industry is threatened. The pattern is the same." The friend asks: "So how do you know which rejected theories are right?" The physicist pauses. "You don't. Not until the evidence becomes overwhelming."

---

Darwin had proposed a mechanism for evolution. But he had carefully avoided applying it to human beings. *The Origin of Species* contained only a single sentence about human evolution, promising that natural selection would "throw light" on the origin of man. Now, twelve years later, he was finally ready to address the question — forced by Wallace's claim that natural selection alone could not explain the human mind. The book that followed would be his most controversial: *The Descent of Man*.
