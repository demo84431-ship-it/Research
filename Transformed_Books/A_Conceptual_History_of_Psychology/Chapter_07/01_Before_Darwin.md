# Chapter 07 · Module 1
# Before Darwin — Evolution's Early Prophets
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In the winter of 1826, a 79-year-old French naturalist died alone in Paris. He was blind, impoverished, and largely forgotten. His name was Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, and he had spent the last years of his life dictating his scientific papers to his daughter because he could no longer see to write them. The Paris Museum of Natural History, where he had worked for decades, refused him a pension. His colleagues dismissed his theories as speculative nonsense. When he died, his family could barely afford a grave. He was buried in a temporary plot, and when the lease expired, his bones were exhumed and moved to a communal ossuary. Nobody recorded where.

This was the man who first proposed a systematic theory of evolution — the idea that species change over time, that life on Earth has a history, that the living world is not fixed but fluid. Lamarck's theory was wrong in its details, but its central insight — that organisms adapt to their environments and pass those adaptations to their offspring — would dominate biology for half a century. It would shape the thinking of Charles Darwin himself. And it would be rejected, eventually, in favor of a very different mechanism: natural selection.

But before Lamarck, before Darwin, before the debates that would split science and religion for a century, there was a simpler question: could the living world change at all? For most of Western history, the answer was no.

---

## The Great Chain of Being

For centuries, the Western world accepted what the philosopher Arthur Lovejoy later called the **great chain of being** — a hierarchical ordering of all existence, from God at the top, through angels, humans, animals, plants, and minerals at the bottom. This was the **scala naturae**, the ladder of nature, and it was immutable. Every species had its fixed place. Every creature was designed by God for its particular role. The world was a completed creation, and nothing fundamental could change.

This was not just a religious doctrine. It was a scientific one. Aristotle had argued that species are eternal and unchanging. The medieval Church had agreed. The Enlightenment had challenged many traditional beliefs, but not this one. Even Helvetius and Adam Ferguson, who developed theories of social progress, presupposed a fixed human nature. Gall's phrenology assumed a fixed hierarchy of neurological function. The chain was so deeply embedded in Western thought that it seemed self-evident.

Then, in the late 18th century, cracks began to appear. The fossil record showed that species had existed in the past that no longer existed in the present. Geological discoveries suggested that the Earth was far older than anyone had imagined. And a small group of thinkers began to ask a dangerous question: what if species are not fixed? What if they change?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** The idea of a fixed hierarchy of nature — with humans above animals, animals above plants — still shapes how many people think about the living world. When someone says humans are "higher" than apes, or that dolphins are "more intelligent" than fish, they are invoking the scala naturae. Is this way of thinking inevitable? Or is it a cultural inheritance that could be different?

## Erasmus Darwin: Habits Inherited

The first serious evolutionary theory in English came from an unlikely source: a portly, gouty physician from Lichfield named Erasmus Darwin (1731–1802). He was the grandfather of Charles Darwin, but he was famous in his own right — a successful doctor, a prolific inventor, and a popular poet. His *Zoonomia* (1794–1796) was a sprawling medical encyclopedia that contained, buried among its pages, a theory of evolution.

Erasmus Darwin's theory was an extension of Hartley's associationist psychology. He argued that learned associations and habits engender modifications of the nervous system that are passed on to future generations. If a parent learns to associate a particular stimulus with a particular response, that association becomes part of the parent's nervous constitution — and is inherited by the child. Over many generations, these accumulated habits produce new species.

Erasmus Darwin was a committed materialist. He dismissed the notion of an autonomous mental realm as a "ghost story." According to his **fluid materialism** — the theory that the electrical nature of the nervous system is the basis of life and mind — there is no soul, no spirit, no immaterial mind. There is only matter, organized in increasingly complex ways.

His work was published in the decades following the French Revolution, and it attracted the same degree of hostility as La Mettrie's *Man a Machine*. The idea that human beings are products of material processes, shaped by habit and experience rather than divine design, was as dangerous in the 1790s as it had been in the 1740s.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Erasmus Darwin argued that habits acquired during a lifetime can be inherited by offspring. Imagine a blacksmith whose arms grow strong through years of hammering. Would his children be born with stronger arms? This seems absurd today, but for most of the 19th century, it was accepted by many serious scientists. Why do you think the idea was so persistent?

## Lamarck: The Drive Toward Perfection

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744–1829) developed the first systematic theory of evolution. Like Erasmus Darwin, he stressed the material continuity of animal species and treated humankind as the most complex form of animal life. But Lamarck went further. He proposed a specific mechanism for evolutionary change: the **inheritance of acquired characteristics**.

According to Lamarck, organisms possess an innate drive to perfect themselves. They strive to adapt to their environments, and the modifications they develop during their lifetimes — through increased use of certain organs or through responses to environmental pressures — are inherited by their offspring. The classic example: if a giraffe stretches its neck to reach high leaves, its offspring will inherit a slightly longer neck. Over many generations, this process produces the characteristically long necks of modern giraffes.

Lamarck believed that this mechanism explained the "transmutation" of species over time — a linear progression from lower and simpler to higher and more complex organisms. Evolution was not random. It was directed. It had a goal: perfection.

The **inheritance of acquired characteristics** played a significant role in late-19th-century evolutionary theory, including Darwin's own. But few embraced Lamarck's specific theory. This was partly because of its association with republican and socialist political movements. Political radicals treated Lamarck's theory as a naturalistic justification for social progress through active "development from below" — the idea that the working class could improve its condition through effort and pass those improvements to its children. Lamarck died in poverty and disrepute.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Lamarck's theory was rejected partly because of its political associations. Can you think of a modern scientific theory that has been resisted not because of its evidence but because of its implications? How do politics and science interact when the stakes are high?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** The inheritance of acquired characteristics was rejected by mainstream biology in the early 20th century. But modern epigenetics has shown that environmental experiences can sometimes influence gene expression in offspring — a phenomenon sometimes called "soft inheritance." Is Lamarck making a comeback? Or is epigenetics fundamentally different from what Lamarck imagined?

## The Road to Darwin: Lyell and Malthus

Two works of the early 19th century laid the intellectual groundwork for Darwin's theory, even though neither was about evolution.

Charles Lyell's *Principles of Geology* (1830–1838) established that geological change is gradual and uniform, operating over enormous periods of time. Lyell estimated the age of the Earth at around 100 million years — far older than the few thousand years allowed by biblical chronology. This was crucial. Evolution by natural selection requires vast stretches of time for small, gradual changes to accumulate. Without Lyell's deep time, Darwin's theory would have been impossible.

Thomas Malthus's *Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798) provided the engine. Malthus argued that populations increase geometrically while food supplies increase only arithmetically. When populations outgrow their food supply — as they invariably do — a struggle for existence ensues. Not everyone can survive. The question is: who does?

Malthus was writing about human societies, not animal species. But his logic applied to all living things. Every organism produces more offspring than can possibly survive. Every generation is a competition. And in that competition, some individuals are better equipped than others. This insight — that life is a struggle, and that the struggle has consequences — would become the cornerstone of Darwin's theory.

In the history of science, the most important ideas are sometimes not the ones that answer questions, but the ones that make new questions possible. Lyell gave evolution time. Malthus gave it an engine. The mechanism — natural selection — was still missing. That would come from a young naturalist who read Malthus on a rainy afternoon in 1838 and saw, in a flash, how it all fit together.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Malthus argued that population growth inevitably outstrips food supply, creating a struggle for existence. Is this still true today? Modern agriculture feeds billions more people than Malthus imagined. Has technology broken Malthus's law, or merely postponed its consequences?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The early evolutionary theories of Erasmus Darwin and Lamarck shared a common assumption: that evolution is progressive, directed, and purposeful. Organisms drive toward perfection. Species ascend a ladder from simple to complex. This was the scala naturae repackaged as a dynamic process — the same hierarchy, but now achieved through effort rather than divine design. Darwin's great innovation was to strip evolution of this purpose. In his theory, there is no ladder, no direction, no goal. There is only variation, selection, and time. The living world is not climbing toward perfection. It is branching, endlessly and without purpose, into forms that happen to survive.

## Speaking of Progress and Purpose...

- A fitness instructor in Lagos tells her clients: "Your body adapts to stress. The more you train, the stronger you get. And if you start young, your children will benefit." This is Lamarck's logic in everyday language — the belief that effort produces biological improvement that can be passed on. It is also, in a sense, true at the epigenetic level, though not in the way Lamarck imagined.

- A history teacher in São Paulo explains the fall of the Roman Empire by saying: "Civilizations rise and fall, just like species. The strong survive, the weak perish." This is Spencer's social Darwinism applied to history — the idea that the same laws govern empires and organisms. It is a powerful metaphor. It is also, as we will see, deeply misleading.

- A tech entrepreneur in Seoul pitches his startup by saying: "We are disrupting the market. The old companies are dinosaurs. They cannot adapt." The metaphor of evolution as competition, with the fittest surviving and the unfit perishing, has become the default language of business. Darwin would have recognized the logic. He would have been less comfortable with the implication that business success reflects biological superiority.

---

Erasmus Darwin said habits could be inherited. Lamarck said organisms drive toward perfection. Malthus said life is a struggle. Lyell said the Earth is old enough for slow change to matter. The pieces were assembling. But the man who would put them together — and who would propose a mechanism so radical that it changed the way we understand ourselves — was a 22-year-old who had just failed at medicine and theology and was about to set sail on a five-year voyage around the world. His name was Charles Darwin.
