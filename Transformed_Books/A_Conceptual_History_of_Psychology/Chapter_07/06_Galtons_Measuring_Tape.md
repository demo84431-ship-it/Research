# Chapter 07 · Module 6
# Galton's Measuring Tape — Individual Differences and the Shadow of Eugenics
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In 1884, a peculiar laboratory opened at the International Health Exhibition in London. Visitors could have their height measured, their arm span recorded, their breathing capacity tested, their reaction time timed, their color vision checked, and their hearing acuity assessed. The laboratory charged threepence per test. Over nine months, more than 9,000 people walked through its doors. The man who designed it was Charles Darwin's half-cousin, and he was not interested in health. He was interested in measuring human beings.

His name was Francis Galton, and he was obsessed with measurement. He measured the beauty of women in different parts of the British Isles (London women were the most beautiful, he concluded, though his criteria were never specified). He measured the boredom of audiences at scientific lectures (he sat at the back and recorded how often people fidgeted). He tried to measure the effectiveness of prayer (he compared the life spans of people who were prayed for with those who were not and found no difference). He once developed a statistical method for making the perfect cup of tea.

Galton was brilliant, eccentric, and dangerous. He invented the correlation coefficient. He pioneered the use of twin studies. He coined the phrase "nature vs. nurture." And he founded the eugenics movement — a program of selective human breeding that would lead, within half a century, to forced sterilization programs in Europe and America, and ultimately to the horrors of Nazi racial hygiene.

---

## Measuring Human Beings

Francis Galton (1822–1911) was born into a wealthy Birmingham family. His grandfather was Erasmus Darwin; his half-cousin was Charles Darwin. He was a child prodigy who could read at age two and a half. He studied at Cambridge but was devastated by the mathematical tripos examination, which he felt rewarded memorization over understanding. He spent his twenties traveling in Africa, where he developed an interest in mapping and measurement that would define his career.

After reading Darwin's *Origin of Species* in 1859, Galton became obsessed with a question: if natural selection operates on inherited variations, how much of human ability is determined by heredity? He decided to find out by measuring.

Galton established an **anthropometric laboratory** — a laboratory for the measurement of human characteristics such as sensory acuity and reaction time. He collected data on thousands of subjects and analyzed it using statistical methods that he invented or refined. He introduced the concepts of the median, the percentile, the correlation coefficient, and regression toward the mean. He developed the first systematic methods for studying the relationship between heredity and ability.

Galton's statistical innovations were genuinely groundbreaking. His development of the **correlation coefficient** — a measure of the strength of the relationship between two variables — and his discovery of **regression toward the mean** — the tendency for extreme measurements to become less extreme in subsequent generations — became foundational tools of modern statistics. His student Karl Pearson later refined these methods and established the statistical framework that psychologists still use today.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Galton invented the correlation coefficient — one of the most widely used statistical tools in psychology. He also founded the eugenics movement. Does the origin of a tool affect its value? Should psychologists use statistical methods developed by someone whose ethical views were deeply problematic? Or is a tool neutral, regardless of who created it?

## Nature vs. Nurture

Galton's most famous contribution to psychology was his coinage of the phrase **nature vs. nurture** — the debate about whether human characteristics are determined by heredity or environment. In *Hereditary Genius* (1869), Galton argued that intelligence is primarily inherited. He studied the family trees of eminent men — judges, scientists, artists, military commanders — and found that eminence tended to run in families. He concluded that genius is hereditary.

Galton's method had a fatal flaw: he could not separate heredity from environment. Eminent families tend to provide their children with better education, more resources, and greater social advantages. The fact that genius runs in families does not prove it is inherited. It might be learned.

Galton tried to address this problem through **twin studies** — comparing the similarity of identical twins (who share all their genes) with fraternal twins (who share about half). He found that identical twins are more similar than fraternal twins and concluded that this demonstrated the hereditary basis of psychological traits. But twin studies cannot fully separate heredity from environment, because identical twins also share more of their environment than fraternal twins (they are treated more similarly by parents, teachers, and peers).

Galton was a **hereditarian** — he believed that most psychological characteristics are primarily determined by heredity. He accepted a model of **hard heredity**, according to which the mechanism of inheritance is independent of an organism's life experience. This put him at odds with Spencer, who favored soft heredity and the inheritance of acquired characteristics.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** The nature vs. nurture debate has been going on for over 150 years. Modern genetics has shown that both heredity and environment contribute to virtually every psychological trait. Is the debate still useful? Or has it been replaced by a more nuanced understanding of how genes and environment interact?

## Eugenics: Breeding Better Humans

Galton's hereditarianism led him to a logical conclusion: if intelligence and ability are inherited, then the human race could be improved by selective breeding. He coined the term **eugenics** (from the Greek for "well-born") for programs that encourage the breeding of the "highly gifted" and discourage the breeding of "idiots and imbeciles."

Galton's vision took two forms. **Positive eugenics** encouraged the "fit" to have more children — through financial incentives, public recognition, and social pressure. **Negative eugenics** discouraged the "unfit" from reproducing — through institutionalization, marriage restrictions, and eventually, forced sterilization.

Galton was not alone. His ideas were embraced by a wide range of scientists, politicians, and intellectuals. The Eugenics Education Society was founded in London in 1907. Eugenics programs were implemented in Britain, the United States, Germany, Scandinavia, and many other countries. In Britain, the Mental Deficiency Act of 1913 authorized the institutionalization of people deemed "mentally defective." In the United States, more than 60,000 people were forcibly sterilized under eugenics laws.

Galton's eugenics was explicitly hereditarian. He maintained that poverty, insanity, and criminality are inherited traits that can be eliminated through selective breeding. This was not merely an academic theory. It was a policy program with devastating consequences.

Darwin himself rejected the more extreme forms of eugenics. In *The Descent of Man*, he acknowledged that sympathy for the weak is a natural human impulse and warned against the "hardening of the feelings" that would result from abandoning the sick and helpless. But Galton pressed on. When Darwin died in 1882, Galton continued to promote eugenics with undiminished enthusiasm.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Galton believed that intelligence is primarily inherited and that the human race could be improved through selective breeding. Modern genetics has shown that intelligence is influenced by hundreds of genes, each with a tiny effect, and that environmental factors (nutrition, education, healthcare) play a substantial role. Does this mean eugenics is impossible? Or does it mean that the *methods* were wrong, even if the *goal* of improving human welfare is legitimate?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Galton's anthropometric laboratory charged threepence per test. Today, you can take a personality test online for free, and your data is collected and sold without your knowledge. Has Galton's vision of measuring human beings been realized — or exceeded? What are the ethical implications of mass psychological measurement in the digital age?

## The Shadow: From Galton to the Nazis

The eugenics movement did not end with Galton's death in 1911. It grew. In the United States, eugenics programs continued into the 1970s. The Supreme Court upheld forced sterilization in the 1927 case *Buck v. Bell*, with Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes declaring: "Three generations of imbeciles are enough."

In Germany, the Nazi regime adopted eugenics as official policy. The 1933 Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring authorized the forced sterilization of people with conditions deemed hereditary — including schizophrenia, epilepsy, and "hereditary blindness." By 1945, an estimated 400,000 people had been sterilized. The program eventually escalated into the T4 euthanasia program, which murdered an estimated 70,000 people with disabilities, and ultimately into the Holocaust.

The connection between Galton's eugenics and Nazi racial hygiene is not a straight line. The Nazis drew on many sources, and their ideology was shaped by factors far beyond British statistical theory. But the intellectual framework — the idea that human populations can be improved through selective breeding, that some lives are more valuable than others, that the "unfit" should be eliminated — was established by Galton and his followers.

In 2001, the historian Nicholas Wright Gillham published *A Life of Sir Francis Galton*, documenting how Galton's scientific contributions cannot be separated from his eugenics. In 1985, Daniel Kevles's *In the Name of Eugenics* traced the history of the eugenics movement from Galton to the present. Both works showed that the eugenics movement was not a fringe phenomenon. It was mainstream science, endorsed by leading universities, governments, and professional organizations.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Forced sterilization programs continued in the United States until the 1970s. In Sweden, they continued until 1976. In some countries, they continue today. The eugenics movement is not a historical curiosity. It is an ongoing moral crisis. What obligations do scientists have to consider the social consequences of their research? Is it enough to say "I was just doing science"?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Galton's legacy is a cautionary tale about the relationship between science and values. His statistical methods — correlation, regression, the analysis of individual differences — are genuinely valuable tools that have advanced psychology and many other sciences. But his eugenics program was a moral catastrophe. The same mind that invented the correlation coefficient also invented a program of forced sterilization. Science does not come with a moral compass. The tools that can illuminate can also destroy. The lesson is not that measurement is dangerous. The lesson is that measurement without moral reflection is dangerous.

## Speaking of Measurement and Its Consequences...

- A university admissions office in São Paulo uses standardized test scores to select students. The tests were originally designed to measure "aptitude" — a concept that Galton would have recognized. Critics argue that the tests measure privilege, not ability. The debate echoes Galton's nature vs. nurture question: are the differences in test scores inherited, or are they products of environment?

- A genetics counselor in Seoul tells a couple that their unborn child has a 30% chance of developing a hereditary condition. The couple asks: "Should we terminate the pregnancy?" The counselor says: "That's your decision." The question is, at its core, a eugenics question — about which lives are worth bringing into the world. Galton would have had a clear answer. Most people today are less certain.

- A social worker in Lagos argues that poverty is not a genetic trait but a structural problem. "You can't breed your way out of inequality," she says. "You have to change the system." This is the fundamental objection to Galton's hereditarianism — and it is as relevant today as it was in 1869.

---

Galton had measured human beings and concluded that intelligence is inherited. But another question was emerging — one that would define the next generation of psychologists: do animals think? A young Englishman named George John Romanes believed they did, and he set out to prove it by collecting stories about clever dogs, wise crows, and philosophical monkeys. His methods were anecdotal, his evidence was weak, and his legacy would shape the entire future of comparative psychology.
