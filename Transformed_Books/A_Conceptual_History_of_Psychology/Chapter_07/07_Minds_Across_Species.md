# Chapter 07 · Module 7
# Minds Across Species — Instinct, Intelligence, and Morgan's Canon
## Psychology · Unit 1 · History of Psychology

---

In the spring of 1873, a British biologist named Douglas Spalding conducted a series of experiments that should have changed the course of psychology. He took newly hatched chicks and placed hoods over their heads, depriving them of all visual experience for the first few hours of life. When the hoods were removed, the chicks immediately pecked at grain, followed moving objects, and showed fear of the shadow of a hawk overhead — all without any prior learning. Spalding had demonstrated, with elegant simplicity, that some behaviors are innate. They do not need to be learned. They are built into the nervous system.

Spalding also showed that there is a **critical learning period** — a narrow window of time during which certain behaviors must be acquired. Chicks that were isolated from their mother for the first two days of life would not follow her when reunited. The instinct to follow had not been activated, and the window had closed. Spalding had discovered what Konrad Lorenz would later call **imprinting** — the rapid, irreversible learning that occurs during a critical period early in life.

These experiments were published in *Nature* in 1872 and 1873. They were methodologically rigorous, theoretically significant, and almost completely ignored. Spalding died of tuberculosis in 1877, at the age of 35. His work was forgotten for decades. It was not until the 20th century that Lorenz independently rediscovered imprinting and critical periods, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1973.

The history of science is full of such ironies. The most important ideas are not always the ones that get the most attention. Sometimes they are the ones that get the least.

## Romanes: Animal Intelligence by Anecdote

The man who *did* get the attention was George John Romanes (1848–1894) — Darwin's informal successor in the field of comparative psychology. Romanes was a talented experimental physiologist, but his contribution to psychology was a book called *Animal Intelligence* (1882), which attempted to demonstrate that animals possess forms of mentality and consciousness similar to those of humans.

Romanes's method was **introspection by analogy** — the attempt to understand animal mentality on the basis of analogies with human mentality accessible to introspection:

> "Starting from what I know subjectively of the operations of my own individual mind, and the activities which in my own organism they prompt, I proceed by analogy to infer from the observable activities of other organisms what are the mental operations that underlie them."
> — George John Romanes, *Animal Intelligence* (1882/1977, pp. 1–2)

This method was not inherently unscientific. Many theoretical inferences in science are based on postulated analogies with observable entities. The wave theory of light was based on an analogy with ocean waves. The Bohr model of the atom was based on an analogy with planetary systems. The scientific adequacy of such theories depends not on their conjectural origin but on the quality of evidence supporting them.

Unfortunately, the quality of Romanes's evidence was poor. Most of it was based on **anecdotal data** — secondhand reports of animal behavior, gleaned from responses to his newspaper solicitations or from overseas correspondents. These reports documented isolated instances of apparently intelligent behavior but were doubtfully representative of the species as a whole.

Edward Thorndike, one of the pioneers of American experimental animal psychology, later complained:

> "Dogs get lost hundreds of times and no one ever notices it or sends an account of it to a scientific magazine. But let one find his way from Brooklyn to Yonkers and the fact immediately becomes a circulating anecdote."
> — Edward Thorndike, *Animal Intelligence* (1911, pp. 24–25)

Romanes's attributions were also excessively **anthropomorphic** — he ascribed complex human emotions and reasoning to animals without sufficient evidence. He attributed hypocrisy to dogs and abstract mathematical knowledge to monkeys. He solemnly reported the trial of a crow, in which a group of crows allegedly held a "palaver" and punished one of their number for some unspecified offense.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Romanes attributed human-like reasoning to animals based on anecdotal reports. Modern animal cognition researchers use controlled experiments to study what animals can and cannot do. Is the pendulum still swinging between these two approaches? When you watch a video of a crow solving a puzzle, do you attribute intelligence to the crow? Or do you look for a simpler explanation?

## Morgan's Canon: The Razor of Comparative Psychology

Romanes had informally designated Conwy Lloyd Morgan (1852–1936) as his successor in comparative psychology. Morgan was an astute observer of animal behavior who had demonstrated that scorpions cannot be induced to commit suicide even under extreme stress — a finding that impressed Romanes. But Morgan adopted an increasingly critical attitude toward Romanes's methods and conclusions.

Morgan is famous for formulating the methodological principle known as **Morgan's canon**:

> "In no case may we interpret an action as the outcome of the exercise of a higher psychical faculty, if it can be interpreted as the outcome of the exercise of one which stands lower in the psychological scale."
> — Conwy Lloyd Morgan, *Introduction to Comparative Psychology* (1894/1977, p. 53)

Formally, Morgan's canon was simply a restatement of Occam's razor: when two competing theories are equivalent in their explanatory success, prefer the simpler one. In comparative psychology, simpler explanations in terms of reflexes, instincts, or learned habits should be preferred over more complex explanations in terms of abstract reasoning — when they account for the same behavior.

Morgan illustrated his canon with a detailed account of how his fox terrier Toby learned to open the latch on a courtyard gate. A casual observer might suppose that Toby understood the mechanism of the latch. But Morgan had watched the learning process from the beginning. Toby did not figure out the latch. He stumbled upon it by accident — by running along a wall and accidentally putting his head beneath the latch, which lifted and opened the gate. Over three weeks, through repeated trials, Toby gradually eliminated his mistakes and went directly to the right spot. This was not comprehension. It was trial-and-error learning — an instance of the Spencer-Bain principle.

Morgan was careful to note that his canon does not prohibit attributing complex cognition to animals. It merely requires that such attributions be supported by evidence:

> "The canon by no means excludes the interpretation of a particular activity in terms of the higher processes, if we already have independent evidence of the occurrence of these higher processes in the animal under observation."
> — Conwy Lloyd Morgan, *Introduction to Comparative Psychology* (1903, p. 59)

Morgan kept an open mind, insisting that questions about animal cognition should be determined by empirical investigation, ideally under controlled experimental conditions.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Morgan's canon says: prefer simpler explanations. But who decides what counts as "simpler"? Is it simpler to say a dog understands a latch mechanism, or that it stumbled upon the solution by accident? The answer depends on what you already believe about dog cognition. Does Morgan's canon actually resolve disputes, or does it just push the disagreement one level deeper?

## Emergent Evolution: Some Things Are Genuinely New

Morgan's canon was based on the assumption that there may be **strong discontinuity** between human and animal psychology — that some human capacities may not be identifiable in any form in animals. This was a direct challenge to Darwin's and Romanes's commitment to strong continuity.

Morgan denied that "any animals have reached that stage of mental evolution at which they are even incipiently rational" (1894/1977, p. 377). He believed that certain human capacities — the perception of relations, abstract thought, behavior based on means-end reasoning — are not attenuated forms of capacities found in animals. They are genuinely new. They emerge at more complex levels of biological organization.

This view — **emergent evolutionism** — was the evolutionary analogue of Mill's principle of mental chemistry. Just as Mill argued that complex ideas can have properties not found in their component sensations, Morgan argued that complex organisms can have capacities not found in their simpler ancestors. The whole is genuinely more than the sum of its parts.

Morgan's emergent evolutionism was entirely consistent with the theory of natural selection. Darwin himself had acknowledged that "certain powers, such as self-consciousness, abstraction, &c, are peculiar to man" (1871, p. 105). Evolution produces novelty, not just elaboration. Some capacities are emergent — they arise at a certain level of complexity and cannot be predicted from the properties of simpler systems.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Morgan argued that some human capacities — like abstract reasoning — are genuinely new, not just more complex versions of animal capacities. Modern comparative psychology has shown that some animals (great apes, corvids, cetaceans) can solve problems that Morgan would have attributed only to humans. Was Morgan wrong? Or is the boundary between human and animal cognition just more blurred than he imagined?

## The Behaviorist Reversal

Morgan's canon was intended to bring scientific objectivity to the attribution of mentality and consciousness to animals. It was not intended to deny that animals have minds. But later psychologists used it for exactly that purpose.

The irony was profound. Morgan had argued that some but not all human behavior can be explained in terms of animal psychology. Later behaviorists argued that all human behavior can be explained in terms of animal psychology — specifically, in terms of stimulus-response learning. They reversed Morgan's explanatory direction: instead of explaining animal behavior as attenuated human cognition (as Romanes had done), they explained human behavior as elaborated animal learning (as Spencer had done).

Jacques Loeb, a biologist at the University of Chicago who taught Watson biology and physiology, tried to account for all forms of psychology and behavior in terms of basic associative processes. He maintained that the higher cognitive capacities of humans and animals are elaborations of associative memory, which is itself an elaboration of more basic mechanisms underlying reflexes and tropisms.

John B. Watson took this further. If Morgan's canon applies to both animal and human behavior — and Morgan agreed that it does — then there is no need to appeal to cognition or consciousness in the explanation of any behavior. As Romanes had presciently warned, any doubts about the legitimacy of explaining animal behavior in terms of mentality and consciousness could be generalized to human behavior. Watson generalized them.

This was the conceptual foundation of behaviorist psychology: the strong continuity between human and animal psychology, combined with Morgan's canon, combined with the sensory-motor theory of the nervous system. If the whole nervous system is a reflexive sensory-motor machine, and if human psychology is continuous with animal psychology, and if simpler explanations should be preferred over complex ones — then all behavior can be explained as chains of stimulus-response connections.

The scientific study of animal mentality and consciousness did not revive until the 1970s, when Donald Griffin's *The Question of Animal Awareness* (1976) challenged the behaviorist orthodoxy and inaugurated the modern study of animal cognition.

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** Morgan's canon was misused to deny animal consciousness — something Morgan himself never intended. Can you think of another case where a scientific principle was extended beyond its original scope to support conclusions its author would have rejected? What safeguards can scientists build to prevent the misuse of their ideas?

> [!TIP]
> **Stop and Think:** The debate about animal consciousness has practical consequences. Factory farming, animal testing, wildlife conservation — all depend on how we answer the question "do animals think and feel?" If Morgan was right that some human capacities are emergent and not found in animals, does that justify treating animals differently? Or is the capacity to suffer enough to warrant moral consideration, regardless of whether an animal can reason abstractly?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The debate about animal minds — whether animals think, feel, and experience consciousness — is one of the oldest in psychology, and one of the most consequential. Romanes attributed too much to animals. Morgan cautioned against attributing too little. The behaviorists denied consciousness entirely. Modern comparative psychology tries to navigate between anthropomorphism and denial. The lesson from this history is that the question "do animals think?" is not just a scientific question. It is a moral one. How we answer it determines how we treat the billions of non-human animals with whom we share the planet. Morgan's canon was meant to be a tool for scientific rigor. It became, in the wrong hands, a license for indifference.

## Speaking of Minds, Canons, and Animals...

- A dog owner in Lagos watches her dog solve a puzzle feeder — pulling levers and sliding panels to get to the treats inside. She says: "She understands what she's doing." A behaviorist would say: "She learned through trial and error." Morgan would say: "Perhaps. But you need evidence before you attribute comprehension." Who is right?

- A primatologist in São Paulo studies capuchin monkeys using stone tools to crack nuts. The monkeys select specific stones, position the nuts on anvils, and strike with precision. This looks like tool use — a capacity once thought to be uniquely human. Is it? Or is it a complex habit shaped by trial and error? Morgan's canon says: prefer the simpler explanation. But what if the simpler explanation is wrong?

- A philosopher in Seoul argues that the question "do animals have consciousness?" is unanswerable, because consciousness is a first-person experience that cannot be directly observed in anyone — human or animal. This was Morgan's original worry, and Romanes's response still stands: if you doubt animal consciousness, you should, for the same reasons, doubt human consciousness. The inference from behavior to mind is the same in both cases.

---

The story of evolutionary theory's impact on psychology is not a story of steady progress. It is a story of ideas borrowed, misused, extended, and distorted. Spencer turned evolution into a cosmic justification for laissez-faire capitalism. Darwin stripped evolution of purpose and direction. Galton used evolution to justify eugenics. Morgan tried to bring scientific rigor to the study of animal minds — and his canon was used to deny that animals have minds at all. The next chapter will show how these evolutionary ideas were institutionalized — how psychology became an academic discipline, with laboratories, journals, and professional organizations. But the questions raised in this chapter — about continuity and discontinuity, nature and nurture, instinct and learning, the minds of animals and the purpose of evolution — have never been settled. They are still being debated. They may always be.
